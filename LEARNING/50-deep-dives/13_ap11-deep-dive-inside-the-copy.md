## In one minute

The card said: scan a brain, translate the scan into a wiring diagram, simulate it, and a mind runs — no understanding required. True to the plan, but the plan hides a machine, and opening the machine breaks the "no understanding" promise in five places.

1. **"How much detail is enough?" is not one question — it is a ladder, and picking a rung is already a theory.** The card named this as one assumption ("scale separation"). Open it and it is a graded ladder of resolutions: at the top, a cartoon neuron that only records *when* it fires; near the bottom, a full biophysical cell with every ion channel; below that, individual molecules and quantum detail. The three levels a expert workshop judged *most plausible* alone span from about **10¹⁸ to 10²⁵ operations per second** to run — a factor of **ten million** — and nobody knows which rung is right. You cannot even begin without choosing, and the choice is a claim about what in the brain matters.

2. **The scan can only see the wires.** To resolve a connection you need **electron microscopy** — light microscopy physically cannot see features that small — and even then you buy resolution at the price of volume: the sharpest method images only a speck. But the deeper limit is not size. A scan captures **structure** (what is connected to what). A mind also runs on **dynamics** (how strong each connection is, whether it excites or inhibits, second by second) — and much of that is a **wireless** chemical broadcast the structural scan is blind to (Part 4).

3. **The "copy" is not a photograph — it is a fallible reconstruction with a human jury.** Turning the raw images into a wiring diagram is done by an AI tracer (a **flood-filling network**) whose errors are still "too high to generate reliable circuit diagrams without extensive human proofreading." A single **merge error** fuses two neurons into one false cell and corrupts the circuit. So the connectome you copy has error bars and human judgment baked in — it is not the objective ground truth the bet assumes.

4. **The 2026 proof: structure alone does nothing.** In 2024 a team took the fly's connectome — real, measured connectivity — built the network in software "**but with unknown parameters for the single-neuron and single-synapse properties**," and it computed nothing. It only came alive when they "**optimized the values of these unknown parameters using techniques from deep learning, to allow the model network to detect visual motion**" — that is, they had to *tell it what the circuit was for* and train it. The wiring constrains; it does not determine. You must supply the function, and the function is understanding.

5. **So "copy without understanding" is not what the machine does.** Every rung of the ladder (which resolution), every cell (which neuron model), every connection (excite or inhibit, how strong), and the whole missing wireless layer must be *filled in* by choices and by fitting to what the circuit is supposed to do. The bet's one unique selling point — *no theory of intelligence needed* — is precisely the thing the opened machine cannot deliver. The card asked "is understanding necessary to build a mind?" The machine answers: **you cannot even build the copy without it.**

The card gave you brain emulation as a photocopy job. This page shows the copy is a **stack of theory-laden reconstructions**, and that the frontier of the field is, exactly, the slow measurement of *how much understanding the copy secretly requires.*

The card's "scan → translate → simulate" hides that "simulate" secretly needs a **fourth input** the scan never captured: the live dynamics. Keep that in view and the machine falls into order.

## Part 1 — the resolution ladder: "how much detail is enough?" is a stack, not a switch

Start where the card stopped. Scale separation, the card said, is the assumption that there is a level of detail that is "enough." Stated like that it sounds like a single yes-or-no unknown: is there a floor, and where? Open it and it is not a switch but a **ladder** — a graded series of levels of detail, and the whole feasibility of the bet depends on *which rung* turns out to be enough, because the rungs are astronomically far apart in cost.

### The two ends of the ladder: a cartoon cell and a biophysical one

Take the single smallest unit — one neuron — and ask how much of it you must reproduce in software. There is a whole discipline of answers, and its two ends make the ladder concrete.

At the **cheap top of the ladder** sits the cartoon. You do not model the cell's biology at all; you model only the one fact that it *fires*. In the standard textbook's words, "**Neuron models where action potentials are described as events are called 'Integrate-and-Fire' models. No attempt is made to describe the shape of an action potential.**" *(An **integrate-and-fire** neuron adds up its incoming signals — "integrate" — and the instant the total crosses a threshold it emits a spike and resets — "fire"; the spike itself is just a marked moment in time, with no internal shape.)* The appeal is exactly its thinness — "**The elegance and simplicity of integrate-and-fire models makes them a widely used tool to describe principles of neural information processing in neural networks of a broad range of sizes.**" One cheap equation and a threshold, per cell.

At the **expensive bottom** sits the real biophysics. Here you simulate the cell as a living electrical device: an insulating membrane acting as a capacitor, pierced by voltage-controlled channels that let specific ions through, each channel a resistor whose openness changes with voltage. This is the **Hodgkin-Huxley model**, from the 1952 work (a Nobel Prize) that first explained the spike. Its authors "**performed experiments on the giant axon of the squid and found three different types of ion current, viz., sodium, potassium, and a leak current**," and turned each into equations of how its channels open and close over time. Now here is the quiet admission that matters for the whole bet: even this "detailed" model is a drastic simplification. In the textbook's own words, "**In the standard Hodgkin-Huxley model there are only three types of channel**" — a sodium, a potassium, and a catch-all leak — whereas a real cortical neuron carries *dozens* of distinct channel types, plus a branching tree of dendrites that each compute locally. To capture *those*, you climb further down into **multi-compartment** models (chop one neuron into hundreds of connected electrical segments), and the cost per cell climbs with every step.

The gap between the two ends is not small. A leaky integrate-and-fire neuron is roughly one differential equation; a detailed multi-compartment Hodgkin-Huxley neuron is thousands. And that is *per cell*, multiplied across tens of billions of them.

### The ladder has rungs all the way down — and each is a theory

Zoom back out from one cell to the whole brain and the ladder gets its full, sobering length. Sandberg & Bostrom's roadmap laid the levels out as an explicit scale, from coarse to fine: at the top, whole **computational modules** and **brain-region connectivity** (model each area as a functional box); below that, an **analog population network**, then a **spiking neural network** (every neuron, integrate-and-fire style); below *that*, **electrophysiology** (add the detailed membrane dynamics — the Hodgkin-Huxley level); then the **metabolome** (add the chemical soup — concentrations, gene expression); then individual **proteins and their states**; then the **stochastic behaviour of single molecules**; and at the very bottom, **quantum** interactions. Each rung down adds a layer of physical detail the rung above threw away.

Now the number that should stop you. For the three levels the roadmap's expert workshop judged **most plausible** — the spiking-network, the electrophysiology, and the metabolome rungs — the estimated computing power to run a whole human brain in real time was, respectively, about **10¹⁸, 10²², and 10²⁵ operations per second** *(as of the roadmap's estimates, carried into the 2025 reassessment; treat as order-of-magnitude, not precise)*. Read those exponents slowly. Between the *most optimistic plausible rung* and the *most pessimistic plausible rung* — and both are considered live options by serious people — the cost of running the copy differs by a factor of **ten million**. And that is only across the three *middle* rungs; the molecular and quantum rungs below run the cost up past numbers with thirty zeros, and the whole-region rungs above collapse it toward something almost tractable. The card's "fifteen zeros of uncertainty" was not hand-waving. It is this ladder, and the fact that **nobody knows which rung is enough.**

Here is the sharp consequence, and it is the thesis of the whole page in miniature. To *begin* the copy at all, you must **choose a rung**. And choosing a rung is not a neutral engineering setting — it is a **substantive claim about how the brain computes**: that everything below your chosen level is noise the mind does not use. Pick the spiking rung and you are asserting that the exact shape of each spike and the chemistry underneath it does not matter for thought. Pick the metabolome rung and you are asserting it does. There is no way to copy "the brain" without first deciding what in the brain is essential — and that decision *is* a theory of intelligence, the very thing the bet swore it did not need. The card's scale-separation assumption, opened up, is not one hopeful gap. It is an unbounded stack of theory-laden choices, and you are standing on it before the scan even switches on.

## Part 2 — the scan: why it must be electron microscopy, and the resolution-versus-volume wall

Grant the choice of rung. Now you must actually *see* the structure at that resolution. The card said the scan "freezes, slices, and photographs" the brain; open the photograph step and two hard walls appear — one set by physics, one set by an unavoidable trade.

### Why ordinary microscopes physically cannot do it

The connection you must resolve is the **synapse** *(the junction where one neuron passes a signal to the next; the card's word)*, and the business gap across it is on the order of **20 nanometres** *(a nanometre is a millionth of a millimetre)*. An ordinary light microscope cannot see anything that small, and not for want of engineering — it is a law of physics. Light of a given wavelength cannot resolve two things closer together than roughly **200 nanometres** *(the diffraction limit — features nearer than about half the wavelength of visible light blur into one)*. A synapse is ten times finer than that blur. So to trace wiring cell-by-cell you are forced to a probe with a far shorter wavelength: a beam of **electrons**. **Electron microscopy** reaches single-nanometre resolution, fine enough to see the membrane, the synaptic gap, and the little chemical-filled sacs that mark a real connection. This is *why* the card's scan destroys the brain: electron microscopy needs the tissue set hard, stained with heavy metal, and cut into slices tens of nanometres thin — there is no gentle version. *(The card's **expansion microscopy** is the clever end-run — swell the tissue in a gel so ordinary light microscopy can suddenly resolve the fine detail; the card owns that trick, so we only point at it here as the exception that proves the rule.)*

### The resolution-versus-volume wall

Electron microscopy gets you the resolution. It does not get you the *volume* — and this is the trade that keeps the scan a speck. The three main methods sit at different points on one unavoidable curve *(all figures a July-2026 snapshot of the volume-EM literature)*:

- **Ion-beam milling (FIB-SEM)** shaves the block with a focused beam and images each freshly exposed face, reaching about **5–8 nanometres in all three directions** — beautifully sharp and even — but the milling damages the sample, so it can only image a volume "**a few tens of microns**" across before artifacts pile up. Sharpest picture, smallest box.
- **Serial block-face (SBEM)** cuts a thin layer off the top of the block and images the new surface, giving roughly **9–16 nanometres** sideways but a coarser **20–30 nanometres** in depth — bigger volume, blurrier between slices.
- **Tape-collecting (ATUM)** slices the block into physical ribbons collected on tape and images each later, allowing very large areas — but its depth resolution is limited by how thin you can physically cut, around **30–100 nanometres**.

The pattern is one line: "**there remains an inherent trade-off between resolution and the size of the 3D volume that can be achieved.**" Sharp enough to trust every synapse, and you can only image a grain of sand; big enough to hold a useful chunk of brain, and you lose the fine detail exactly where the synapses live. This is the mechanism under the card's "the best human map so far covers one cubic millimetre." It is not that no one has tried to go bigger — it is that going bigger means going blurrier, and going sharp means going tiny. A whole human brain at synapse resolution is not one scan; it is more than a million of the sharpest specks, each destroying its slice of tissue, and it does not exist because the physics of the microscope will not let it exist yet.

## Part 3 — the translate: the connectome is a reconstruction, not a photograph

Say you have the images — a mountain of them, tens of thousands of slices through a speck. You still do not have a connectome. You have pictures. Turning pictures into a wiring diagram — the card's "translate" step — means tracing every one of the tangled, branching cells through the whole image stack and recording every place two of them touch. This is where a second surprise lives: the connectome is not *read off* the images, it is **reconstructed** by a fallible machine, and the reconstruction has error bars the card's clean word "translate" hides.

### The tracer is an AI, and its errors are the expensive kind

There is far too much to trace by hand — a single speck holds kilometres of wiring — so the tracing is done by a specialized neural network. The leading method is the **flood-filling network** (Januszewski et al., 2018): a convolutional network with a memory loop that starts inside one neuron and iteratively "floods" outward, painting the cell's shape as it grows along every branch. It was a real advance — "an **order of magnitude better than that of previous approaches**." And yet the paper's own opening sentence is the honest wall for the whole approach: "**Automated approaches have been developed for tracing, but their error rates are too high to generate reliable circuit diagrams without extensive human proofreading.**"

Why can't you just trust the tracer? Because its two failure modes are not equal, and one is quietly catastrophic. A **split error** cuts one neuron into two pieces — annoying, but a human can see the loose end and rejoin it. A **merge error** does the opposite: it fuses two *different* neurons into one, inventing a cell that was never there and a set of connections that do not exist. A merge is poison, because it silently rewrites the wiring diagram — and the wiring diagram is the whole product. The flood-filling paper measured itself exactly this way: "**we achieved a mean error-free neurite path length of 1.1 mm, and we observed only four mergers in a test set with a path length of 97 mm**." Read that as the field's real unit of progress — not "accuracy" but *how far can the tracer follow a wire before it makes a mistake* (here, on average 1.1 millimetres of clean tracing between errors). That is a triumph relative to the past. It is also a confession: on a brain whose wiring runs to *millions of kilometres*, an error every millimetre means an astronomical number of mistakes, every one of which must be caught.

### The proofreading tax

So the connectome is finished the way the card's fly was — by people. Behind the 2024 fly connectome sat a project of hundreds of human proofreaders checking and correcting the AI's tracing, cell by cell. This is the **proofreading tax**, and it is not a temporary inconvenience; it is structural. The tracer is good but not perfect; the product must be near-perfect (a merged pair of neurons is a fabricated circuit); so a human jury has to sit between the two, and the jury's size scales with the tissue. And note the further cost the flood-filling paper itself flags — the better accuracy came "**with substantially increased computational costs**," so even the machine half of the translate step is expensive.

Step back and see what this does to the bet. The card's picture is of *copying* an existing, objective structure — the brain's true wiring, sitting there waiting to be duplicated. The machine says the connectome is not found but **built**: an AI's best reconstruction, corrected by human judgment, carrying a known rate of splits and (rarer, worse) merges. What you copy is not the brain's structure; it is *your reconstruction* of the brain's structure, with error bars. For a bet whose entire promise is *faithfulness* — a copy so exact it thinks like the original — a translate step that is lossy, fallible, and human-refereed is not a footnote. It means the "faithful copy" has, from the first step, a gap between it and the original that no one can fully measure, because the original was sliced away to make the images.

## Part 4 — the dynamics gap, opened: the wired brain, and the wireless one

Now the deepest part, and the place the card's Stuck #1 ("a wiring diagram is not a running brain") turns from a slogan into a specific mechanism with a 2026 experiment attached. The card said, correctly, that a connectome tells you *that* two cells connect but almost nothing about *how*. Open the "how," and it splits into two distinct problems — one about the wires you *did* map, and one about a whole communication system you *cannot* map with a scan at all.

### Problem one: the wires have no values, and structure alone computes nothing

Grant a perfect connectome — every cell, every synapse, no merge errors. It is still a diagram with the components rubbed out: it does not say whether a given connection *excites* the next cell (pushes it toward firing) or *inhibits* it (holds it back), how *strong* the push is, or how fast it acts. Those are the **parameters** that turn a static graph into a running network, and the scan does not carry them.

For four decades this was an argument. In 2024 it became an experiment, and the result is the single cleanest proof of this whole page's thesis. A team took the fruit fly's **connectome** — the real, measured wiring of its motion-detecting visual circuit — and built the network in software. In their exact words: "**We constructed a model neural network with the experimentally determined connectivity for 64 cell types in the motion pathways of the fruit fly optic lobe but with unknown parameters for the single-neuron and single-synapse properties.**" With the wiring known and the parameters blank, the network did nothing useful. It came alive only at the next step: "**We then optimized the values of these unknown parameters using techniques from deep learning, to allow the model network to detect visual motion.**" Once trained, the model went on to reproduce more than two dozen real experimental findings about the fly's visual neurons — a genuine, remarkable success.

But look at *what made it work*. The connectome supplied the **structure**; it left the **dynamics** unknown; and to recover the dynamics the team had to (a) bring in **deep learning** and (b) — crucially — *tell the network what the circuit was for*: detect visual motion. That task specification is the smuggled understanding, in the open. The wiring diagram, by itself, does not pick out the fly's actual dynamics from the infinitely many settings its graph could take; it takes an outside claim about the circuit's **purpose** to do that. The team's own framing names the gap precisely — there is a large distance between the static snapshot of the connectome and the live computation of the brain, and bridging it needed the task and the training. Structure **constrains** the mind; it does not **determine** it. And the missing ingredient is exactly the kind of understanding — *what is this circuit for?* — the copy-without-understanding bet promised to skip. This is the fly, with 140,000 neurons. The card's worm, with 302, has never even been bridged this far.

### Problem two: the scan sees the wired brain; the mind also runs on a wireless one

Even that understates it, because it still assumes the whole story is in the synapses — the *wires*. It is not. A real nervous system also computes through a second, parallel channel that a structural scan is, by its nature, blind to.

Alongside the fast, point-to-point synaptic wiring, neurons release **neuromodulators** and **neuropeptides** *(chemical messengers — such as dopamine, serotonin, and a large family of small signalling proteins — that a neuron squirts out to change how other neurons behave)* that spread by **volume transmission** *(the chemical diffuses through the surrounding tissue and reaches cells microns away, with no wire between them — a broadcast, not a phone call)*. This is the **wireless connectome**, and its defining feature is that it does **not** line up with the wired one. Mapping work on the worm found that the "wireless" chemical network and the "wired" synaptic network **overlap only weakly** — they are two different graphs laid over the same cells. And the wireless layer is not a minor trim: the neuroscientist Cornelia Bargmann's much-cited argument, "**beyond the connectome**," is that the *same* wiring diagram produces *different* behaviours depending on which neuromodulators are washing over it — a circuit is not one machine but many, switched between states by chemicals the connectome never shows.

Sit with what that means for the scan. Electron microscopy, however perfect, images **structure** — where the membranes are, where the synapses sit. It cannot see which cell is, right now, bathing its neighbours in a peptide that flips their behaviour, because that is a matter of **live chemical state**, not of shape. So there is an entire computational system in the brain — one that reconfigures the wired circuit moment to moment — that the card's scan **cannot capture in principle**, no matter how fine its resolution. This is why a bigger, sharper scan does not close the dynamics gap: the gap is not a resolution problem, it is a *different kind of measurement*. The 2025 field split named it as its own pillar — **Neural Dynamics**, "recording brain function" — separate from Connectomics, "mapping brain structure." Structure you can scan (destructively, at a speck). Function — the live electrical-and-chemical state of every cell and the wireless traffic between them — you would have to *record from a living brain*, at a completeness and scale nobody has any method for. The card's Stuck #1, opened, is this: the copy is missing not a few numbers but a whole second nervous system that leaves no structural trace.

## Part 5 — the 2026 frontier and the honest synthesis

Line the machine up against the current evidence, as of July 2026.

- **Structure mapping (connectomics) is real, accelerating, and increasingly automated — the card's Leg 2, confirmed from the inside.** The fly brain (2024) and the specks of mouse and human brain (2025) are genuine, and the flood-filling-plus-proofreading pipeline that produced them is a real engineering achievement. This is the pillar that is going well.
- **Function recording (neural dynamics) has no comparable path, and it is the true bottleneck.** The 2025 stock-take of the field puts the two pillars side by side precisely because the second is the laggard: we can map structure at a speck, but we cannot record the live, complete dynamics of even a small circuit at anything like the needed fidelity — and Part 4 showed why (much of it is a wireless chemical state that leaves no structural trace). The card's Stuck #1 was right, and the mechanism is now clear: the copy is missing the dynamics, and the dynamics are the hard pillar.
- **The frontier is not "scan a whole brain" — it is "can structure predict function at all?"** The most interesting 2026 work is the fly-style question: take a real connectome and see how much of a circuit's actual behaviour you can recover by *constraining* a trained network with the wiring. The 2024 fly-vision result says: a surprising amount — but only after you add deep learning and tell the model the circuit's job. That is the field *measuring how much understanding the copy cannot avoid* — which is the opposite of the bet's promise, run as an experiment.

Now the synthesis, the point of the whole page. The card's central question was: **is understanding necessary to build a mind — or can a good-enough copy do it blind?** Open the machine, and the honest 2026 answer at the level of *mechanism* is that the machine, as actually built, **cannot copy blind** — understanding re-enters at every step:

- **You cannot choose what to scan without a theory of what matters.** The resolution ladder (Part 1) forces a claim — spiking rung? metabolome rung? — that is a substantive bet on how the brain computes, made before a single slice is cut.
- **You cannot turn the scan into a network without supplying the dynamics** (Part 4), and the fly result shows the dynamics are recovered only by importing deep learning *and the circuit's purpose*. Structure constrains; understanding determines.
- **And the scan is structurally blind to the wireless brain** (Part 4), so even a perfect wiring diagram omits a whole computational system that only *functional recording* — not scanning — could ever capture.

*As of July 2026, the machine we can actually build does not deliver the card's promise. "Copy without understanding" was the bet's one unique advantage over every theory-driven approach — and it is exactly the property the opened pipeline lacks: the copy is a stack of resolution choices, fallible reconstructions, and dynamics filled in by fitting to what circuits are for.* The card asked whether a copy could be a mind with no theory of minds behind it. The machine answers, mechanically: not this machine — every rung of it needs the theory back. Which leaves the bet in a strange place. Its distinctive escape from the risk of a wrong theory of intelligence turns out to be an illusion of the slogan; the moment you build the copy, you are making the same theory-laden bets as everyone else — only about *neurons and ions* instead of *tokens and rewards.*

## Putting the machine together

Hold the whole picture in one view.

1. **The resolution ladder (Part 1).** "How much detail is enough?" is not one assumption but a graded ladder — from a cartoon **integrate-and-fire** neuron (just mark when it fires) down through the biophysical **Hodgkin-Huxley** cell (ion channels; and even *it* keeps "only three types of channel" out of a real cell's dozens) to molecules and quantum. The three *plausible* rungs alone span **10¹⁸ → 10²⁵ operations per second** — ten-million-fold — and choosing a rung is already a theory of what in the brain matters.
2. **The scan (Part 2).** Synapses (~20 nm) are ten times finer than light microscopy's ~200 nm limit, so you must use **electron microscopy** — which forces the destructive freeze-stain-slice, and hits an **inherent resolution-versus-volume trade** (FIB-SEM: 5–8 nm but a speck; ATUM: large area but 30–100 nm deep). A whole brain at synapse resolution is over a million of the sharpest specks; the physics of the microscope is why it doesn't exist yet.
3. **The translate (Part 3).** The connectome is *reconstructed*, not photographed, by a **flood-filling network** whose errors are "too high … without extensive human proofreading"; **merge errors** fabricate false cells; the real unit of progress is *error-free path length* (~1.1 mm). So the copy carries error bars and a **proofreading tax** from the first step.
4. **The dynamics gap (Part 4).** A perfect connectome is a diagram with the components rubbed out: no signs, strengths, or speeds. The 2024 **fly result** proved structure alone computes nothing — the network worked only after **deep learning** filled the "**unknown parameters**" against a **task** (detect motion). And the scan is blind to the **wired-and-wireless** split: neuromodulators broadcast by **volume transmission** form a second network that reconfigures the circuit and leaves no structural trace.
5. **The synthesis (Part 5).** As built in 2026, the machine **cannot copy without understanding** — understanding re-enters at the rung choice, the dynamics fitting, and the missing wireless layer. The bet's one advantage (no theory needed) is the property the pipeline lacks.

## Judging the machinery: where the concrete emulation mechanisms are stuck

The [AP11 card](../20-the-approaches/11_ap11-whole-brain-emulation.md) judged the **bet** (four cracks: wiring-diagram-isn't-a-brain · scale-beyond-us · slowest-path-teaches-nothing · the ethics). This page judges the **machine we actually build** — a sharper question: *given the resolution ladder, the scan, the reconstruction, and the dynamics gap, what does the concrete pipeline reveal that "scan → translate → simulate" could not?* Be fair first: connectomics is real, accelerating, automated science, and the fly-vision result is a genuine, exciting demonstration that structure carries real information about function. Nothing here says the program is worthless. But the machine has its **own** four cracks, and each sharpens a card-crack into a mechanism.

### Stuck #1 — the copy is not a copy; it is a chosen resolution

The card treated "scan the brain" as duplicating a definite object. The mechanism shows there is **no single "the brain's structure" to duplicate** — there is a **ladder of resolutions** (Part 1), and the bet cannot even start without picking a rung, which is a claim that everything finer is noise the mind ignores. The three plausible rungs span ten-million-fold in run-cost, and nobody knows which is right. So "copy without understanding" fails at step zero: you must understand the brain *well enough to choose what to keep* before you copy anything. That is a model with a theory-laden resolution dial, not the blind copy the bet advertised. **[Established — the levels ladder is explicit in the field's own roadmap; that choosing a level is a substantive claim is not seriously disputed.]**

### Stuck #2 — structure alone underdetermines the mind, and the gap is filled by fitting to purpose

This is the deepest crack, because it turns the card's *philosophical* Stuck #1 into a *demonstrated experimental fact.* A connectome is a diagram with the components rubbed out; a network needs signs, strengths, and speeds the scan does not carry. The 2024 fly result is the proof: with "**unknown parameters for the single-neuron and single-synapse properties**," the wired network computed nothing, and it worked only once those parameters were "**optimized … using techniques from deep learning**" against the circuit's **task**. So the dynamics are not read off the structure — they are *recovered by importing understanding of what the circuit is for.* Structure constrains; purpose-plus-fitting determines. For a bet whose whole claim is that copying needs no theory, "we filled the missing half by telling the model its job and training it" is the theory returning openly. **[Established for the fly circuit that structure underdetermines function and needs task-optimization; Contested how far this scales to a whole brain, where the "task" of the mind is not a clean objective you can write down.]**

### Stuck #3 — the scan is blind to the wireless brain, so a sharper scan cannot close the gap

The card's Stuck #1 says a wiring diagram is not a running brain; the mechanism says *why it can never be, by scanning alone.* Beyond the mapped synapses runs a **wireless** network of neuromodulators and neuropeptides broadcast by **volume transmission**, which overlaps the wired network only weakly and **reconfigures the same circuit into different machines** depending on the chemical state. Electron microscopy images **shape**; this is **live state**; the two are different measurements. So the missing dynamics are not a resolution problem that a better microscope fixes — they are a **functional-recording** problem with no scalable method, which is exactly why the 2025 field split lists **Neural Dynamics** as a separate, lagging pillar from **Connectomics**. A perfect scan omits, in principle, a whole second nervous system. **[Established — the wired/wireless distinction and volume transmission are well-documented; that a structural scan cannot capture live neuromodulatory state is definitional.]**

### Stuck #4 — the copy cannot be verified, because the original was destroyed to make it

The card's cousins get their power from **checkability** — [AP2](../20-the-approaches/02_ap2-reasoning-and-test-time-compute.md) and the [robot-brain deep dive](12_ap10-deep-dive-inside-the-robot-brain.md) both turned on whether you can cheaply test an output *right or wrong*. Emulation has the weakest checker on the whole map. The only real test of "faithful" is to compare the copy's behaviour to the original's — but destructive scanning **kills the original**, so for a human upload there is nothing left to compare against; and "same behaviour" would itself require understanding what to measure. The fly and worm are checkable only because we can compare a model to *living* animals of the same kind — a luxury a one-of-a-kind human brain, sliced away to be imaged, does not offer. So even in success the strong version is unfalsifiable: a mind that claims to be a copied person cannot be checked against the person it replaced. This sharpens the card's Stuck #4 (identity) into an engineering fact: the machine destroys its own ground truth. **[Established — destructive scanning and the absence of a behavioural checker for a unique brain are intrinsic to the method as it exists.]**

### The big question under all four

The card asked: *can you get a mind by faithful copying alone, with no understanding of how minds work?* The machine answers a sharper, more mechanical version: **can the pipeline, as built, copy a brain without importing a theory of what matters — or does understanding re-enter at every rung?** As the machine is actually assembled in 2026, the answer is that **understanding re-enters everywhere**: at the resolution choice (which level is "enough"), at the dynamics (recovered by fitting to the circuit's purpose), and at the boundary of the scan itself (blind to the wireless brain). The bet's unique promise — *no theory of intelligence required* — is the one property the opened machine cannot deliver; the copy turns out to be a model with the same theory-laden bets as every other approach, only about ions instead of tokens. And notice the clean contrast with its twin: the [AP6 deep dive](10_ap6-deep-dive-predictive-coding-and-the-learning-rule.md) found that wherever a brain *principle* is made precise it becomes a more efficient *implementation* of what deep learning already does; this page finds that wherever a brain *copy* is made concrete it re-imports the very *understanding* it hoped to skip. Both twins, opened, end in the same place: the brain does not hand you a shortcut around theory — whether you try to distil its algorithm (AP6) or photocopy its structure (AP11), you are back to needing to know how minds work. *As of July 2026 this is the longest-horizon question on the map, and the pipeline's verdict is that "copy without understanding" is not a road around the hard problem — it *is* the hard problem, only relabelled as a scan.* **[Contested — the key open question, now located in the pipeline: can structure-plus-fitting ever recover a whole mind, or does the wireless, unrecordable dynamics put a faithful human copy permanently out of reach?]**

## How to use this (if you want to direct AI work)

- **When someone says "we scanned/simulated a brain," ask which rung of the resolution ladder.** (Part 1.) The word "simulate" is meaningless without a level: a spiking-network sim and a metabolome sim differ by ten-million-fold in cost and in what they claim matters. A pitch that won't name its resolution — or waves at "full detail" — is hiding its single biggest assumption.
- **Separate the two pillars, and find the function pillar.** (Parts 3–4.) A connectome is *structure*; a mind needs *dynamics* too. Ask: do they have the connection signs, strengths, and speeds — and how did they get them? If the answer is "we trained a network to fit a task" (the fly method), that is legitimate and impressive — but it means the model's behaviour comes from the *task and the fitting*, not purely from the scan. Do not accept a wiring diagram as evidence of a working mind.
- **Ask what the scan cannot see.** (Part 4.) Neuromodulation, live chemical state, the wireless network — none of it is in an electron-microscope image. If a project's claim depends on having captured "the brain's computation," ask how it accounts for the state-dependent, volume-transmitted layer that structural scanning omits by construction.
- **Read a connectome as a reconstruction with error bars.** (Part 3.) It was traced by a fallible AI and corrected by humans; merge errors fabricate circuits. Ask about the error-free path length and the proofreading effort before treating the map as ground truth — and remember the original tissue was destroyed to make it, so the map cannot be re-checked against the source.
- **Discount emulation timelines hardest of all, but for a mechanism, not a mood.** (Whole page.) The card said "decades." The mechanism says *why*: the function pillar has no scalable method, the scan is blind to the wireless brain, and the copy can't be verified against a destroyed original. These are not effort gaps that money closes on a schedule.
- **What you delegate vs what you keep.** *Delegate:* running the microscope, training the flood-filling tracer, building the simulator, fitting connectome-constrained models. *Keep for yourself:* choosing (and defending) the resolution rung, checking that "simulate" means structure *and* dynamics, asking what the wireless layer contributes that no scan captures, and holding the hard truth that a "faithful copy" imports a theory of what matters at every rung — so it is not, and cannot be, the theory-free shortcut the bet advertised.
