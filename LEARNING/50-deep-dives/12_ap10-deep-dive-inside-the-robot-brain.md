## In one minute

The card said: teach a web-trained model to output actions as tokens, and web knowledge flows into a body. True — but that one sentence hides the machine, and the machine has a surprise in it.

1. **An action is not really a word, and pretending it is breaks on real control.** The card's picture — chop the action into tokens and predict it like the next word — is exactly how the first VLAs worked (RT-1, RT-2, OpenVLA). But it pays three prices that matter for dexterous hands: it is **coarse** (a chopped-up action loses millimetre precision), it is **slow** (predicting one token at a time runs at a few times a second, far too slow for smooth motion), and it **averages** (a task usually has many right ways to do it, and a machine trained to output one action splits the difference into a wrong one). So the field moved to generating the action **continuously**, all at once, with a **diffusion / flow-matching** action head.

2. **No single network can be both smart and fast — so the robot brain splits in two.** A big model that carries web knowledge is slow (it "thinks" a few times a second); smooth motor control needs 50–200 decisions a second. You cannot have both in one network. As Figure's engineers put it flatly: *"VLM backbones are general, but not fast, and robot visuomotor policies are fast but not general"* *(a **visuomotor** policy = a vision-to-movement one — it turns what the camera sees straight into motor commands)*. So the modern robot brain is **two systems**: a slow, web-pretrained **System 2** that understands the scene and the instruction, and a fast reactive **System 1** that emits the motor commands — wired together by a thin **latent** summary passed from the slow one to the fast one.

3. **This is the card's own two heroes, reconciled.** The fast System 1 is Brooks's *"the world is its own best model"* — a controller that doesn't model the world, just reacts to it, quick. The slow System 2 is the deliberate, knowing mind that Brooks wanted to throw away. The machine keeps **both**, running at two clock speeds. It is also [AP2](../20-the-approaches/02_ap2-reasoning-and-test-time-compute.md)'s fast-vs-slow thinking made *physical*, and the same two-box shape as [AP7's](11_ap7-deep-dive-the-integration-problem.md) loose join.

4. **The data comes from a lop-sided pyramid, and the useful layer is the smallest.** Web pretraining (huge, cheap) supplies the *meaning*; pooled cross-robot logs and simulation (medium) supply variety; and a tiny layer of **teleoperated demonstrations** — a human puppeteering the robot through the exact task — supplies the actual skill. That bottom layer is where the real learning happens, and it is the one you can least scale: every robot-hour is physical, breakable, real-time. This is the card's "you can't download experience," seen from the inside.

5. **So the machine quietly answers the card's big question — and the answer is uncomfortable for the strong bet.** Open the robot brain and the *knowing* lives in the web-pretrained System 2; the body mostly trains the fast System 1 controller, and the two meet through a pinhole. That looks far more like *a web-trained mind with a fast pair of hands attached* than *a mind grown through a body.* The body supplies a controller, not the concepts. The frontier (2026) is precisely the attempt to change that — to let the robot's own experience reshape the mind, not just drive it — but as the machine stands today, it leans toward the card's rival bet, not the card's.

The card gave you the robot brain as one idea. This page shows it is a **negotiated truce between a slow knower and a fast doer** — and that the truce, read honestly, tells you where the "a mind needs a body" bet is really standing in 2026.

## One line of base, then we build

Three reminders, each owned by another page, so here they are *pointed at*, not re-taught.

- The [AP10 card](../20-the-approaches/10_ap10-embodiment.md) gave the **VLA** (vision-language-action model): a big model, first trained on the web, taught to **output robot actions** as an extra thing it can produce, so its book-knowledge flows into a body. The card owns *that idea* and its headline result (RT-2 reaching for a rock when told to find "something to use as a hammer"). This page opens *how the action is actually produced*, which the card left as "as tokens."
- The card named the **data bottleneck** (Stuck #2): there is no internet of robot actions to scrape, so experience must be collected in the real world, slowly; and the two escape routes — **simulation** and **human video** — each have a gap (**sim-to-real**, **human-to-robot**). This page opens the *mechanism* of how the field actually assembles its training data, and why the shape of that data is the deepest constraint on the whole approach.
- From [AP2](../20-the-approaches/02_ap2-reasoning-and-test-time-compute.md): **fast-vs-slow thinking** — the idea (from the psychologist Daniel Kahneman) that a mind has a fast, automatic **System 1** and a slow, effortful **System 2**. AP2 owns the concept. This page shows a robot brain split into a literal System 1 and System 2 — two *networks*, running at two *clock speeds* — and that split is the spine of everything below.

One framing to carry, because the whole page turns on it. A robot brain has to do two jobs that pull in opposite directions: **know** (understand a messy scene and a vague instruction — which needs a big, slow, web-trained model) and **move** (emit smooth, precise motor commands many times a second — which needs a small, fast, reactive one). The tension between *knowing* and *moving* — between general-but-slow and fast-but-narrow — is the single force that shapes every design choice in this page. Keep it in view and the machine falls into order.

## Part 1 — the representation fork: how do you turn "intelligence" into a motor command?

Start at the smallest, sharpest question, because it splits the whole field: once a model has decided *what to do*, how does it actually say *move the arm like this*? A robot action is a bunch of numbers — for a single arm, seven of them each instant: how far to move in each of three directions (x, y, z), how to rotate the wrist (roll, pitch, yaw), and how far to open the gripper. A "policy" *(the function that maps what the robot sees to what it does — the term you met in [AP4](../20-the-approaches/04_ap4-rl-from-interaction.md))* has to emit those numbers, smoothly, over and over. There are two deeply different ways to do it, and the choice decides what the robot can and cannot do.

### The card's answer: chop the action into tokens and predict it like a word

The card's version is the first one the field tried, and it is elegant. A big model is already brilliant at predicting the next **token** *(a token = a small chunk the model reads or writes — usually a piece of a word)*. So: turn the action into tokens too, and let the same machine predict *those*. Concretely, in Google's **RT-1** each of the action's numbers is **discretized** — *chopped into a fixed set of steps*. In the paper's exact words, "each action dimension in RT-1 is discretized into **256 bins**" *(a **bin** = one of 256 labelled buckets the continuous number is rounded into — like rounding a temperature to the nearest degree)*. Now the arm's smooth motion has become a little "sentence" of discrete tokens ("x-bin-137, y-bin-42, …"), and the model predicts them one after another the way it predicts words. This is **autoregressive** generation *(autoregressive = producing an output one piece at a time, each piece conditioned on the pieces already produced — exactly how a language model writes)*. RT-2 (the card's hero) scaled this up; the open-source **OpenVLA** (2024) is the clearest example — a 7-billion-parameter model built on a Llama language model with a vision encoder, "trained on 970k robot episodes from the Open X-Embodiment dataset," emitting its actions as discrete tokens. This works, and it inherits the web brain's knowledge. Good.

### Why chopping-into-tokens breaks on real dexterity

Now watch it fail, because the failure is what forces everything else. Three prices come due, and all three bite hardest exactly where the card most wants to win — fine, dexterous, physical manipulation.

- **It is coarse.** 256 bins per dimension sounds like a lot until you need to slot a battery or thread a cable tie, where — in the ALOHA team's blunt words — "**Millimeters of error would lead to task failure**." Rounding every motion to the nearest bin throws away exactly the precision the hard tasks need.
- **It is slow.** Autoregressive means one token at a time, and a robot's action is many tokens; the big model has to run again for each. RT-1, the paper notes, "does this at **3 Hz**" *(Hz = times per second; 3 Hz = three decisions a second)*. Three times a second is fine for slow pick-and-place; it is hopeless for the smooth, reactive motion of folding cloth or catching a wobble, which needs *tens to hundreds* of decisions a second. Predicting actions like words is inherently too slow for dexterous control.
- **It averages, and the average is wrong.** This is the subtle one, and it matters most. A physical task usually has **many** equally-good ways to do it — to get around a mug you could sweep left *or* right, both fine. If you train a network to output the single "correct" action by fitting it to demonstrations, and the demonstrations went left *and* right, the network learns to output the **average** — go straight — which drives the gripper through the mug. Robot actions, the Diffusion Policy paper explains, have "the existence of **multimodal distributions** … and the requirement of high precision" *(a **multimodal distribution** = one with several separate good peaks — several distinct right answers — not a single blurry middle)*, and a plain averaging predictor cannot represent "left or right"; it can only represent their bad midpoint.

Put the three together and the lesson is that an action is *not* really like a word: it needs precision a bin can't hold, speed one-token-at-a-time can't reach, and the honest representation of "several right answers at once" that a single-output predictor can't express.

### The modern answer: generate the whole action continuously, with a diffusion / flow-matching head

So the field changed how the action is produced. Instead of *chopping* the action into discrete tokens and emitting them one by one, generate the whole continuous action **all at once**, using the same kind of machine that draws images from noise. This is **Diffusion Policy** (2023): "a new way of generating robot behavior by representing a robot's visuomotor policy as a **conditional denoising diffusion process**." Unpack that, because it is the second master-key of the page (the first was the card's tokenizing).

**Diffusion** *(the method behind modern image generators)* works like this: start from pure random noise, then repeatedly nudge it a little toward something valid, following a learned sense of "which way is toward a real answer," until the noise has been sculpted into a clean output. **Denoising** = that step-by-step cleaning-up of noise into signal. A **diffusion policy** does exactly this but the "answer" it sculpts from noise is *a robot action* (or a short sequence of them), guided by what the camera sees. And this buys back all three things the token approach lost, in the paper's own list — the diffusion formulation is good at "**gracefully handling multimodal action distributions, being suitable for high-dimensional action spaces, and exhibiting impressive training stability**":

- **It represents "several right answers" honestly.** Because it sculpts from random noise, it can land on the left-way *or* the right-way on different runs, instead of collapsing them to the wrong middle. The multimodal problem is solved by construction.
- **It emits a whole smooth sequence at once**, not one rounded step. "This property allows the policy to **jointly infer a sequence of future actions instead of single-step actions**, which is critical for encouraging temporal action consistency and avoiding myopic planning." Precise, continuous, coordinated over time.

The leading generalist robot model, Physical Intelligence's **π0** (late 2024), builds this straight onto a web-trained brain. It states the fork exactly. Older VLAs, it notes, "employ **autoregressive discretization** to represent actions in a manner analogous to text tokens" — the card's approach — and then: "our model … [produces] actions via **flow matching**, a variant of diffusion." **Flow matching** is a close, faster cousin of diffusion — same idea of turning noise into a valid action along a learned path, trained a little differently. π0 attaches this as a dedicated **action expert**: "we use a novel action expert that augments the standard VLM with flow-based outputs" *(here "expert" just means a specialized extra module bolted onto the main model to do one job — produce the continuous action)*. And the payoff is precisely the speed and dexterity the token approach could not reach: flow matching "allows us to handle high-frequency action chunks (up to **50 Hz**) and highly dexterous tasks, which we show **pose a major challenge for prior autoregressive VLAs**." Fifty decisions a second, continuous and precise — versus RT-1's three, rounded.

So here is the fork, the first thing to carry out of this page: a robot brain either **speaks its actions in discrete tokens** (autoregressive, like RT-2 / OpenVLA — simple, inherits the language machinery, but coarse and slow) or **sculpts them continuously from noise** (diffusion / flow-matching, like Diffusion Policy / π0 — precise, fast, honest about multiple right answers). For dexterous control the field has largely crossed to the continuous side. But notice what that crossing did *not* fix — and could not. Flow matching made the *doing* fast. It did nothing to make the *knowing* fast. And that unfixed half is what splits the whole machine in two.

## Part 2 — the clock-speed problem, and the fast-slow split it forces

Follow the speed number one more step and the deepest structural fact of the field falls out. To *know* — to look at a cluttered kitchen, read the instruction "clear the table," and work out that the mug goes to the sink — you need a big, web-trained vision-language model. Big models are **slow**: they run a few times a second, because each run is an enormous computation. But to *move* — to keep a gripper on a smooth, correcting path — you need **50 to 200 decisions a second**. These two requirements cannot live in one network. Figure's engineers wrote the tension in one sentence, and it is worth memorising as the thesis of the page: *"**VLM backbones are general, but not fast, and robot visuomotor policies are fast but not general.**"* You can have general-but-slow, or fast-but-narrow. Not both, not in one box.

The field's answer comes in two moves.

### Move 1 — action chunking: let a slow brain drive a fast body

The first move softens the speed gap without splitting anything. Instead of predicting one action and then thinking again, predict a whole **chunk** of the next several actions at once, then *execute the chunk* while the slow brain works on the next one. This is **action chunking** — the ALOHA team's **Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT)**, which "learns a generative model over **action sequences**" (note: a sequence, not a single step — the same move Diffusion Policy made). π0.5 makes the size concrete: it emits "a 50-step (1-second) action chunk" of continuous joint commands at a time. A brain that thinks once a second can still drive a body that moves fifty times a second, if each thought hands down a one-second ribbon of motion.

Chunking pays a second dividend the field cares about a lot. A robot copying human demonstrations suffers **compounding error** — in the ACT paper's words, "errors in the policy can compound over time" *(compounding error = a small mistake pushes the robot into a slightly unfamiliar spot, where it makes a bigger mistake, which pushes it further off — errors snowball)*. Deciding one twitchy step at a time makes this worse; committing to a coherent chunk of motion makes it better. Chunking is the term to keep: **the slow brain speaks in ribbons of future motion, not single twitches.**

### Move 2 — the dual system: two networks, two clock speeds, one thin wire

Chunking stretches one network across the gap. The bigger, now-standard move is to stop pretending one network can do both jobs and **split the brain into two**, each running at its own natural speed. This is the **System 1 / System 2** architecture — [AP2's](../20-the-approaches/02_ap2-reasoning-and-test-time-compute.md) fast-and-slow thinking, built out of two actual pieces of silicon:

- **System 2 — the slow knower.** A big, web-pretrained vision-language model. It looks at the scene and the instruction, works out *what to do*, and — crucially — does **not** output motor commands. It outputs a **latent** *(a compact vector of numbers that summarises its conclusion — an encoded "here's the plan/intent," not words and not joint angles)*. In Figure's Helix: "System 2 (S2): An onboard internet-pretrained VLM operating at **7-9 Hz** for scene understanding and language comprehension." Slow, general, knowing.
- **System 1 — the fast doer.** A small reactive policy (typically a diffusion/flow action expert from Part 1). It takes System 2's latent *plus* the freshest camera frame and emits smooth continuous motor commands, fast. In Helix: "System 1 (S1): A fast reactive visuomotor policy that translates the latent semantic representations produced by S2 into precise continuous robot actions at **200 Hz**." Fast, narrow, reacting.
- **The wire between them is deliberately thin.** System 2 does not hand System 1 a paragraph; it hands it that one latent vector. Helix: "The latent vector from S2 is projected into S1's token space and concatenated with visual features from S1's vision backbone." The slow brain's entire influence on the fast one flows through that pinhole.

Why bother with two boxes and a wire instead of one clever network? Because the split lets each half run at the speed its job actually needs — Helix again: "This decoupled architecture allows each system to operate at its **optimal timescale**. S2 can 'think slow' about high-level goals, while S1 can 'think fast' to execute and adjust actions in real-time." System 2 ponders the kitchen a few times a second; System 1 keeps the gripper on track two hundred times a second, correcting for every wobble the slow brain is far too sluggish to catch.

This is not one company's trick; it is the shape the field converged on. NVIDIA's **GR00T N1** (2025), an open humanoid foundation model, describes the identical anatomy: "The **vision-language module (System 2)** interprets the environment through vision and language instructions. The subsequent **diffusion transformer module (System 1)** generates fluid motor actions in real time" — and, importantly, "Both modules are **tightly coupled and jointly trained end-to-end**" *(end-to-end = the whole two-part system trained together as one, so the slow brain learns to emit latents the fast controller can actually use)*. Google DeepMind's **Gemini Robotics 1.5** (2025) is the same split with the wire made of *words* instead of a latent: it "thinks before acting," reasoning through intermediate steps in plain language before the action stage runs — an explicit, readable System 2 feeding the doer.

### The card's two heroes, reconciled

Now step back, because the split quietly settles a fight the card set up and left open. Recall Brooks's *"the world is its own best model"* (the card's Claim 1): don't build a slow inner model and reason over it — just sense the real world and react, fast. And recall that classic AI did the opposite: a big deliberate model of the world, reasoned over slowly. The card presented these as rival philosophies. The modern robot brain says: **they were each half of the machine.** System 1 *is* Brooks's fast, model-free, reactive controller — it doesn't represent the world, it just reacts at 200 Hz. System 2 *is* the slow deliberate knower that Brooks wanted to throw out — kept, but demoted to a few hertz and denied any direct control of the motors. The ACT paper even states Brooks's insight as an engineering fact: for fine manipulation, "learning the manipulation policy is **much simpler than modeling the whole environment**" — react, don't model. The field did not pick a side in the card's debate; it **stacked** the two sides at two clock speeds and wired them together. That is the machine. And, as the next parts show, that wire — thin, one-directional, with all the *knowing* upstream of it — is exactly where the card's deepest question comes back to bite.

## Part 3 — the data pyramid: where the training signal actually comes from

The card named the data bottleneck but left it as a slogan ("you can't download experience"). Open it, because the *shape* of the training data is the hardest constraint on the whole approach — harder than any architecture choice. The field assembles its training signal as a **pyramid**: widest and cheapest at the top, narrowest and most precious at the bottom. GR00T N1 states the three layers in one line — it is trained on "a heterogeneous mixture of **real-robot trajectories, human videos, and synthetically generated datasets**" — but the important thing is not the list, it is that the layers are wildly different sizes, and the *smallest* one does the *most* important work.

- **Top layer — internet pretraining (enormous, nearly free, already paid for).** The vision-language model that becomes System 2 was trained on the whole web before it ever saw a robot. This is where the *meaning* comes from — what a mug is, what "left" means, that a rock can serve as a hammer. π0 is explicit that this is the entire reason to start from a VLM: "we inherit the general knowledge, semantic reasoning, and problem-solving abilities of language- and vision-language models." This layer is trillions of tokens. It is the card's "riding on our grounded text," made concrete.
- **Middle layer — pooled robot logs and simulation (large, but not web-large).** Because no single lab can collect enough robot data, the field **pools** it: the **Open X-Embodiment** dataset gathers robot episodes from many labs and many different robot bodies into one training set (OpenVLA's 970k episodes), a practice called **cross-embodiment training** *(training one model on data from many kinds of robot at once, so it learns skills that transfer across bodies rather than memorising one machine)*. Alongside it, **simulation** generates cheap synthetic experience inside a physics video-game. Bigger than any one lab could manage — but still a rounding error next to the web.
- **Bottom layer — teleoperated demonstrations (tiny, gold, expensive).** This is where the actual physical *skill* is taught, and it is made by hand. A human **teleoperates** the robot — puppeteers it through the exact task while every camera frame and joint angle is recorded, producing perfect state-and-action pairs. The ALOHA rig shows the mechanism plainly: "The user teleoperates by **backdriving** the leader robots, with the follower robots mirroring the motion" *(backdriving = the human physically moves a small "leader" arm by hand, and the real "follower" arm copies it exactly — so the human's dexterity is recorded as robot actions)*. The model then learns by **imitation learning** (also called **behavioral cloning**) — *copy the human's recorded action for each situation, as plain supervised learning.* π0 reaches its hardest skills by fine-tuning on only "a few thousand demonstrations" of this gold data.

Two mechanical facts about this pyramid decide the fate of the approach.

**First — co-training, so the web brain doesn't forget.** If you take a web-trained model and train it only on robot data, it gets better at moving and *worse at knowing* — it suffers **catastrophic forgetting** *(when a network trained on a new task overwrites the knowledge from its old one)*, losing the very web semantics that were the point. So the field **co-trains**: mix web data and robot data together during training, so the model learns control *without* forgetting meaning. The web layer keeps supplying the concepts; the robot layer adds the control. Notice what this quietly confirms — the *knowing* and the *doing* come from two different data sources, and they are kept from overwriting each other on purpose.

**Second — the pyramid is upside-down compared to text, and that is the whole bottleneck.** In language models, the cheap-and-huge layer (the internet) is *also* where the core capability comes from — scale the free data and the model gets smarter. In robotics it is reversed: the layer that actually teaches the physical skill (the bottom, teleoperation) is the **smallest and least scalable**, because every example is a physical, breakable, real-time robot-hour with a human attached. OpenVLA states the scale gap flatly: "even the largest robot manipulation datasets **only have 100K to 1M examples**" — against the *trillions* of tokens behind System 2. This is the card's Stuck #2 shown from the inside: you cannot download experience, and the one layer of the pyramid you most need to grow is the one you can least afford to. Simulation and human video are the two attempts to widen that bottom layer cheaply — and each, as the card said and Part 4 now opens, leaves a gap.

## Part 4 — the four taxes the machine pays

The robot brain works, and it is a real achievement. But every design choice above bought its win by paying a price, and the four prices, lined up, are sharper and more specific than the card's one-line "it's stuck on data." Each is a *mechanism* the card could not name because the card never opened the machine.

**Tax 1 — the mind is a seam, and the knowing is all upstream of it.** The machine's headline feature — the fast-slow split — is also its deepest limit. The two systems meet through a **single thin latent wire**, and every scrap of *understanding* lives on the upstream side (System 2, the web-pretrained knower). System 1, the part that actually touches the world through the body, understands nothing; it is a fast reflex that turns a latent-plus-a-camera-frame into motion. So the "grounding" the card's whole bet is about — meaning earned by acting on the world — enters this machine through a pinhole *above* the part that acts, from a brain that was trained on text and pictures, not on touching anything. The body is downstream of the meaning, not the source of it. This is not a slogan; it is the data-flow of the actual architecture.

**Tax 2 — continuous control cannot be cheaply checked.** Recall what made [AP7's](11_ap7-deep-dive-the-integration-problem.md) loose join and [AP2's](../20-the-approaches/02_ap2-reasoning-and-test-time-compute.md) reasoning tractable: an **exact checker** — a proof verifier, a calculator, a unit test — that can say *right or wrong* cheaply. A diffusion/flow motor policy has no such checker. Its actions are *sampled* (stochastic — the same input can yield slightly different motions), continuous, and their only real test is *did the physical task succeed*, which you can learn only by running the real robot many times and counting. Evaluation is therefore slow, noisy, and hard to reproduce — you cannot unit-test a grasp. This is why robot progress is measured in painfully-collected success rates rather than the crisp benchmarks of text AI, and why the verification asymmetry that powers reasoning models does not transfer to bodies.

**Tax 3 — the sim-to-real gap, and its patch.** The card named the sim-to-real gap; the mechanism under it is that a simulator gets **contact physics** subtly wrong — friction, how a soft or bendy object deforms when squeezed, exactly the "millimetres and forces" that dexterity depends on — so a skill perfected in simulation often fails on the real robot. The field's main patch is **domain randomization**: train across a *distribution* of randomized simulators so the real world looks like just one more variation the policy has already seen. OpenAI's **Dactyl** work automated this as "**automatic domain randomization (ADR)**," which "automatically generates a distribution over randomized environments of ever-increasing difficulty." It genuinely helps — but read what it is: a way to buy robustness by **blurring** the simulator until reality falls inside the blur, not a way to make the simulator *correct*. The gap is managed, not closed.

**Tax 4 — the data pyramid is upside-down, and scale keeps flowing into the wrong layer.** Tie Part 3 to the card's Bitter Lesson (Stuck #3). The layer that teaches the actual physical skill (teleoperation) is the least scalable, while the layer that scales freely (web pretraining) feeds only the *knower*. So as compute and data grow, they mostly make **System 2** — the ordinary large model — better, while the "embodied" half stays a fast controller trained on a hand-collected trickle. That is the Bitter Lesson biting at the level of mechanism: the winning robots are *ordinary large models with an action head bolted on*, so scale keeps rewarding the web-trained brain and the body keeps supplying only a controller. The card asked whether embodiment is a road or scale's last mile; the data pyramid answers that, as built, the scalable part of the machine is the part that isn't the body.

Line the four up and the pattern is the same each time: the very moves that made the robot brain *work* — split the mind for speed, generate actions continuously, lean on web pretraining for meaning, patch the simulator — are the moves that keep the **body** on the far side of a thin wire, downstream of a knowing that came from text. The machine is real. But the machine, read honestly, keeps the *doing* and the *knowing* apart — and puts all the grounding on the knowing side, which never touched anything.

## Part 5 — the 2026 frontier and the honest synthesis

Line the machine up against the current evidence, as of July 2026.

- **The generalist robot brain is real, and improving fast — on the loose, two-system plan.** Physical Intelligence's line runs π0 → **π0.5** (open-world generalization — it can tidy a kitchen it has never seen, emitting a high-level action in words and then a one-second continuous chunk of joint commands at 50 Hz, April 2025) → **π0.6** (which adds **reinforcement learning from the robot's own experience** — a first move past pure imitation) → **π0.7** (April 2026). NVIDIA's **GR00T N1** put the dual-system design in the open. Figure's Helix has reached **Helix 02** (May 2026), reported running multi-hour autonomous shifts on a warehouse line. Google DeepMind's **Gemini Robotics 1.5** (October 2025) added explicit "think-before-acting" reasoning and cross-body **motion transfer**. Every one of these is the same anatomy this page opened: a web-pretrained slow knower, a fast continuous-action doer, a thin wire between, and a hand-built data pyramid underneath. *(All dated snapshots; the humanoid field remains hype-heavy — the card's tele-operation caveat still stands.)*
- **The whole stack still rests on borrowed meaning.** The concepts that let a robot obey "clear the table" came from System 2's web pretraining — human text and images — not from the robot's own contact with the world. The body, in every shipping system, trains the fast controller and connects it to the physical world; it does not author the mind's concepts. The machine, as built in 2026, grounds its *control* in the body and its *meaning* in the web.
- **The frontier is precisely the attempt to change that.** The most interesting 2026 research points at making the **body reshape the mind**, not just drive it: reinforcement learning from a robot's *own* experience (π0.6 — learning from what the body actually caused, the true sensorimotor loop the card cares about), learning **world-models** *inside* the policy so the robot predicts the consequences of its actions ([AP5's](../20-the-approaches/05_ap5-world-models-jepa.md) territory, now pulled into control), and letting *action* shape the learned representations rather than inheriting them frozen from the web. These are early. But they are aimed squarely at the gap this page found.

Now the synthesis, which is the point of the whole page. The card's central question was: **is a physical body the *source* of a general mind — the thing that grows the concepts from the bottom up — or is it an *output device*, a way to collect grounded data and act, that a big web-trained model can drive?** Open the machine, and the honest 2026 answer at the level of *mechanism* leans toward the second, with a live opening toward the first:

- **As the robot brain is actually built, the body is closer to the hands than the source.** The *knowing* lives in System 2 (web-pretrained, on text and pictures). The *doing* lives in System 1 (a fast controller trained on a small pyramid of demonstrations). The two meet through a thin latent wire, with all the meaning upstream of the part that touches the world. That is far more "a web-trained mind with a fast pair of hands" than "a mind grown through a body." The mechanism points where the card's Stuck #1 and Stuck #3 feared it would.
- **But the machine is not finished, and the frontier is a bet on the strong claim.** If reinforcement learning from the robot's own experience, and world-models learned from action, come to supply real *new* grounding — concepts the web could not give, earned by the body — then embodied cognition's strong claim gets its mechanism, and the body becomes a source after all. If instead the pyramid stays top-heavy and scale keeps flowing into System 2, the body stays the hands.

*As of July 2026, the robot brain we have actually built answers the card's question in the rival bet's favour — the concepts come from the web, and the body drives a controller — while the field's whole research frontier is the attempt to build the machine that would answer it in the card's favour.* Which of those two machines wins is, exactly, whether "a mind needs a body" is a claim about the **source** of intelligence or only its **hands** — and, unlike a philosophy argument, it is now being settled by which architecture folds more of the mind's *meaning* into the part that acts.

## Putting the machine together

Hold the whole picture in one view.

1. **The representation fork (Part 1).** A robot brain either **speaks actions in discrete tokens** (autoregressive — RT-2, OpenVLA; simple, inherits language machinery, but coarse, slow at ~3 Hz, and averages away multiple right answers) or **sculpts them continuously from noise** (diffusion / flow-matching — Diffusion Policy, π0; precise, fast to 50 Hz, honest about "several right ways"). Dexterity pushed the field to the continuous side.
2. **The forced fast-slow split (Part 2).** No one network is both smart and fast (*"VLM backbones are general, but not fast … policies are fast but not general"*). So the brain splits: a slow web-pretrained **System 2** knower (~7–10 Hz) hands a thin **latent** to a fast reactive **System 1** doer (50–200 Hz). This is [AP2's](../20-the-approaches/02_ap2-reasoning-and-test-time-compute.md) fast-vs-slow made physical, and it reconciles the card's Brooks-vs-classic-AI fight by *stacking* both at two clock speeds. **Action chunking** (ribbons of future motion) lets the slow brain drive the fast body and tames compounding error.
3. **The data pyramid (Part 3).** Meaning comes from the huge, cheap **web** top layer; variety from **pooled cross-robot logs + simulation**; the actual skill from a tiny, gold, expensive bottom layer of **teleoperated demonstrations** learned by **imitation**. **Co-training** stops the web brain forgetting. The pyramid is **upside-down** vs. text — the skill-teaching layer is the least scalable.
4. **The four taxes (Part 4).** The mind is a **seam** with all the knowing upstream of the body · continuous control has **no cheap checker** · the **sim-to-real** gap is only *blurred* shut by domain randomization · the pyramid is upside-down so **scale flows into System 2**, not the body.
5. **The synthesis (Part 5).** As built in 2026, the body trains the **controller**, not the **concepts** — meaning comes from the web, so the machine leans to the card's *rival* bet (body as hands). The frontier — RL from the robot's own experience, world-models from action — is the attempt to make the body a *source*. Which machine wins settles the card's question.

## Judging the machinery: where the concrete robot-brain mechanisms are stuck

The [AP10 card](../20-the-approaches/10_ap10-embodiment.md) judged the **bet** (four cracks: necessity-vs-sufficiency / grounding-vs-embodiment · the data problem · the Bitter Lesson · hard to falsify). This page judges the **machine we actually build** — a sharper question: *given the representation fork, the fast-slow split, and the data pyramid, what does the concrete robot brain reveal that the card's "give it a body" could not?* Be fair first: these are real, working, rapidly-improving systems, and the fast-slow VLA is a genuine engineering achievement — robots that learn like large models are, at last, doing real work. Nothing here says embodiment is worthless. But the machine has its **own** four cracks, and each sharpens a crack the card only stated.

### Stuck #1 — the mind is a seam; no single net is both smart and fast

The card treated "an embodied mind" as one thing. The mechanism shows it is **two things on a thin wire**, and the split is *forced*, not chosen — the field converged on it because *"VLM backbones are general, but not fast, and robot visuomotor policies are fast but not general."* A robot brain is a slow web-pretrained **System 2** and a fast reactive **System 1**, communicating through a single latent vector. That is a real achievement, but it is the opposite of the card's picture of a unified mind grown whole through a body: the machine is a negotiated truce between a knower and a doer, and the seam between them is permanent — you cannot collapse them into one network without giving up either the knowing or the speed. **[Established — every shipping generalist robot brain in 2026 is an explicit or implicit two-system split.]**

### Stuck #2 — the grounding enters above the body, from a brain that never touched anything

This is the deepest of the four, because it turns the card's *philosophical* worry (grounding vs embodiment) into a fact about the **data-flow of the architecture.** All the *understanding* in the machine lives in System 2, which was trained on **web text and images** and outputs a latent. System 1 — the only part wired to the body — understands nothing; it is a fast reflex converting that latent into motion. So the "meaning earned by acting" that the whole bet is about does not enter where the body is; it enters *upstream* of the body, from a brain that never acted on anything. The card asked whether grounding needs a body or could come from data; the machine answers, mechanically, that in every system we actually build the grounding *does* come from data (the web), and the body only supplies a controller for it. If the strong embodied-cognition claim is right — that concepts are *reshaped* by acting — today's architecture does not implement it; it inherits its concepts frozen and merely attaches a mover. **[Contested — the data-flow is established; whether it *must* be this way, or is a temporary limit the frontier will lift, is the open question.]**

### Stuck #3 — continuous control has no cheap checker, so the machine can't be verified like a mind that reasons

The card's cousins get their power from **checkability**: [AP2](../20-the-approaches/02_ap2-reasoning-and-test-time-compute.md) and [AP7](11_ap7-deep-dive-the-integration-problem.md) lean on exact verifiers (a proof-checker, a calculator, a unit test) that say *right or wrong* cheaply, so the system can be scored, searched, and improved fast. A diffusion/flow motor policy has **no such checker**: its actions are sampled and continuous, and the only real test is running the physical robot and counting successes — slow, noisy, unreproducible. This is a genuine, structural disadvantage of *bodies* relative to *reasoning*: you can verify a proof a million times a second and a grasp maybe a few times an hour. It caps how fast embodied AI can improve, and it is why the crisp benchmark-and-verify loop that drives text models does not carry over to robots. **[Established — the absence of a cheap action-verifier is intrinsic to real-world control.]**

### Stuck #4 — the data pyramid is upside-down, so scale keeps feeding the web brain, not the body

The card's Stuck #3 (the Bitter Lesson) worried that scale would absorb the body. The mechanism makes that concrete and dated. The pyramid that trains a robot brain has its *scalable* layer (web pretraining) feeding **System 2** and its *skill-teaching* layer (teleoperation) at the bottom, tiny and un-scalable — "even the largest robot manipulation datasets only have 100K to 1M examples." So growth in compute and data mostly improves the ordinary large model, and the winning robots *are* ordinary large models with an action head. The body's contribution — the hand-collected demonstration trickle — is the part that scales worst. One escape is now being tried in public (π0.6's reinforcement learning from the robot's own experience — grow the bottom layer from the robot's *own* acting instead of human puppeteering). Whether that can widen the base fast enough to matter is the live question; until it does, the Bitter Lesson keeps rewarding the web-trained knower over the body. **[Contested but sharpened — the upside-down pyramid is established; whether RL-from-experience or better simulators can invert it is open, July 2026.]**

### The big question under all four

The card asked: *is a physical body the necessary **source** of a general mind, or just a useful **output device** a web-trained model can drive?* The machine answers a sharper, more mechanical version: **does the body reshape the mind — supply concepts the web could not, earned by acting — or does it only drive a mind the web already built?** As the robot brain is actually assembled in 2026, the answer is *drive*: the concepts live in the web-pretrained System 2, the body trains the fast System 1 controller, and the two meet through a thin latent wire with all the meaning on the upstream, never-embodied side. That is the card's *rival* bet (AP1's scale) written into the architecture — a body as the last mile of a web-trained mind. And yet the entire research frontier — reinforcement learning from a robot's own experience, world-models learned from action, representations shaped by doing rather than inherited frozen — is the field's attempt to build the *other* machine, the one where the body genuinely authors part of the mind. If that frontier succeeds, the card is vindicated at the level of mechanism, not just philosophy. If it stalls, and scale keeps pouring into System 2 while the body stays a hand-fed controller, then "a mind needs a body" turns out to name embodiment's *hands*, not its *source*. *As of July 2026, the machine we have leans to the second and the machine we are trying to build leans to the first — and which one wins is, precisely, the card's open question, now being decided by architecture instead of argument.* **[Contested — the key open question, now located in the data-flow: does grounding enter the machine through the body, or above it?]**

## How to use this (if you want to direct AI work)

- **Ask where a robot policy sits on the representation fork.** (Part 1.) *Discrete-token* (autoregressive) is simple and inherits the language stack but is coarse and slow — fine for slow pick-and-place. *Continuous* (diffusion / flow-matching) is what dexterous, high-frequency manipulation needs. If someone pitches a "robot foundation model" for delicate work and it emits actions one rounded token at a time, ask how it hits the precision and the frame rate.
- **For any real robot, find the two clocks.** (Part 2.) Almost every working system splits into a slow knower and a fast doer. Ask: *what runs the slow loop (the web-pretrained brain), what runs the fast loop (the reactive controller), and what passes between them?* A pitch that claims one single network does perception, reasoning, and 200 Hz control all at once is either hiding the split or hasn't hit real-time dexterity yet.
- **Budget for the bottom of the pyramid, because that is where the skill is.** (Part 3.) The model is rarely the hard part; the hand-collected **teleoperation** layer is. Before believing a demo, ask how many teleoperated demonstrations it took, who collected them, and whether the plan to scale that layer is honest (or hand-waves "we'll get more data"). A simulation-only result is not a working robot until it survives the sim-to-real gap.
- **Read "grounded" carefully — check which system the grounding is in.** (Tax 1, Stuck #2.) When a robot is said to "understand" a task, the understanding almost always lives in the web-pretrained System 2, not in anything the body did. That is fine for many products — but it means the meaning is second-hand (from text and images), and the body is a controller. If your task truly needs concepts the web can't supply, a standard VLA won't grow them for you; that is the frontier, not the shelf.
- **Don't expect text-AI's verify-and-scale loop to work on bodies.** (Tax 2, Stuck #3.) There is no cheap checker for a grasp. Improvement is gated by slow, noisy real-world evaluation. Plan for that: it is why robot progress is measured in painstaking success rates, and why you cannot iterate on a physical policy as fast as on a chatbot.
- **What you delegate vs what you keep.** *Delegate:* building the VLM backbone, training the action expert, wiring the two systems, running the teleoperation rig and the simulator. *Keep for yourself:* deciding whether the task needs continuous or token actions, checking that the fast-slow split is honest and real-time, respecting the upside-down data pyramid before believing any timeline, and holding the hard question — whether the body in your system is *authoring* any of the mind's meaning (the frontier) or merely *driving* a mind the web already built (the shelf).
