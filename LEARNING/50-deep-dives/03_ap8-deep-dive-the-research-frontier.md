## In one minute

Deep dive #2 showed the tricks that win on ARC today. But read them again and each one is standing on a crutch:

- the search still runs over a **toolkit humans built by hand** (the card's oldest open problem);
- the neural help is still mostly a **guide** that picks the next step, not something that **writes a whole program**;
- the winning scores are often bought with an **enormous, expensive search**;
- and the whole test is still **static** — you are handed the finished puzzle and asked for the answer.

This page is the frontier: the research trying to **remove each crutch**, plus the surprising place the road ends up. Four frontiers and a bridge:

1. **Where the broad priors come from.** DreamCoder grows the *middle* of its toolkit but humans still lay the *foundation*. The frontier is growing the whole vocabulary in an **open** world — softer, neural "libraries" that stretch to problems harder than the ones you trained on.
2. **Nets that propose whole programs.** Not just guiding a step-by-step search, but a net that hands you a candidate program directly. The newest idea searches a **learned space of programs** (a system called **LPN**) — and hits a hard wall the field has fought for decades: **compositionality** (chaining new steps, not just blending old ones).
3. **The efficiency frontier.** Deep dive #1 said intelligence *is* efficiency. Now the competition takes that literally: the top prize is **cost-capped**, so "throw thousands of dollars of search at each puzzle" is disqualified by design. Efficiency stops being a bonus and becomes the **problem statement**.
4. **The agentic turn.** The newest benchmark, **ARC-AGI-3**, stops handing you the puzzle. It drops you into a little world with **no instructions**, and you must *explore* to discover what the puzzle even is before you can solve it.

And the bridge: take AP8's "search for a program," point it **outward** at a problem nobody has solved, let a language model **propose** and an exact score **check** — and it already **discovers new mathematics** (**FunSearch**). That is the exact seam where program synthesis **merges into open-endedness** ([AP9](../20-the-approaches/09_ap9-open-endedness.md)). The road does not end at a solved ARC. It runs into its neighbours.

## Part 1 — where the broad priors come from: growing the vocabulary in an open world

Start with the deepest open problem on the whole program-synthesis road — the one the card raised first and the one every solver quietly dodges.

Recall the shape of it. Program search only works if something narrows it down, and that "something" is a good **toolkit** *(the set of building blocks the search is allowed to snap together — the DSL)*. Deep dive #2 showed DreamCoder learning to **grow** its toolkit: it watches its own solutions, finds fragments it keeps reusing, and chunks each into a new tool. But two limits stayed:

- It grows the toolkit **on top of a starter set humans chose** — the foundation is still hand-laid.
- It learned its towers of tools in **small, tidy worlds** (drawing simple pictures, basic list tasks), not the messy open-ended world where new problems keep arriving that look nothing like the old ones.

The research frontier is exactly those two limits: **can the system grow its whole vocabulary, by itself, in an open world?** Three moves are being tried, and they come clearest from Kevin Ellis and Zenna Tavares thinking aloud about where their own work goes next.

**Move one — make the library *soft*.** DreamCoder keeps an **explicit** library: an actual list of invented functions, stored and reused. The newer idea is to drop the explicit list and let a **neural network** carry the same job — you show it code that worked before, and it learns to **produce similar code** for new problems. The "library" is no longer a shelf of named tools; it is a *feel* for the kind of code that tends to work, held in the weights of a net. Ellis calls this the more **diffused** version of library learning — the knowledge is spread through a network instead of pinned to a list. *(Diffused = spread out, not stored in one exact place.)* The trade is real: a soft library is easier to build and bend, but harder to inspect or reuse cleanly than a crisp list of functions. Ellis' own view is that you probably want **both** — a hard core of trustworthy tools and a soft neural sense around it — and that the honest research question is the **exploration–exploitation dilemma** of the library itself: *how much do you keep?* *(Exploration–exploitation = the constant trade between trying new things and reusing what already works; here, between hoarding every tool you ever made and keeping only the few that keep paying off.)* You want to keep a tool if, over all the uses you expect now and later, it will make your life easier — a decision you could, in principle, weigh up rationally rather than guess.

**Move two — stretch the library to *harder* problems than you trained on, using dreams.** This is the direct answer to "open world." Deep dive #2 explained DreamCoder's **dreaming** — the system invents its own practice problems by assembling random programs from its library and running them. The frontier use of dreaming is to handle **distribution shift** *(when the problems you meet later are a different shape from the ones you trained on — the world moved)*. Ellis describes the modern recipe like this: start from a small pile of human-written example programs; **dream** many similar ones and train a program-writer on them, so your writer is fluent at *short* programs. Then go out into the real world, which turns out to have **longer, harder** programs than your dreams ever showed you. Solve a few of those hard ones anyway (slowly, with lots of search), remember the solutions, and in the **next** sleep cycle dream about *those* — so your dreams, and your writer, drift to match the harder world. The library is not fixed; it **chases the world** as the world gets harder. That drift — pre-train on the easy, then keep re-aiming at whatever hard thing you actually hit — is the current best sketch of how a toolkit could grow in an open world instead of a tidy one.

**Move three — give up on one *true* toolkit.** There is a tempting wrong picture buried in all of this: that somewhere there is a single, correct set of primitive building blocks, and the job is to find it. Ellis pushes back on that. In how people actually think, Ellis notes, the right **abstraction** *(a simplified way of seeing a problem that hides the details that don't matter)* is **defined on the fly, per problem** — there is no one ground-truth set of abstractions that is right for everything. The world hands you data; you *choose* what to pay attention to and what to ignore, and the good choice depends on **the questions you expect to ask.** The framework Ellis reaches for is **resource-rationality** *(do the best you can with the limited thinking-time and memory you actually have — be smart about being lazy)*: keep the abstraction that pays off for the questions you expect, given that you cannot afford to model everything. So "where do the priors come from?" partly dissolves. There is no fixed answer to find; there is a **process** that keeps inventing the right-enough abstractions for the problems at hand.

The honest limit, stated now. None of this is solved. A soft neural library is real but hard to trust; the dream-and-chase recipe works in research demos, not yet in a genuinely open world; and "define the abstraction per problem" is a direction, not an algorithm. But notice where move three points. Ellis makes one more claim that sets up the rest of the page: you can only really tell whether an abstraction is *good* by **using it to act.** In pure program synthesis you never *falsify* a library — you just shrug that a tool "isn't useful right now." But put the system in a **world** where it has to act and see what happens, and a bad abstraction gets **caught** — reality pushes back. That is a thread we pick up in Part 4. For now, hold the frontier: growing the vocabulary in an open world is unsolved, and the deepest fix may not be a cleverer library at all, but *acting in a world* — or, as Part 5 will argue, *an endless generating process*.

## Part 2 — nets that propose whole programs (and the wall they hit)

Deep dive #2 gave you two ways a neural net can help a program search. DreamCoder's **policy** *guides* the search — it picks the next block, one step at a time. Greenblatt's language model *writes* the whole program — but blindly, throwing out a thousand guesses per puzzle and keeping the ones that happen to fit. The frontier is a **third** shape, and it is the most interesting: don't guide a search through code, and don't blurt a thousand programs — instead **learn a smooth space of programs and search *that*.** The clearest realisation is a system called the **Latent Program Network (LPN)**, from Clément Bonnet and Matthew Macfarlane (*"Searching Latent Program Spaces,"* which won a paper award at the ARC Prize 2024). Go slowly, because the idea is genuinely new.

Start with the word **latent**. *(Latent = hidden; here, a compressed inner code that stands for something bigger — like a single point that stands for a whole program.)* LPN's bet is that all the programs you might need can be laid out as points in one smooth **space** — imagine a map where each location is a different little program, and programs that behave similarly sit near each other. If you had such a map, solving a puzzle would stop being "search through tangled code" and become "find the **right spot** on the map" — a much friendlier kind of search. The whole system is built to make that map and then move around on it. It has three parts:

- **An encoder** — a net that reads the two or three example pairs of a puzzle and squeezes them into a **first-guess point** on the map. *(Encoder = a net that compresses input down to a small code.)* This is like a human glancing at an ARC puzzle and thinking, in a fraction of a second, "something about shapes moving" — a rough hunch, not the answer.
- **A search step in the middle** — the heart of the system. The first guess is usually a bit wrong, so LPN **nudges the point** across the map to a better spot: it tries a nearby point, checks how well that point's program reproduces the examples, and keeps stepping toward points that fit better. Because the map is smooth, it can use the slope to steer — the same downhill-nudging idea as ordinary training, but done at answer-time, *on the map*, to find a better program.
- **A decoder** — a net that takes the final point and the new test input and **runs the program that the point stands for**, producing the answer grid. *(Decoder = a net that turns a code back into a full output.)*

Two things make LPN a real step past deep dive #2, and both matter for the frontier.

**It needs *no hand-built toolkit at all.*** Every solver in deep dive #2 searched over a DSL — a box of human-written operations. LPN has no DSL. The smooth map of programs *replaces* the toolkit: the "building blocks" are directions on the map, learned from data, not commands a person wrote. That is a direct attack on Part 1's problem — the human-laid foundation — from a completely different angle: don't hand-build the vocabulary, **learn a continuous one.**

**It is trained to be *searchable*, and it uses no internet priors.** The clever training trick: LPN switches the middle search **on during training**, so the map is not just built to store programs but shaped to be **easy to move around on** — a good first guess is always *near* a great answer. *(This is meta-learning — learning in a way that makes later learning fast; the system learns how to be searched.)* And LPN is trained from scratch on nothing but the 400 public ARC training tasks, blown up into many variants — **no pretrained language model, no internet knowledge.** That makes it one of the most honest **developer-aware** tests on the board (deep dive #1's idea: new even to the builder, because no outside knowledge leaked in). The payoff is modest but striking: about **10%** on the evaluation set with *zero* borrowed priors, and — the key finding — turning the middle search **on doubles** its score on out-of-distribution puzzles *(ones unlike anything in training)*. The search itself is doing the adapting *(as of the 2024–25 results)*.

Now the wall — and it is the most important thing on this page, because it is a wall the whole field keeps hitting. A single smooth map has a hard limit. Bonnet is blunt about it: a fixed continuous space can do **superposition** but not **composition.** *(Superposition = blending a few known programs into a mix. Composition = chaining programs into new, longer structures — do this, then this, then this.)* You can slide to a point that is a **blend** of programs you already learned; you cannot build an **arbitrarily long new chain** of steps that never appeared before. And long chains are exactly what the hardest puzzles need — deep dive #2's "too deep" wall, back again. So LPN reaches the easy and medium puzzles and stalls on the genuinely deep ones, the "type 2" problems that need real step-by-step construction.

That wall has a name and a long history. It is the **compositionality** problem — the free power to recombine known parts into endless new wholes — and it is the exact gap that [AP7 · neurosymbolic AI](../20-the-approaches/07_ap7-neurosymbolic-and-hybrid-ai.md) is built around (there it is called *systematicity*). Here it shows up sharp and located: neural program **proposal** can blend, but it cannot freely compose. The current research guesses are all about bolting composition **on top** — run several searches on the map at once and have an outer, deliberate process **stitch** their results into a chain — which is deep dive #2's System-1-guides-System-2 picture, one level up: the map is a fast intuition for single steps, and something slower has to compose the steps. Whether that works is open.

A sharp coda, because it reframes something from deep dive #2. Bonnet argues that the induction-vs-transduction split (deep dive #2's two answer styles) may be less important than it looks — what really matters, on this view, is **compression**: does the method squeeze the task into a *small* representation (a real rule) or not? *(This is deep dive #1's program-length ruler, used as the dividing line instead of "program vs grid.")* And it explains *why* Greenblatt's thousand-guesses approach is so wasteful. A language model, Bonnet notes, is **"exponentially not creative"**: if you plot how often it stumbles onto a genuinely novel program as you sample more and more, the good ones sit way out in a long tail *(the far, thin end of the range, where rare things live)* — you need *exponentially* more samples to reach them. The model *can* be creative, but only by brute-force sampling, which is the opposite of efficient. The hope behind searching a program space — rather than sampling a language model — is to reach that novel, compositional territory **cheaply**, without the exponential lottery. Whether a learned map can actually do that, past the composition wall, is the open bet.

## Part 3 — the efficiency frontier: when the score is not the point

Deep dive #1 proved that intelligence, on Chollet's definition, is **efficiency** — how much jump you get per unit of what you were given — and noted that ARC-AGI-2 rebuilt its leaderboard to score **cost** alongside accuracy. This page takes that one step further, into how the frontier competition is actually *shaped*, because the shaping changes what counts as progress.

Here is the shift, in Chollet's own framing at the ARC-AGI-2 launch: efficiency is **the problem statement, not a bonus.** The point is not "get the answer, and by the way try to be cheap." The point is: intelligence is *finding the answer in very few steps, with very little compute* — so a solution that needs a giant, expensive search has, in an important sense, **not demonstrated intelligence at all**, however high its score. Recall the card's headline number: OpenAI's o3 beat the first ARC by spending on the order of **$3,500 per puzzle** at its high-compute setting. Under the new framing, that result is impressive as *capability* and almost worthless as *efficiency* — paying thousands of dollars to solve a puzzle a child does for free is the very opposite of what the test measures.

So the competition is built to **forbid** the expensive road. The ARC Prize runs two tracks, and the top prize lives on the **efficiency** track: to claim it you must **open-source** your solution and run it inside a **fixed compute budget** on Kaggle *(a platform that runs everyone's code on the same limited hardware, so nobody can win by simply buying more machines)*. A method that only works at thousands of dollars per task is **disqualified by design.** As of the 2025 competition, the big prize stayed **unclaimed** — no one had reached the human-level bar *within* the budget — and the ARC Prize 2026 carries over **$2 million** in prizes, still built around that efficiency bar *(as of the ARC Prize 2026 announcement)*.

Why this matters to anyone who wants to *do* this research, not just watch it. The cost cap changes which ideas are worth having. It rewards exactly the mechanisms that are **cheap by construction** — a small model that adapts fast, a compact program search, a compressed map like LPN's — and it punishes "wrap a frontier model in a huge answer-time search." That is the frontier's quiet gift to small teams: the efficiency track is **winnable without a giant tech company's compute.** LPN got its ~10% on modest hardware with about 40 million parameters; deep dive #2's **CompressARC** outlier ran on a single gaming graphics card. The open research question the cap makes precise is the gap between two numbers: **what can an unbounded search do, versus what can a *cheap* solver do?** Closing that gap — matching the expensive search on a tiny budget — is the real measure of progress on this frontier, and it is where genuinely new ideas (not bigger models) still pay off.

## Part 4 — the agentic turn: ARC-AGI-3, when you don't even know the puzzle

The card mentioned **ARC-AGI-3** in a single line — frontier AI under 1%, humans 100%. That one line hides the biggest shift in the whole ARC story, so here is what ARC-AGI-3 actually *is*, and why it is a change of *kind*, not just difficulty.

Every ARC you have met so far — versions 1 and 2 — is **static**. You are shown all the example pairs at once, in a single frozen snapshot, and you output an answer. **ARC-AGI-3**, launched **25 March 2026**, breaks that mould: it is the ARC series' **first fully interactive benchmark.** Instead of frozen puzzles, it is *"hundreds of original turn-based environments, each handcrafted by a team of human game designers"* — little games you play move by move. And the defining rule, in the benchmark's own words, is what it takes *away*:

> "There are no instructions, no rules, and no stated goals."
> *(ARC Prize, ARC-AGI-3 launch announcement, March 2026.)*

Sit with that. You are dropped into a small unknown world and told **nothing** — not the controls, not the aim, not what "winning" even means. Before you can *solve* anything, you have to **figure out what the game is.** You must poke at it, watch what your actions do, build a picture of how this world works, guess what the goal must be, and then plan toward it — and carry what you learned in one level forward into the next. The benchmark names four abilities it is testing, and they are a ladder: **explore** (act to find out what is even here) → **model** (build an inner picture of the rules) → **set goals** (work out what winning looks like, since nobody told you) → **plan and act** (get there). Only the last rung is the kind of thing ARC-1 and ARC-2 tested; the first three are entirely new.

Why is this the *right* next step, and not just a gimmick? Chollet's argument, from the launch, is that the static tests were always a simplification of the real thing. In the real world you are **never handed the whole task**. You have to *collect* the information yourself — take actions, set your own goals, discover what your environment even is and what you can do in it — and do all of that **efficiently**, because in the real world being slow is punished (spend too long, take too many risks, and, as Chollet puts it, a predator eats you). So ARC-AGI-3 folds Part 3's efficiency and this new exploration into one test: gain the **most understanding of an unknown world for the least action.** It is the same intelligence the whole card is about — handling the genuinely new from little data — but now the "little data" is *data you have to go and get.*

And here the page's threads tie together. This agentic turn is exactly where Part 1 pointed. Recall Ellis' claim that you can only *falsify* an abstraction by **acting** in a world — that a library of tools is untestable until it makes real predictions you can check against reality. Ellis' own frontier project pushes precisely here: moving from **static** ARC toward an **"active"** ARC — a system that not only models and reasons but **acts**, with an action-space and the ability to intervene, so its abstractions can be tested by consequences rather than just declared useful. In other words, both the benchmark designers *and* the program-synthesis researchers arrived at the same door from opposite sides: **static grids were never enough; the road runs through a world you act in.**

That door opens onto the rest of the map. An agent that must explore, model, and plan in an interactive world is no longer doing pure program synthesis — it is reaching straight into [AP4 · RL from interaction](../20-the-approaches/04_ap4-rl-from-interaction.md) (learning from acting and its consequences), [AP5 · world models](../20-the-approaches/05_ap5-world-models-jepa.md) (building an inner model to plan against), and [AP10 · embodiment](../20-the-approaches/10_ap10-embodiment.md) (meaning earned by acting, not just told). AP8's newest test quietly concedes that program synthesis on frozen puzzles was one narrow probe of intelligence, and that the fuller thing needs its neighbours bolted on. The score, so far, marks how far there is to go: at launch, humans solved every environment and frontier AI managed *"Frontier AI scores 0.51%"* — a gap as wide as ARC-AGI-1's was back in 2019 *(as of the ARC-AGI-3 launch, March 2026)*.

## Part 5 — the bridge: program search that discovers new knowledge

Pull the camera all the way back. Every section so far pointed AP8's "search for a program" **inward** — at cracking a fixed puzzle you were handed. Now ask the question that turns the whole approach around: **what if you point the same idea *outward* — at a problem nobody has solved yet?**

You get a discovery engine. The clearest one is **FunSearch** (Google DeepMind, published in *Nature*, December 2023 — *"Mathematical discoveries from program search with large language models"*). Its machine is deep dive #2's **refinement loop**, scaled up and aimed at open science. It has three parts turning in a circle:

- a **language model** that **proposes** programs — small pieces of code, each one an attempt at a better solution-method;
- an automated **evaluator** that **runs** each proposed program and scores it — the exact **"you can run it" checker** from deep dive #2, now the load-bearing part;
- a **program database** that keeps the best programs found so far and feeds a few of them back to the language model as examples, so the next proposals build on the best of the last.

Round and round: propose, run, score, keep the good, propose again. It is an **evolutionary search** *(keep the best, make many varied copies of them, test those, keep the best again — improvement by breeding)* — but the crucial reframe, and the reason it works, is *what* it searches over. FunSearch does **not** search for the *answer* to a problem. It searches for a short **program that generates** answers. And that is the whole trick, straight out of deep dive #1's **program-length ruler**: a short program that scores well has captured a **general method**, not a single lucky answer — so when FunSearch finds one, it has found a **discovery**, something that works far beyond the cases it was tested on.

And it found things humans had not. On the **cap-set problem** *(a hard question in a corner of pure mathematics about the largest pattern-free set you can build — long studied, still open)*, FunSearch discovered a new construction better than any known — a genuinely new mathematical result. On **online bin-packing** *(the practical puzzle of fitting arriving items into as few bins as possible, without seeing the future)*, it found better packing rules than the standard hand-designed ones. This is AP8's own engine — **guided program search with an exact checker** — producing **new knowledge**, not just ARC points.

Now the bridge, and it is the reason this is the *last* AP8 deep dive. Watch what FunSearch becomes if you stop asking it to solve one fixed problem and just **let the loop run forever**, always chasing the next interesting improvement. That endless version is no longer "program synthesis for a puzzle." It is a process that **keeps generating novelty** — which is the whole subject of the next approach on the map, [AP9 · open-endedness](../20-the-approaches/09_ap9-open-endedness.md). AP9 already owns FunSearch's more powerful successor, **AlphaEvolve** — the same propose-run-keep shape, scaled up, which beat a matrix-multiplication record that had stood since 1969 (that result, and open-endedness itself, live on the [AP9 card](../20-the-approaches/09_ap9-open-endedness.md), so they are only pointed at here, not retold).

So the two roads meet. AP8 asks: *build one small program for one novel puzzle.* Run that exact loop — a proposer, an exact checker, a growing archive — **open-endedly**, and you get AP9's *keep generating novelty forever.* And that quietly answers Part 1's deepest question in a new way. **Where do the broad priors come from?** Perhaps not from a cleverer hand-built library at all — but from an **endless generating process** that keeps inventing new problems and new tools for itself, the way [AP9](../20-the-approaches/09_ap9-open-endedness.md) bets a mind grew out of evolution. That is the handoff. The program-synthesis road does not dead-end at a solved ARC; it **runs into open-endedness**, and one road's open problem becomes the next road's starting point.

## Putting the frontier together

Hold the whole edge in one view. Each frontier is a crutch deep dive #2 leaned on, and the research trying to remove it:

1. **Hand-built toolkit → grow the vocabulary in an open world** (Part 1). Soft neural libraries instead of explicit lists; dream-and-chase to stretch to harder problems; abstractions defined per problem, not one true set. *Unsolved.*
2. **Neural-*guided* search → nets that *propose* whole programs** (Part 2). Search a **learned smooth map** of programs (LPN) with no hand-built toolkit — blocked by the **compositionality** wall (blend yes, chain no). *Wall is real and old.*
3. **Expensive search → efficiency as the problem statement** (Part 3). The top prize is **cost-capped**, forbidding the brute-force road; progress = matching an unbounded search on a tiny budget. *The frontier's gift to small teams.*
4. **Static one-shot puzzles → the interactive, agentic turn** (Part 4). **ARC-AGI-3** hands you no instructions and makes you *explore* to find the puzzle — reaching into RL, world-models, and embodiment. *Frontier AI ~0.5%, humans 100%.*

And the road's destination (Part 5): the **same engine pointed outward** — a proposer plus an exact checker — already **discovers new mathematics** (FunSearch), and when you let the loop run forever it **becomes** [AP9 · open-endedness](../20-the-approaches/09_ap9-open-endedness.md). Program synthesis is not an island. Its open problems hand off to its neighbours.

## Judging the frontier: are these cracks, or dead-ends?

The first two deep dives judged things that *work* — a theory, a set of solvers. This page judges **open problems**, so the question is different: is each frontier a **crack that will close** with more work, or a **dead-end** that the road cannot pass? Be fair first: these are the live edges of a real, fast-moving field, and each has a serious research program behind it. Hold that. Now the four honest doubts.

### Stuck #1 — the priors problem may be irreducible

Watch what happens across Part 1 and Part 2. The "where do the priors come from?" problem keeps getting **pushed around** but never **removed**. A hand-built DSL (deep dive #2) → DreamCoder's grown library on a hand-built base → a soft neural library → LPN's learned map trained on a human-made task generator → and, in Part 5, an open-ended generator. At **every** stage, the system still starts from *some* human-chosen substrate: the core-knowledge grid format, the training-task generator, the language model's internet priors. Nobody has grown broad, general priors **from nothing.** So the sceptic's fair worry is that this is not a problem you *solve* but one you *relocate* — each new method hides the human contribution one layer deeper without eliminating it. Whether "priors from nothing" is even a coherent goal, or whether some seed must always be given, is genuinely unsettled. **[Contested — the field's oldest open problem, relocated many times, closed never; as of July 2026.]**

### Stuck #2 — the compositionality wall is decades old and still standing

LPN's "superposition, not composition" (Part 2) is not a fresh, LPN-specific bug. It is the **latest incarnation of a 35-year-old objection** — the systematicity critique that [AP7](../20-the-approaches/07_ap7-neurosymbolic-and-hybrid-ai.md) owns: neural networks blend, but they struggle to freely recombine parts into arbitrarily deep new structures. Every neural attempt to *propose* programs runs into it. The current fix — stitch multiple searches together with an outer, more symbolic process — is exactly the neurosymbolic marriage AP7 has been chasing (with, so far, only tidy-domain wins). So the honest status is: neural program proposal has **located** the wall precisely, but not passed it, and the wall is the same one the field has failed to climb for a generation. Betting it falls *this* time is a bet, not a result. **[Established that the wall is real and load-bearing; Contested whether latent/hybrid search scales past it.]**

### Stuck #3 — "efficiency progress" can be an illusion of cheaper hardware

The cost-capped frontier (Part 3) is a genuine advance, but it carries a subtle trap. A score inside a fixed compute budget can climb for **two very different reasons**: because someone found a **smarter, cheaper method** (real progress on intelligence), or simply because **hardware got cheaper and faster**, so the same brute-force search now fits inside the budget (no progress on intelligence at all). Unless you **hold the hardware fixed** and separate the two, a rising cost-capped score can mislead — it can look like solvers are getting cleverer when really the chips are getting bigger. Deep dive #1's warning applies with full force here: efficiency is the point, but "efficiency" measured against a moving hardware baseline is a slippery number. **[Contested — a measurement subtlety, not yet cleanly controlled for in public results.]**

### Stuck #4 — the agentic turn may just move the goalposts, again

ARC-AGI-3 (Part 4) restores a wide human-machine gap by adding exploration and interaction. But this is **exactly the card's Stuck #3** — *each ARC version falls, and a harder one restores the gap* — now happening at the level of the whole *paradigm*, not just a puzzle set. So the sharp doubt is the same one, one level up: is **interaction** a genuinely *new axis of intelligence* the test has uncovered, or is it just **today's blind spot** — the thing current systems happen to be worst at — dressed up as a game, so that the gap will close the moment agentic models catch up (the way o3 closed ARC-AGI-1)? And there is a second concession hidden inside the turn: by moving to interactive worlds, the field admits that **pure program synthesis on static grids was never the whole of intelligence** — it needs RL, world-models, and embodiment bolted on. Which lands AP8 on the **map-wide question** every card faces: is program synthesis a *separate road* to AGI, or a *component* the other roads absorb? **[Contested — the key open question; the versions-keep-falling pattern is documented, and now aimed at the method itself.]**

### The big question under all four

Every doubt here pulls the same direction, and it is worth stating plainly because it is where this whole three-part deep dive lands. **Is program synthesis a *destination*, or a *waypoint*?** Look at where each frontier hands off: the priors problem hands off to **open-endedness** (Part 5); the agentic turn hands off to **RL, world-models, embodiment** (Part 4); the discovery engine **merges into AP9** (Part 5); the compositionality wall hands off to **neurosymbolic AI** (Part 2). Almost nothing on this page stays *inside* AP8. That is the honest frontier read: program synthesis may be less a **rival road** to AGI than the **reasoning organ** — the "build a new procedure for a new problem" capacity — that the *other* roads have to grow around and inside them. The card's own Stuck #2 already showed the shape of this, when the thing that finally beat ARC-AGI-1 was [AP2](../20-the-approaches/02_ap2-reasoning-and-test-time-compute.md)'s test-time compute running on an [AP1](../20-the-approaches/01_ap1-scale-and-foundation-models.md) model — AP8's own cure arriving *inside* the approaches it set out to refute. *As of July 2026, whether program synthesis is a separate destination or a part every path needs is genuinely open* — and, tellingly, the most exciting work on this page is the work where AP8 stops being AP8. **[Contested — the key open question.]**

## How to use this (if you want to direct AI work)

- **Ask "where do the priors come from?" of any program-synthesis system — and follow the trace all the way down.** Every such system starts from *some* human-chosen substrate: a DSL, a task generator, a pretrained model's internet knowledge. When a demo claims a system "learns the abstractions itself," find the layer where a human still laid the foundation (Stuck #1). There is always one; the interesting question is how deep it is hidden and how much it is doing.
- **Test for composition, not just blending.** When a neural system is claimed to "propose programs" or "reason," push it on **depth**: can it chain genuinely new steps into a long, novel procedure, or only recombine patterns near its training data (Part 2's superposition-vs-composition)? A system that shines on short puzzles and collapses on deep ones has hit the compositionality wall — the oldest unclimbed wall in the field. Demand a long-chain example before believing "it composes."
- **Read cost-capped scores, and hold the hardware fixed.** A headline accuracy means little; the number that tracks intelligence is accuracy **inside a fixed compute budget** (Part 3). And when a cost-capped score rises, ask the Stuck #3 question: *smarter method, or cheaper chips?* Only a result that improves with hardware held constant is telling you about intelligence rather than about the hardware market.
- **For genuine discovery, look for the FunSearch shape.** If you want a system to find something *new* (a better algorithm, a new construction, a novel design), the durable recipe is **a proposer + an exact, cheap checker + a growing archive of the best-so-far** (Part 5). The checker is everything here — no exact score, no reliable discovery. If a "discovery system" cannot cheaply and correctly grade its own candidates, it is guessing, however fluent it sounds (deep dive #2's checker lesson, aimed at open problems).
- **Watch for the hand-off — and don't fight it.** The most valuable frontier work here is exactly where AP8 *stops being AP8*: the agentic turn reaching into RL and world-models, the discovery loop merging into open-endedness. When a program-synthesis project's next step is "put it in a world and let it act" or "let the loop run open-endedly," that is not scope-creep — it is the road merging into its neighbours (the big question above). Fund the merge; do not insist the approach stay pure.
- **What you delegate vs what you keep.** *Delegate:* building the LPN, wiring the FunSearch loop, writing the task generators and the evaluators, running the ARC-AGI-3 agents. *Keep for yourself:* tracing where the priors really come from, insisting on **composition** (not just blending), reading **cost as efficiency** with hardware held fixed, demanding a **real checker** for any discovery claim, and — the deepest one — judging whether a given result keeps program synthesis a **separate road** or shows it **merging** into a neighbour, because that judgement is the whole map's open question aimed at one card.
