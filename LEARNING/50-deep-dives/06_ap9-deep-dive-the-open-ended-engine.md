## In one minute

The card named four tools and made each sound different. Here is the surprise this page is built on: **they are all the same machine.** Under every open-ended method — old or brand-new — sits one loop, and the tools differ only in *two small slots* inside it.

1. **The one loop — keep, vary, keep again (the archive loop).** Hold a **collection** of the things you have made so far (an *archive*). Pick one and make a **changed copy** of it. Test the copy and decide, by some **rule**, whether it is worth keeping. Put the keepers back in the collection. Repeat forever. That is the whole skeleton. Every method below is this loop; they change only *how you make the changed copy* and *what rule decides you keep it.*

2. **"New" becomes a distance (novelty search).** To reward "different," you first stop looking at how a thing is *built* and look at how it *behaves*. Then "new" is simply: **how far this behaviour sits from the behaviours you have already seen** — the average distance to its nearest neighbours. Far out in empty space = new = keep it.

3. **"Keep every kind" becomes a grid you fill (MAP-Elites).** Chop the space of possible behaviours into boxes. In each box, keep only the single best performer found so far. The **filled grid itself** is the answer — a whole shelf of different, good solutions, which is exactly the card's "pile of stepping stones."

4. **"Generate the problems" becomes two rules (POET).** Only add a new problem if it is **neither too easy nor too hard** for your current solvers (the *minimal criterion* — this is "novel *and* learnable" made mechanical). And every so often, **test every solver in every problem and move it over if it does better** (*transfer*) — that transfer is the stepping-stone idea turned into a working subroutine.

And the punchline for the whole modern turn: the 2025–26 foundation-model systems (AlphaEvolve, the Darwin Gödel Machine) are this **exact loop**, with a language model dropped into two slots — it does the "make a changed copy" step (it *writes* a smarter variant instead of a random one), and, when there is no exact score, it also does the "is this worth keeping?" step. The card showed you the wins; this page shows the loop that produced them — and where that loop is quietly still stuck.

## One line of base, then we build

Two reminders, because the whole page turns on them.

- The card's tools were given as **slogans**: novelty search "rewards things for being different"; quality-diversity keeps "the best of every kind"; POET "generates the problems too." Each slogan is a promise about *what* the method wants. **None of them said how you compute it** — how a machine turns "different" or "worth keeping" into a number it can act on. **Parts 2–4 are that "how."**
- The card's deepest worry (its Stuck #1) was that **nobody can write down what "interesting" means.** Hold that. This page shows that in practice you do not write it down — you **choose a stand-in** for it, by hand, in every method. Watching *where* that human choice enters the machine is the thread that runs to the end (it becomes the engine's first crack).

Now the frame. The card judged the **bet** (should you grow a mind or design one? — its four cracks). This page is one level down: **how the growing-process is actually built, and whether the *machine itself* — apart from the bet — holds up.** So read every part as a piece of a machine: here is a job the card said must happen → here is the concrete mechanism → here is what it costs. We are **not** re-judging the bet. We are opening the engine the bet rides on, and only at the end asking whether the *engine's own* limits change the picture.

## Part 1 — the one shape under everything: the archive loop

Start with the reveal that makes the rest simple. Every open-ended method on the card, from the 2011 toys to the 2026 self-improving coders, is built from the **same three moves**, turning in a circle. Learn this skeleton once and you can read any of them.

1. **An archive** *(archive = a stored collection; here, a memory of the things the process has made so far — creatures, images, programs, agents)*. This is the process's growing library. It starts nearly empty.
2. **Variation** *(variation = making a changed copy of something)*. Reach into the archive, pick one thing, and produce a new version of it with some change — a small random tweak, or (later) a whole rewrite proposed by a language model. In evolution-language this changed copy is a **mutation** *(mutation = a small random change passed to an offspring)*, and the picked thing is its **parent**.
3. **Selection** *(selection = a rule that decides what to keep and what to throw away)*. Test the new thing, then apply a keep-rule: does it earn a place in the archive? If yes, store it (sometimes in place of something worse). If no, discard it.

Loop those three forever and you have an open-ended process. That is it. **The entire difference between novelty search, MAP-Elites, POET, and AlphaEvolve is which of two slots they fill differently: the *variation* step (how you make the changed copy) and the *selection* step (the keep-rule).** Everything else is shared plumbing.

### Why the keep-rule decides everything

Here is why this framing is worth the trouble. Take the most obvious keep-rule: **"keep the new thing only if it scores higher than the best I already have."** That is ordinary **optimisation** *(optimisation = pushing a single score as high as it will go)* — the keep-rule almost every other bet on the map uses. Run it and the archive collapses to **one thing**: the current champion, plus near-copies of it. Everything that is merely *different* — the odd, side-ways, not-yet-useful things — gets thrown away at once, because it scores lower than the champion today.

And **that is the objective paradox in miniature** — the card's central idea, so it is only pointed at here, not re-explained: the stepping stones to a great thing don't look like the great thing, so a keep-rule that only keeps "looks better than the champion" throws the stepping stones straight in the bin. So the real problem open-endedness has to solve is a **keep-rule problem**: *how do you decide what to keep so that you do not discard the strange, low-scoring stepping stones you will need later?* The next three parts are three different answers to exactly that question. (Notice the pattern to come: each answer works by refusing to reduce "good" to a single number.)

## Part 2 — novelty search, mechanically: turning "new" into a distance

The card's first tool said: *throw away the goal, reward things for being new and different.* Beautiful — but a machine cannot act on "new and different" until "new" is a number it can compute. Here is how you build that number. There are two mechanical ideas, and both matter.

### Idea 1 — measure how a thing *behaves*, not how it is *built*

This is the move that makes novelty search work, and it is easy to miss. When you ask "is this new?", you must decide *new in what respect.* Novelty search's answer: **not new in how it is built — new in what it does.**

- The way a thing is **built** is its **genome** *(genome = the full recipe/blueprint inside it — for a creature, its genes; for a robot brain, all its connection numbers; for a program, its code)*. Two things can have very different genomes and yet **do the exact same thing** (two different-looking robot brains that both walk straight into a wall). Measuring newness by genome would call those two "new," which is useless.
- The way a thing **acts** is its **behaviour**. Novelty search measures newness *here*. You, the builder, pick a few numbers that describe **what the thing did** — this is called a **behaviour characterization** *(characterization = a short description that captures the important features; here, a small list of numbers summarising the behaviour)*. For a robot let loose in a maze, the classic choice is just **the (x, y) spot where it ended up.** That pair of numbers is its behaviour. All the possible (x, y) endings together form the **behaviour space** *(behaviour space = the space of all the different ways a thing could act, laid out so that "acts similarly" means "sits close together")*.

Why this is the crucial trick: it collapses a huge, messy blueprint down to a tiny summary of *outcomes*, and it is outcomes we actually care about being new. Two robot brains that both end up in the top-left corner are "the same behaviour" no matter how differently they are wired.

### Idea 2 — "new" = far from the crowd (the novelty score)

Now that every thing is a point in behaviour space, "new" has an obvious meaning: **a behaviour is new if it sits in an empty region — far from all the behaviours you have already seen.** Here is the exact recipe (Lehman & Stanley, 2011):

- Keep an **archive of past behaviours** — the novel points you have already found.
- For a new behaviour, find its **k nearest neighbours** *(nearest neighbours = the handful of already-seen points closest to it; **k** is just how many you look at, say the 15 closest)* — the closest points in behaviour space, drawing from the archive and the current crowd.
- Its **novelty score** is the **average distance to those k neighbours.** This average is called the **sparseness** *(sparseness = how empty the space is right around this point; high = it is off on its own, low = it is packed in with lots of similar behaviours)*. In symbols, the sparseness ρ of a behaviour x is `ρ(x) = (1/k) · Σ dist(x, μᵢ)` — read plainly: *add up the distances to the k nearest neighbours μᵢ, and divide by k to get the average.* *(**dist** = how far apart two behaviours are; **Σ** = "add up over all of them.")*

High sparseness = "this behaviour is unlike anything I've seen" = **keep it, and add it to the archive.** That is the whole keep-rule. Notice it **never once looks at the goal.** There is no "is this closer to the exit?" anywhere in it. And that is exactly why it cannot be tricked by a **deceptive** problem *(deceptive = where heading straight for the goal walks you into a dead end — the card's maze, only pointed at here)*: a wall between you and the exit cannot fool a searcher that isn't aiming at the exit in the first place. It just keeps filling empty corners of behaviour space until, as a side effect, one of those corners is the exit.

### The seed of the first crack

Sit with one uncomfortable thing, because it grows into this engine's deepest problem. **You** chose the behaviour characterization. For the maze robot you picked "where it ended up." But you could have picked "how fast it walked," or "how often it turned left," or "how much battery it used" — and each choice sends the search exploring a *completely different* kind of "new." The method is sold as *goalless*, yet the moment you pick what to measure, you have quietly told it what kind of variety to care about. So the goal you thought you got rid of comes straight back — hidden inside the behaviour characterization. Hold that; Part 3 makes it sharper, and it becomes the engine's Stuck #1.

## Part 3 — quality-diversity, mechanically: MAP-Elites as a grid you fill

Novelty search has a real weakness the card flagged: chasing *pure* difference collects things that are merely different, and some of them are **rubbish** (a robot that falls over in a brand-new way is novel and worthless — the card's broken-TV worry, pointed at, not re-explained). **Quality-diversity** is the fix: don't ask only "is it new?", ask "**is it the *best* of its kind?**" Keep, for every distinct *kind* of behaviour, the highest-quality example you have found. The card gave the slogan; here is the machine, and its name is **MAP-Elites** (Mouret & Clune, 2015).

### The grid

Take the behaviour space from Part 2 (say, robot walks described by two numbers: *how tall* the robot holds itself and *how fast* it goes). **Chop that space into a grid of boxes** — this is called **tessellating** it *(tessellate = tile a space completely with boxes that don't overlap, like bathroom tiles)*. Each box is one "kind" of behaviour: "short and slow," "tall and fast," and so on. In each box you will store at most **one** thing: the best performer you have ever found whose behaviour falls in that box. That stored champion-of-the-box is called an **elite** *(elite = the single best solution found so far for one particular kind of behaviour)*. The whole grid of elites is both your working population **and** your final answer — this is the key twist: **the archive is the output.**

### The loop

The three moves of Part 1, made concrete:

1. **Start** by throwing in a handful of random solutions; each lands in whatever box its behaviour falls into.
2. **Vary:** pick a filled box **at random**, take its elite, and make a small changed copy of it *(perturb it — perturb = nudge/change slightly)*.
3. **Select:** run the copy, read off two things — **which box** its behaviour lands in (its **behaviour descriptor** *(descriptor = the address of the box a behaviour belongs to)*), and **how good** it is (its **fitness** *(fitness = the quality score — how well it did the actual job, e.g. distance walked before falling)*). Now the keep-rule: put it in that box **only if the box is empty, or the newcomer's fitness beats the elite already sitting there.** Otherwise throw it away.

Loop. Slowly the grid fills, and every filled box holds the best-yet solution of that kind. The output is not one winner — it is the **whole map** of diverse, high-quality solutions. In the authors' own words:

> "MAP-Elites produces a large diversity of high-performing, yet qualitatively different solutions, which can be more helpful than a single, high-performing solution."
> *(Mouret & Clune, *Illuminating search spaces by mapping elites*, 2015)*

This is why the method is called an **illumination** algorithm, not an optimisation one *(illumination = lighting up the whole landscape of what is achievable, everywhere, instead of climbing to one single peak)*. And it directly *builds* the card's "shelf of stepping stones": the odd corners — "tall and slow," which you would never chase if you only wanted the fastest walk — are kept anyway, and one of those odd corners is often the seed the next breakthrough grows from. Diversity and quality, held together on purpose.

### The first crack, now in plain sight

And here the seed from Part 2 becomes impossible to ignore, stated by the authors themselves. The grid's boxes are laid out along axes *you* pick:

> "It creates a map of high-performing solutions at each point in a space defined by dimensions of variation that a user gets to choose."
> *(Mouret & Clune, 2015 — emphasis added)*

*"That a user gets to choose."* The machine illuminates diversity — but **only along the axes a human hand-picked.** Choose "height and speed" and you get a beautiful map of heights and speeds; the machine will never discover diversity in some dimension you didn't think to name. The "goalless" explorer is exploring a space whose shape a person drew. Keep this; it is the engine's Stuck #1, and by now you have seen it enter the machine twice.

## Part 4 — POET, mechanically: generating the problems, and the two tricks that make it work

The card's boldest tool was **POET** — the first method that generates the **problems** as well as the solutions, so that nobody has to write the reward. The card said *that* it does this; it did not show *how* a machine keeps inventing problems that are actually worth solving. Two mechanisms carry the whole thing (Wang, Lehman, Clune & Stanley, 2019), and both are worth slowing down for, because each answers a question the card left open.

The setup, in one line the paper states plainly: POET "pairs the generation of environmental challenges and the optimization of agents to solve those challenges." So it runs a growing population of **pairs** — each pair is an **environment** *(environment = a problem/world to solve, e.g. an obstacle course for a simulated walking robot)* together with an **agent** *(agent = the solver being trained on that environment)* currently assigned to it. New environments are made by mutating old ones (a slightly different obstacle course). But that raises the two hard questions.

### Trick 1 — the minimal criterion: keep only problems in the sweet spot

**Question:** if you keep mutating environments, most new ones will be junk — either trivially easy or utterly impossible. How do you keep only the *useful* new problems?

**Answer — the minimal criterion.** A newly generated environment is admitted to the population **only if it passes a test**: it must be neither too easy nor too hard for the agents you already have. The paper's own description:

> "the minimal criterion is a lower and upper bound for the paired agent's fitness score that filters out environments that are either too simple or too complicated for the current population of agents."
> *(POET, Uber AI, 2019)*

Read what that does. **Too easy** (score above the upper bound — the current agents already ace it) → nothing left to learn, throw it out. **Too hard** (score below the lower bound — no agent can get anywhere) → unlearnable, throw it out. What survives is the narrow band in between: **new, and just barely solvable** — problems that stretch the current agents without breaking them. That band is the sweet spot, and it is precisely the card's **novel-and-learnable** test turned into a runnable filter (the card's abstract definition, now a lower-and-upper bound on a score). Keep the environments in that band and let them *drift harder over time as the agents improve*, and you have the card's **automatic curriculum** *(automatic curriculum = a sequence of problems that gets harder at just the right pace, generated by the system itself instead of written by a human — the card's phrase, pointed at)* — not written by anyone, but maintained by a bound on a number.

### Trick 2 — transfer: the stepping-stone idea, turned into a subroutine

**Question:** the objective paradox says the key to a hard problem is often a stepping stone that came from *somewhere else entirely* — so how does a skill learned on one problem ever reach the problem it secretly unlocks?

**Answer — transfer.** Every so often POET stops and runs a swap test across the whole population:

> "POET also periodically performs transfer experiments to explore whether an agent optimized in one environment might serve as a stepping stone to better performance in a different environment."
> *(POET, Uber AI, 2019)*

Concretely: take **every** agent and try it out in **every other** environment (either dropped in as-is, or given one quick practice step there first). Then the keep-rule: "Existing paired agents in the target environments are replaced if a transfer is better" — that is, **if some agent does better on environment B than B's own current agent, move it over.** 

This little subroutine *is* the objective paradox made mechanical, and it is the core of POET. A skill that an agent slowly learned in order to cross environment A may be exactly the unexpected key that cracks environment B — a key that direct training on B alone would **never** have found, because on B it looked like a step in the wrong direction. Transfer is how the stepping stone gets carried from the problem where it happened to appear to the problem where it turns out to matter. Without transfer, each pair is a lonely solver that gets stuck; with it, the whole population shares its accidental discoveries, and the search reaches paths no single line of effort could. (And note what this quietly settles — the card's point that POET answers AP4's *"who writes the reward?"* with *nobody*: here you can see the whole reward-writing job is done by the minimal-criterion bound plus the transfer swap, no human in the loop. The card made that claim; the machine above is why it is true.)

## Part 5 — the modern engine: the same loop, with a foundation model in two slots

Now the recent turn — the card's Leg 3, the reason big labs suddenly care. The 2025–26 systems look new and powerful, and they are; but mechanically they are **not a new machine.** They are the Part-1 archive loop, unchanged, with a **foundation model** *(one big model pretrained on the whole internet, everything else built on top — the AP1 idea, pointed at)* dropped into two of the loop's slots. Seeing that is the whole payoff of this page.

**Slot one — variation.** In the old methods, "make a changed copy" was a **random** mutation (a small blind tweak). Now a language model **writes** the changed copy — it reads the current best and proposes a *plausibly better* version, not a random one. This is a far smarter variation step: instead of blind tweaks, you get educated guesses. This is exactly the **FunSearch loop** from [AP8 · deep dive #3](03_ap8-deep-dive-the-research-frontier.md) — a model proposes, a checker scores, a database keeps the best (that page owns FunSearch's loop, so it is pointed at, not re-explained). **AlphaEvolve** (Google DeepMind, 2025 — the card owns its Strassen-beating result) is that loop scaled up: a **program database** feeds the best-so-far programs back into the prompt, an **ensemble** *(ensemble = a group of models used together)* of Gemini models *(Gemini = Google's large model family)* writes mutated versions (a fast model for many cheap guesses, a stronger model for occasional deep ones), evaluators score them, and the database keeps and re-feeds the winners. Same three moves — archive, vary, select — with the "vary" slot now filled by a foundation model.

**Slot two — selection, and the split that matters.** The keep-rule slot now comes in **two flavours**, and the difference between them is the sharpest new idea on this page:

- **An exact checker.** Where the answer can be **scored exactly and cheaply** — does this program pack the boxes into fewer bins? multiply the matrices in fewer steps? pass the test suite? — the keep-rule is a hard, honest number nobody can fake. AlphaEvolve and FunSearch live *here*, which is why they produce genuinely new, checkable knowledge (the card's and AP8 #3's results, pointed at, not retold).
- **The model as judge.** Where there is **no exact score** — is this new agent "interesting"? is this invented task worth learning? — you have nothing hard to measure against, so the **foundation model itself** becomes the judge, rating candidates the way a human might. This is the card's **OMNI** move (borrow "interesting" from a model trained on human text — pointed at). It fills the hole novelty search's designer had to fill by hand — but it fills it with a *frozen snapshot of human taste* (a crack we return to below).

**And the archive slot, too, is often the old machinery.** To stop the loop from collapsing to one winner, these systems keep a **diverse** archive — frequently a MAP-Elites-style map (Part 3) or a set of separate "islands" that evolve in parallel and occasionally share. The clearest example is the **Darwin Gödel Machine** (2025; published at ICLR 2026 *(ICLR = a top AI research conference)*), which keeps an archive of *coding agents* and grows it exactly as Part 1 describes:

> "The DGM maintains an archive of generated coding agents. It grows the archive by sampling an agent from it and using a foundation model to create a new, interesting, version of the sampled agent."
> *(Darwin Gödel Machine, 2025)*

Read that against Part 1 and it is move-for-move the archive loop: *an archive* (of coding agents), *variation* (a foundation model writes a new version), *selection* (keep it if it tests well, into a "growing tree of diverse, high-quality agents"). It lifted its own score on a standard coding **benchmark** *(benchmark = a shared, standard test used to compare systems)* from 20% to 50% — the card owns that number — by *discovering better tools for itself* along the way. The point for us is not the score; it is that **the old open-ended machinery of Parts 2–4 is literally running inside the newest, most powerful systems.** The foundation model did not replace the loop. It moved into two of its slots.

So the state of the engine, July 2026: a wave of self-improving-agent systems (the Darwin Gödel Machine and its 2026 successors, and related "AI scientist" loops) are all this archive loop with a foundation model as the variation operator and, where an exact checker is missing, the judge. The novelty search, the diversity grid, the minimal-criterion curriculum — none of it was thrown away. It was scaled up.

## Putting the machine together

Hold the whole engine in one view.

1. **The one loop (Part 1) — archive, vary, select.** Keep a collection; make a changed copy of something in it; keep the copy if a rule says so; repeat forever. *Every* method is this loop. Only two slots change: **how you vary** and **the keep-rule.** And the keep-rule is the whole game, because a naive "keep only if it beats the champion" throws away the stepping stones (the objective paradox, pointed at).
2. **Novelty (Part 2) — "new" as a distance.** Describe a thing by what it **does** (a behaviour characterization), not how it is built (its genome); then novelty = **average distance to the nearest neighbours** in behaviour space (sparseness). Keep the far-out ones. It never looks at the goal, so a deceptive problem cannot fool it.
3. **Quality-diversity (Part 3) — a grid you fill (MAP-Elites).** Tessellate behaviour space into boxes; keep the best **elite** in each; the **filled map is the output** — a shelf of diverse, high-quality stepping stones. "Illumination," not optimisation.
4. **POET (Part 4) — generate the problems.** The **minimal criterion** (a lower-and-upper fitness bound) keeps only problems that are new *and* just-solvable — "novel-and-learnable" as a filter, an automatic curriculum. **Transfer** (test every solver in every problem, move it if better) is the stepping-stone idea as a subroutine — the engine's real heart.
5. **The modern turn (Part 5) — a foundation model in two slots.** The same loop, with a model doing the **variation** (writing smart variants, not random tweaks — AlphaEvolve, DGM) and, where no exact checker exists, the **selection** (judging "interesting" — OMNI). The archive is often still a MAP-Elites map or islands. The wins ride on the old machinery, scaled.

## Judging the machinery: where the open-ended engine itself is stuck

The card judged the **bet** (four cracks: you can't measure what it optimises · is it a road or a loop bolted onto scale · it's expensive and stalls · it's the least controllable path). This page judges the **engine** — a different, narrower question: *set the bet aside; does the machine, as a machine, hold up?* It does, in part: it really can run for a long time, keep a diverse archive, and — in the checkable cases — discover genuinely new things. Be fair and hold that. But the machinery has its **own** cracks, and each one sharpens the card's by showing exactly *where in the loop* the trouble lives.

### Stuck #1 — the measure is not discovered, it is *chosen* — and the choice does the steering

The card's Stuck #1 said "nobody can define interestingness." The engine reveals the sharper, more uncomfortable truth: in practice you don't *fail to measure* it — you **quietly choose a stand-in for it, by hand, in every single method,** and whatever you choose secretly steers the whole search. You saw it enter three times: the **behaviour characterization** in novelty search (Part 2 — *you* picked "where it ended up"), the **grid axes** in MAP-Elites (Part 3 — *"dimensions of variation that a user gets to choose"*), and the **minimal-criterion bound** and **model-judge** in POET and the modern systems (Part 4–5). Each is a human choice, placed at the exact spot the method claims to have removed all human choices. A different behaviour characterization → a different world explored; a different judge → different "interesting." So the goalless engine is not really goalless; it is **goal-*hidden*** — the goal moved from an explicit target into the choice of what to measure, where it is harder to see and easier to get wrong. A bet whose whole selling point is "stop choosing a goal" turns out, mechanically, to choose one every time — just somewhere less honest. **[Contested — the mechanical face of the card's Stuck #1, and, I'd argue, a stronger statement of it.]**

### Stuck #2 — the loop is thin; the power is in the operator

Look again at the skeleton (Part 1): archive, vary, select. Written out, it is a few lines of bookkeeping. **All the real power lives in the two slots** — the **operator** *(operator = the part that does the actual work in a slot: here, the thing that makes the changed copy, and the thing that scores it)*. And in the systems that actually impress (Part 5), both of those slots are filled by a **foundation model.** So a hard question lands right on the card's Stuck #2 (*is open-endedness a real road, or a clever loop wrapped around scale?*), now made concrete and hard to dodge: **strip the foundation model out of the two slots and the identical loop crawls** — random mutations, tiny networks, toy mazes. That is not a guess; it is open-endedness's entire history before 2024. So a critic can now *see* the shape of the worry: the loop is a **wrapper**, and the intelligence is mostly in the **operator** you dropped into it. AlphaEvolve's new mathematics may be less "open-endedness works" than "a very strong model, sampled and filtered cleverly, works." The card records the defenders' reply (the *process* creates novelty a frozen model alone won't produce, and a model with no loop goes nowhere) — that reply is real and pointed at, not re-argued. The mechanical point this page adds is just: **once you see that the loop is three lines and the operator is a frontier model, it is the loop that has to prove it adds anything.** **[Contested — the mechanical face of Stuck #2.]**

### Stuck #3 — the real discoveries need an exact checker, and most of the world has none

This is a genuinely new crack the mechanism exposes, and it is the split from Part 5. The engine has **two** selection modes, and they are very different in how far you can trust them. Where a **cheap, exact checker** exists — matrix-multiplication counts, bin-packing efficiency, a test suite that passes or fails — the loop produces real, checkable, new results. That is AlphaEvolve's, FunSearch's, and the DGM's home, and it is the field's strongest evidence. But look at *what* has a cheap exact checker: a narrow slice of maths, algorithms, and code. The **general** open-ended dream — invent *interesting* new problems in an *open* world, the way evolution and culture did — has **no checker at all.** So there the engine must fall back to the **model-as-judge**, and the moment it does, the whole loop's taste is capped by a **frozen snapshot of human text** (the OMNI worry, pointed at) — which may be exactly the wrong compass for finding the strange, superhuman stepping stones the bet is supposed to be good at. So the crack: **the engine is powerful precisely where the world is a graded exam, and weakest exactly where the world is open** — which is where the whole bet needs it to be strong. Its best wins live in the small checkable corner; step out of that corner and the measurement problem (Stuck #1) comes straight back as "whose judgement, and how do you trust it?" **[Established that the two modes exist and differ sharply; how far the checkable corner can be stretched is Contested, July 2026.]**

### Stuck #4 — stalling is built into the loop, and every fix is a hand-added patch

Left to itself, an archive loop tends to find a few good things and then **stop producing anything genuinely new** — it **stalls** *(stall = the process keeps running but stops discovering; it settles into a boring rut)*. This is the card's Stuck #3, seen now from inside the machine: it is not bad luck, it is the loop's *default*. Which is why **every** real system adds a hand-built **anti-stall device** — and once you know to look, you see them everywhere on this page: the **novelty pressure** of Part 2 (reward difference so the search can't settle), the **diversity grid** of MAP-Elites (Part 3, force the archive to spread across boxes), POET's **minimal criterion + transfer** (Part 4, manufacture fresh problems and share escapes), and the modern systems' **islands** and the DGM's explicit choice to "keep exploring beyond immediate gains to avoid stagnation." These devices *work* — but here is the honest verdict, and it echoes exactly what the [AP5 · learning engine](05_ap5-deep-dive-learning-without-a-teacher.md) found with its anti-collapse guards (pointed at as a parallel, not re-taught): they are a **bag of tricks that keep the process alive,** not a principled guarantee that it climbs forever. Nobody has an open-ended loop *proven* never to stall; the card's own note stands that essentially no method is even meant to run for years. So "keeps inventing forever" is, mechanically, "keeps inventing until the patches stop working" — and when it will stop, no one can yet say. **[Established that stalling is the loop's default and the fixes are heuristic; a principled cure is unsolved, July 2026.]**

### The big question under all four

The card asked: *do you grow a mind with a goalless process, or design and aim at one?* The engine answers a narrower, mechanical version: **the "grow it" side now has a real, runnable engine — but does the engine run on its own, or on fuel a human keeps pouring in?** And the honest answer, from every part above, is the second. The archive loop genuinely solved the **plumbing** problem — how to keep a diverse collection, generate variation, and not immediately collapse to one champion. That is a real achievement, and it is why the recent wins exist (the card's Leg 3, pointed at). But the loop cannot supply its own two essentials: it needs a **human-chosen measure of interesting** (the behaviour characterization / grid axes / judge — Stuck #1), and it needs either an **exact checker** or a **frozen-taste model-judge** to score keepers (Stuck #3), while its "never stall" property is a set of **hand-added patches** (Stuck #4) wrapped around an **operator that does the real work** (Stuck #2). So: the *solved* part is the machine that keeps a process alive and varied; the *unsolved* part is exactly the card's — measuring interestingness, and keeping the process climbing **on its own, forever, in an open world with no exam to grade it.** **As of July 2026, open-endedness has a working engine and no self-supplied fuel — which is precisely where the bet still hangs.** The engine runs beautifully; what it runs *on* is still handed to it. **[Contested — the key open question, now located precisely: it lives in the gap between a loop that stays alive and a loop that steers and fuels itself.]**

## ⚠️ Honesty box

- **The archive loop is the durable core; the systems are snapshots.** *Keep a collection, vary something in it, keep the interesting variants, repeat* — and the three tools' answers to "what's the keep-rule?" — are load-bearing ideas that will still be central in a decade. The specific systems (**novelty search, MAP-Elites, POET, AlphaEvolve, the Darwin Gödel Machine, OMNI**) and their scores are 2011–2026 snapshots that will move. Learn the loop; treat the systems as illustration. **[Established for the mechanism; the specifics are dated.]**
- **The one idea to keep is the loop itself.** Of everything here, "every open-ended method is *archive → vary → select*, and it differs from every other only in how it varies and what it keeps" is the single most clarifying point. If you keep one thing, keep the loop — then you can read any new system on the frontier by asking which two slots it fills and how. **[Established.]**
- **"Goalless" is the engine's proudest claim and its least honest one.** Mechanically, the goal is never removed — it is **moved**, into the behaviour characterization, the grid axes, or the judge, where a human still chooses it (Stuck #1). Do not read "novelty search has no objective" as "no human decided what it explores." Someone always did. **[Contested.]**
- **The exact checker is what separates discovery from vibes.** AlphaEvolve's new mathematics is real *because* it could be checked exactly and cheaply (Stuck #3). Where there is no such checker, the loop falls back to a model's frozen taste, and "it found something interesting" means only "a model trained on human text liked it." Ask for the checker before believing the discovery. **[Established.]**
- **"It keeps inventing forever" is a hope with patches under it, not a proven property.** Stalling is the loop's default; novelty pressure, diversity grids, curricula, and islands are hand-built devices that delay it, not a guarantee against it (Stuck #4). No artificial open-ended loop has been shown to climb indefinitely. Treat "open-ended" as an aspiration the engineering is still chasing. **[Established / Contested.]**
- **Numbers and names age fast.** POET (2019), MAP-Elites (2015), novelty search (2011), AlphaEvolve and the Darwin Gödel Machine (2025), OMNI — these are dated snapshots in a fast corner. The lasting parts are the **archive loop**, **behaviour-space novelty**, the **illumination grid**, the **minimal-criterion + transfer** pair, and the **foundation-model-in-two-slots** picture. **[Established core, dated specifics.]**

## How to use this (if you want to direct AI work)

- **For any open-ended or evolutionary system, ask three questions: what is the archive, how does it vary, and what is the keep-rule?** (Part 1.) Those three answers *are* the system. Most of the design effort — and most of the bugs — live in the keep-rule and the variation step; the rest is plumbing. If a team cannot state all three crisply, they do not yet understand their own loop.
- **Find the hidden choice: who chose the behaviour measure, and what does it steer?** (Stuck #1.) Whenever someone sells a method as "goalless" or "unsupervised exploration," locate the behaviour characterization, the diversity axes, or the judge — because *that* choice is silently deciding what gets explored. Change it and you change everything. Never accept "it has no objective" without asking where the objective was actually hidden.
- **Demand an exact checker before believing any "discovery."** (Stuck #3.) A system that finds *new* results is trustworthy exactly to the degree its candidates can be scored cheaply and correctly (matrix mults, test suites, packing efficiency). If the only judge of "good" is a foundation model rating its own outputs, you have model-taste, not discovery, however fluent it sounds. No checker, no discovery.
- **Ask where the intelligence really lives — the loop or the operator.** (Stuck #2.) If your system is an evolutionary or search wrapper around a foundation model, honestly test what the loop adds *over just prompting the model well.* Prefer designs where the process demonstrably creates value the frozen model could not, or you may be paying a fortune to sample a model you could have queried directly.
- **Treat stalling as the default and budget for the anti-stall device from day one.** (Stuck #4.) Do not assume a loop "keeps improving." Assume it will settle into a rut, and decide up front what keeps it alive — novelty pressure, a diversity archive, a self-generated curriculum, islands — and how you will *detect* a stall when it comes. "We'll just let it run" is not a plan.
- **What you delegate vs what you keep.** *Delegate:* building the archive, wiring the variation and selection steps, running the evolutionary search, tuning the grid. *Keep for yourself:* choosing the behaviour measure (it is the hidden goal), insisting on a real checker for any discovery claim, judging whether the loop or the operator is doing the work, watching for the stall, and holding the open question — whether an open-ended engine can ever supply its *own* measure and fuel, or will always need a human choosing at the two slots.

## Connections

- **Keep only three things:** ① Every open-ended method is **one loop** — an **archive** of what you've made → **vary** something in it (a random tweak, or a model-written rewrite) → **select** the keepers by some rule → repeat. Methods differ *only* in the variation step and the keep-rule; and the keep-rule is the whole game, because "keep only what beats the champion" throws away the [objective paradox](../20-the-approaches/09_ap9-open-endedness.md)'s stepping stones. ② The three classic keep-rules, made mechanical: **novelty search** measures "new" as **average distance to the nearest behaviours** (in *behaviour* space, not *genome* space — it never looks at the goal, so it can't be deceived); **MAP-Elites** keeps the **best of every box** in a grid and hands back the **whole filled map** (illumination, not optimisation — the shelf of stepping stones); **POET** generates the *problems* too, kept in a sweet spot by the **minimal criterion** (a lower-and-upper fitness bound = "novel-and-learnable" as a filter) and shared by **transfer** (test every solver in every problem, move it if better — the stepping-stone idea as a subroutine). ③ The 2025–26 wins (**AlphaEvolve**, the **Darwin Gödel Machine**) are that *same loop* with a **foundation model** in two slots — writing the variants, and (where no exact checker exists) judging "interesting." So the engine is real, but it can't supply its own **measure of interesting** (you choose it) or its own **checker** (most of the world has none), and its "never stall" is a **bag of patches** — the solved part is keeping a process alive and varied; steering and fuelling it is not.
