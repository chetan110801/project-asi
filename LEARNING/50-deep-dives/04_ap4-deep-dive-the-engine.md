# AP4 · Deep dive — the engine: how trial and error becomes a better policy

*This is a **deep dive** past the [AP4 card](../20-the-approaches/04_ap4-rl-from-interaction.md). The card told you the whole bet — put an agent in a world, give it a reward, and let it learn by trial and error — and it named the moving parts: the **policy** (the agent's way of acting) and the **value** (its guess at how good a situation is). But it stopped at a wall. It said "learning means slowly improving the policy," and even that "**learning good value estimates is most of what makes an RL agent smart**" — and then never opened the box to show **how**. How does a machine that only ever gets a thin trickle of score actually turn that into a better way of acting? That "how" is the **engine** of reinforcement learning, and this page is the engine. It has one deep idea at its heart (learn a guess from a guess), two ways to turn learning into doing, one combination that wins, and one escape hatch from its worst weakness — and, as a bonus, the single most beautiful fact in the whole field: the maths at its heart was later found firing inside real brains. Then it ends by showing you that this exact engine is what trains the AI you use every day. Everything the card already said is referenced, not repeated.*

## In one minute

The card said an agent "learns by trial and error." Fine — but *learning* is not one thing. Underneath, the engine has four parts, and they stack:

1. **The heart — learn a guess from a guess.** The agent does not wait until the end of the game to find out how it did. After every single step, it makes a tiny correction: it compares what it *expected* with what *just happened plus what it now expects next*, and nudges its guess to close the gap. That one trick — called **temporal-difference learning** — is the beating heart of RL, and (the beautiful part) it is the exact signal that real **dopamine** neurons fire in living brains.
2. **Two ways to turn learning into doing.** Once the agent can judge situations, it still has to *act*. There are two families. One learns the **value of each action** and then picks the best one (this is how the card's Atari machine worked). The other skips value and **adjusts the way it acts directly**. Each is good at what the other is bad at.
3. **The combination that wins — actor-critic.** Have both: an **actor** that acts and a **critic** that judges. The critic's running judgement steadies the actor. This is the workhorse behind the card's Dota result — and, it turns out, behind ChatGPT.
4. **The escape hatch — build a model of the world.** Everything above learns *only* from real tries, which is why RL is so hungry for experience (the card's Stuck #2). The escape is to learn a **model** of the world and practise *inside your own head* — which is the whole of the next approach, [AP5 · world models](../20-the-approaches/05_ap5-world-models-jepa.md).

And the ending that matters most for 2026: this engine is not a museum piece. Take the actor-critic of part 3, make the "agent" a language model and the "reward" a learned judge of good answers, and you have exactly how today's chatbots and reasoning models are trained. **The engine the card hid is the engine running the frontier.**

## One line of base, then we build

Two reminders from the card, because the whole page turns on them.

- The agent's job is to improve its **value** and its **policy**. The card said value is "a guess at the size of the return from this state on" and called good value estimates "most of what makes an RL agent smart" — but it never showed the *update rule* that improves the guess. **Part 1 is that rule.**

The engine's first job is to *solve* it, cheaply, and it does so with one surprising move. **Part 1 is also that move.**

Now the one new frame this page adds. The card was about *what RL is and whether the bet is right*. ## Part 1 — the heart: learn a guess from a guess (temporal-difference learning)

Start with the single deepest idea in reinforcement learning. Everything else is built on it.

Here is the problem the card left open. The agent wants to learn the **value** of a situation — "starting here, how much total reward is coming?" But the reward is sparse and delayed: you might play a whole game and only find out at the very end whether you won. So how do you ever learn the value of a move made in the *middle*?

**The obvious way — and why it is too slow.** The plain answer is: play the whole game to the end, add up all the reward you actually got, and use that real total to correct your guess for every situation you passed through. This is called **Monte Carlo** learning *(a name borrowed from gambling — it means "learn from complete, played-out samples," the way you would estimate a dice game by rolling it many whole times)*. It works, but it has two heavy costs. First, **you must wait until the end** — no learning happens mid-game, and some tasks never really "end." Second, the played-out total is **jumpy**: one game's total is thrown around by every lucky and unlucky break along the way, so your correction is noisy.

**The trick that changed everything.** Temporal-difference learning — "TD" for short — refuses to wait. *(Temporal difference = the difference between two guesses made one moment apart in time.)* Its move is almost too simple to believe. Take one step. Now you know two things you did not know a moment ago: the **reward you just got**, and the situation you **landed in** — and you already have a guess for how good *that* new situation is. Add them: *reward just received + my guess for the new situation.* That sum is a slightly **better** guess for the situation you just left — because it contains one real piece of the world (the actual reward) bolted onto your old estimate. So nudge your old guess toward it. The textbook says it in one line:

> "TD methods estimate v(s) using an estimate of v(s). It bootstraps and makes a guess from a guess; it uses an estimated return instead of the actual return."
> *(Miguel Morales, Grokking Deep Reinforcement Learning)*

*(Here "v(s)" just means "the value of a situation." **Bootstrap** = pull yourself up using something you already have — here, improve a guess by leaning on another guess. It is the same word as "bootstrapping a business," lifting yourself with your own resources.)* Read it slowly, because it sounds circular and is not: you improve one guess by comparing it to *the same kind of guess made one step later, after a real reward has landed.* And that real reward is the escape from the circle. The textbook's own image is perfect:

> "That reward signal Rt+1 progressively 'injects reality' into the estimates."
> *(Grokking Deep Reinforcement Learning)*

*(Rt+1 = the reward that arrives on the next step.)* Each step adds one true fact — the actual reward — to a tower of guesses, and over many steps the guesses are pulled, bit by bit, toward the truth. You never have to wait for the end. You learn on every single step.

**The one quantity that runs the whole engine: the TD error.** Give the gap between the two guesses a name. Your old guess said "this is worth X." One step later, reward-plus-new-guess says "actually, more like Y." The difference, **Y − X**, is the **TD error** — and it is the most important number in the field. Read it as **surprise**: how much better or worse things turned out than you expected. If the TD error is positive, the situation was better than you thought — raise its value. If negative, worse — lower it. Learning, in RL, is nothing but *reducing surprise, one step at a time.*

And notice what this quietly solves. The card called credit assignment — "which of forty past moves earned the win?" — the nastiest problem. TD solves it without ever answering the question directly. Value flows **backward one step at a time**: a good final move raises the value of the situation just before it; next time through, that raised value raises the value of the situation before *that*; and so on, until, after enough plays, the credit has seeped all the way back to the clever move forty steps early. No detective work. Just a local nudge, repeated, letting good news trickle upstream. *(This is why RL agents need many plays: the credit has to seep back one link per play.)*

### The most beautiful fact in the field: the brain does this

Here is where the engine stops being mere engineering. Richard Sutton and Andrew Barto worked TD learning out as **mathematics** in the 1980s — an abstract rule for updating guesses, invented at a desk. It won them the 2024 Turing Award (the card noted this). But the astonishing part came from a completely different direction.

In the 1990s, neuroscientists recording from **dopamine** neurons — the brain's famous "reward" chemical — found something they could not explain with the old "dopamine = pleasure" story. The neurons did **not** fire for reward. They fired for *surprise about* reward. A monkey given juice it did not expect: a burst of dopamine. The *same* juice, once the monkey learned a light predicts it: **no burst** at the juice — the burst moved earlier, to the light. And when a predicted juice was **withheld**: a *dip* below baseline, exactly when the juice should have come. Put those three together and you are not looking at a pleasure signal. You are looking at **surprise about reward** — better than expected, as expected, worse than expected. You are looking at the **TD error**. A computational-neuroscience textbook states the link plainly:

> "the basal ganglia are bathed in dopamine, which drives learning in response to rewards and punishments"
> *(O'Reilly et al., Computational Cognitive Neuroscience; the dopamine-as-prediction-error link traces to Montague, Dayan & Sejnowski, 1996, and Schultz, Dayan & Montague, 1997)*

*(The basal ganglia = a set of deep brain structures involved in choosing actions and learning from their results.)* Let that land. An update rule invented on paper to make machines learn turned out to match, tick for tick, a signal the brain had been using for millions of years. This is not a loose analogy; it is one of the tightest bridges between artificial and biological intelligence we have. And it is a genuine point *for* the [AP4 card](../20-the-approaches/04_ap4-rl-from-interaction.md)'s "reward is enough" bet: the card argued a mind could be built on chasing reward, and here is evidence that a real mind's learning machinery **is** a reward-prediction engine. *(Honesty, kept for the judge-it section below: a match is not proof of identity, and the story has since grown more complex — but the core correspondence is real and has been deepened, not overturned, as of 2026.)*

That is the heart. One rule — *nudge your guess toward reward-plus-next-guess* — learns value on every step, seeps credit backward through time, and appears to be how brains learn too. Now: once you can *judge* situations, how do you use that to *act*?

## Part 2 — from judging to doing: the two families

Part 1 gave the agent a way to learn how good situations are. But knowing the score of a position is not the same as knowing what to *do*. There are two different answers to "turn learning into action," and the whole field splits along the line between them. The textbook draws the line exactly:

> "Value-based methods: Refers to algorithms that learn value functions and only value functions. Q-learning, SARSA, DQN, and company are all value-based methods. Policy-based methods: Refers to a broad range of algorithms that optimize policies."
> *(Grokking Deep Reinforcement Learning)*

Take them one at a time, in plain words.

**Family one — value-based: learn the worth of each action, then take the best.** Extend Part 1's value from "how good is this *situation*" to "how good is this *action*, taken here." That richer number has a name: the **action-value**, usually written **Q** *(just a letter, standing for "quality" of an action in a situation)*. If you know the Q of every action available to you, acting is trivial: **look at all your options and take the one with the highest Q.** That is the whole policy — be **greedy** for value *(greedy = always grab the best-scoring option right now)*, with an occasional random poke to keep exploring (the card's explore-vs-exploit). This is **Q-learning**, and the card's own headline — the **DQN** system that learned Atari games from raw pixels — is exactly this: a neural network that looks at the screen and outputs a Q for each button, and the machine presses the button with the highest Q.

Value-based methods have a real strength: they can be **sample-efficient**, because they can learn from old, stored experience over and over. *(This is called **off-policy** learning — you can learn from tries made by an older version of yourself, or even by someone else, because you are just estimating the value of situations, which does not care who visited them. Off-policy = you can learn from behaviour that is not your current behaviour.)* DQN kept a big memory of past frames and replayed them thousands of times. But value-based methods have a matching weakness, and it is fatal in some worlds: **"take the highest-Q action" needs you to compare all the actions.** If there are five buttons, easy. But if the action is "how much torque to send to each of a robot's forty joints" — a smooth, continuous dial with infinitely many settings — you cannot list and compare them. The "take the best" step breaks.

**Family two — policy-based: skip value, adjust how you act directly.** The second family throws out the middleman. Do not learn the worth of each action; learn the **policy itself** — the dial that maps a situation to what you do — and adjust that dial *directly* to earn more reward. The plainest version, **REINFORCE**, does exactly what the card's dog-and-treats picture suggested: try a whole episode, and if it went well, **make every action you took a little more likely**; if it went badly, a little less. Over many episodes, the behaviours that tend to pay off float up. *(The "gradient" in "policy gradient" just means: figure out which small change to the dial would raise the reward, and take a step in that direction — the same downhill-nudging idea used to train any neural network, here pointed at "more reward." Gradient = the direction of steepest improvement.)*

The policy family's strength is exactly the value family's weakness: because it never has to compare all actions, it **handles smooth, continuous, enormous action spaces with ease** — which is why every serious robot-control and the card's Dota-playing system are policy-based at their core. And it tends to converge more gently. Its weakness is the mirror image: it is **high-variance and sample-hungry.** *(High variance = the learning signal is noisy and jumps around, because it judges a whole episode by its final total — the very jumpiness Part 1's Monte Carlo suffered.)* And it is mostly **on-policy** — it can only learn from its *own current* behaviour, so it throws away experience after one use. Expensive.

So the field has two engines. One learns value and acts greedily — sample-thrifty, but stuck on small, listable action sets. One adjusts the policy directly — fluent in huge action spaces, but noisy and wasteful. Each is strong exactly where the other is weak. The obvious move is to want both at once.

## Part 3 — the combination that wins: actor-critic

You can have both. The idea that dominates modern RL is to run the two families **together**, and it has a vivid name: **actor-critic.**

- The **actor** is a policy (family two): it looks at the situation and *acts.*
- The **critic** is a value estimate (family one, learned by Part 1's TD rule): it watches and *judges* — "that was better than expected" or "worse."

Watch how they fix each other. The pure policy method of Part 2 was high-variance because it had to wait for the final total to know whether an action was good. But the critic can deliver that verdict **immediately**, on every step, using the **TD error** from Part 1 — *surprise* is exactly the signal "that action turned out better/worse than I expected." So the actor no longer waits for the end of the episode; it gets a crisp, low-noise nudge after each move, straight from the critic. The critic tames the actor's variance; the actor handles the huge action spaces the critic alone could not. The textbook's definition ties it back to Part 1 precisely:

> "Actor-critic methods: Refers to methods that learn both a policy and a value function, primarily if the value function is learned with bootstrapping and used as the score for the ... policy gradient."
> *(Grokking Deep Reinforcement Learning)*

*(There it is: the critic learns "with bootstrapping" — that is Part 1's guess-from-a-guess — and its verdict becomes the "score" the actor learns from.)* This is not a niche trick; it is the shape of the workhorse. The single most widely used RL algorithm today, **PPO** *(Proximal Policy Optimization — Schulman et al., 2017; "proximal" just means it takes **careful, small steps**, never lurching the policy too far in one update, which keeps training stable)*, is an actor-critic method. The card's OpenAI Five (Dota) was trained with PPO. And — the point we return to in Part 5 — so was the first version of ChatGPT.

There is a lovely symmetry to notice here, because it shows the three parts so far are really **one** machine. The critic is Part 1 (TD learning). The actor is Part 2's policy family. Actor-critic is Part 3. The whole engine is: *a policy that acts, a value that judges by surprise, and the surprise steering the policy.* Hold that as the finished core. The last two parts ask what the core still cannot do — and where that pushes the road next.

## Part 4 — the escape hatch: learn a model, and practise in your head

The whole engine so far shares one deep property, and it is the root of the card's second crack. Everything in Parts 1–3 is **model-free**: the agent has **no picture of how the world works.** It never learns "if I do this, that happens." It only learns, from raw experience, a value or a policy — a reflex, tuned by reward. A model-free agent that has mastered a game still cannot tell you what any move *does*; it only knows which move tends to score.

That is precisely **why RL is so hungry for experience** — the card's Stuck #2, now explained from the inside. A model-free agent squeezes every drop of its knowledge out of the thin trickle of the reward signal, one TD nudge at a time. To learn a lot, it must try a *lot.* There is no shortcut, because it never built an understanding it could reason from — only a reflex it slowly tuned. AlphaZero's millions of games and OpenAI Five's ten months are the price of being model-free.

So here is the escape hatch, and it is the other great branch of the field. Instead of learning only a reflex, **learn a model of the world** — a predictor of "situation + action → next situation + reward" — and then do your trying **inside the model**, in imagination, for free. Play out thousands of imagined futures in your head, see which lead to reward, and act on the best — without paying for a single real try. This is **model-based** RL, and its logic is a direct assault on the sample-inefficiency wall: real experience is precious and slow, so learn a cheap copy of the world and burn *that* instead.

The landmark to know is **MuZero** (Schrittwieser et al., *Nature*, 2020). It is the successor to the AlphaZero you met on the card — but with a stunning twist. AlphaZero was *given* the rules of chess and Go. MuZero is **not told the rules at all.** It **learns its own model** of the game's dynamics from scratch — just enough of one to plan against — and then plans inside that self-made model by imagining move sequences and picking the best. It reached superhuman play in Go, chess, shogi *(Japanese chess)* *and* the Atari games, **with no rulebook handed in.** A machine that learns a working model of a world it was never explained, then thinks ahead inside it — that is the model-based dream made real.

And here the road runs straight into its neighbour. "Learn a model of the world and plan inside it" is not a footnote to AP4 — it is the **entire thesis of the next approach on the map**, [AP5 · world models](../20-the-approaches/05_ap5-world-models-jepa.md). AP5 is the deep home for how you *build* such a model from raw video, why predicting a useful summary (not every pixel) is the trick, and the systems doing it (its DreamerV3 literally trains an agent inside a learned "dream" — the model-based idea in its purest form). So this page hands the model-based branch **to AP5 and does not re-teach it.** The point to keep here is only the fork itself: **model-free** (learn a reflex from real tries — simple, general, desperately sample-hungry) versus **model-based** (learn a world and plan in it — sample-efficient, but only as good as the model it learned). The card's biggest practical crack, sample-inefficiency, is exactly the cost of choosing the first; the second is the escape, and the escape is a whole approach of its own.

## Part 5 — the bridge: this engine now trains the AI you use

Pull the camera back to 2026. The card made a sharp observation: reinforcement learning "came back" not as a lone agent in a game, but **inside big text models** — and warned you not to mistake "RL is everywhere again" for "the reward-is-enough bet has been won." This page can now show you *the mechanism* behind that revival, because it is nothing but the engine you just built, pointed at a language model. *(This part is a bridge — the full story lives on other cards, flagged as we go — so it stays a sketch, not a re-teach.)*

Here is the whole move, in one breath. Take the **actor-critic** engine of Part 3. Make the **actor** a language model — its "actions" are the words it writes. Now you need a **reward**. But who writes it? This was the card's Stuck #1: in the real world, *someone has to turn "a good answer" into a number*, and that is hard. The frontier's answer is elegant and slightly unnerving: **learn the reward too.** Show people pairs of answers and ask which they prefer; train a second model — a **reward model** — to predict those preferences; and then let that learned model hand out the score. This is **RLHF** — *Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback* — and it is how the first ChatGPT was turned from a raw text-predictor into a helpful assistant. The algorithm underneath is **PPO**, the actor-critic workhorse from Part 3, now juggling four pieces at once: the actor (the policy being trained), the critic (value), the learned reward model, and a "reference" copy of the original model to keep the trained one from drifting too far from sensible language *(as of 2025, PPO remains the standard RLHF algorithm)*.

Notice what just happened to the card's cracks — because the engine does not escape them, it **relocates** them. "Who writes the reward?" is answered by a *learned* reward model — clever, but now the agent can **hack the reward model** instead of a hand-written rule (learn to produce answers the judge-model *rates* highly but humans would not — flattery, confident nonsense). The card's reward-hacking crack did not close; it moved up one floor. Hold that thought for the judge-it below.

And the newest turn, the one driving 2025–26 reasoning models: swap the *learned* reward for a **checkable** one. For maths and code you do not need a preference model — you can just **run the code or check the final answer**, and reward the model only when it is *provably right.* This is **RL from verifiable rewards** (its home is the [AP2 card](../20-the-approaches/02_ap2-reasoning-and-test-time-compute.md)'s reasoning story), and the engine of choice shifted too: **GRPO** *(Group Relative Policy Optimization — introduced by DeepSeek, the method behind the DeepSeek-R1 reasoning model, 2024→2025)*, which **drops the critic entirely** — instead of learning a value function, it samples a *group* of answers to the same question and scores each one **relative to the group average.** *(That is a real design choice with a Part-2 flavour: it is a policy-gradient method that replaces the learned critic with a cheap, on-the-spot baseline — the group's own average — sidestepping the deadly triad the judge-it below describes.)* GRPO drove the 2025 reasoning-model boom; a wave of variants (DAPO, GSPO, and more) followed through 2026.

So the bridge is built, and it runs both ways. The **engine** of this page — TD-learned value, a policy adjusted by gradient, the two married as actor-critic — is not a game-playing curiosity. **It is the exact machinery training the frontier models of 2026.** But — and this is the card's warning, now precise — what changed at the frontier is mostly the *engine*, bolted onto an [AP1](../20-the-approaches/01_ap1-scale-and-foundation-models.md) model that already knew almost everything from human text. The pure AP4 dream (an agent growing a whole mind from reward in a world, from scratch) is **not** what RLHF and GRPO are. They are the reward-engine sharpening a mind that scale already built. The full story of how RL shapes language models — reward models, preference data, the sycophancy problem, verifiable rewards at scale — is big enough to be its own deep dive, and it belongs to [AP2](../20-the-approaches/02_ap2-reasoning-and-test-time-compute.md) and the [alignment page](../30-across-the-approaches/02_alignment-control-and-self-improvement.md) as much as to AP4. Consider it pointed at, and queued.

## Putting the engine together

Hold the whole machine in one view.

1. **The heart (Part 1) — temporal-difference learning.** Learn value by nudging each guess toward *reward + next guess* — "a guess from a guess," with real reward injecting truth one step at a time. The gap is the **TD error** = surprise, and it seeps credit backward through time. Real **dopamine** neurons fire this same signal.
2. **Two families (Part 2).** **Value-based** (learn each action's worth **Q**, act greedily — DQN/Atari; sample-thrifty and off-policy, but stuck on huge action sets) vs **policy-based** (adjust the way you act directly — robots/Dota; fluent in continuous actions, but noisy and sample-hungry).
3. **The winner (Part 3) — actor-critic.** An **actor** acts, a **critic** judges by TD-error surprise; the critic tames the actor's noise, the actor handles the actions the critic could not. **PPO** is the workhorse.
4. **The escape hatch (Part 4) — model-based.** All of the above is **model-free** (a reflex learned from real tries — which is *why* it is so sample-hungry). The escape is to **learn a world model and plan inside it** (**MuZero** learns its own; the branch's deep home is [AP5](../20-the-approaches/05_ap5-world-models-jepa.md)).

And the bridge (Part 5): this engine — actor-critic with a reward — is precisely what trains 2026's chatbots (**RLHF/PPO**) and reasoning models (**verifiable rewards/GRPO**). The card's "RL came back" is this engine, pointed at a scaled language model.

## Judging the engine: where the machinery itself is stuck

The card judged the *bet*. This page judges the *engine* — a different question. The engine is real, it works, and it powers the frontier; be fair and hold that. But the machinery has its own cracks, separate from the bet's, and they are worth knowing because they shape what is and is not possible.

### Stuck #1 — the deadly triad: the three things you most want to combine, fight

Here is a hard, specific fact that surprises newcomers. The three most *desirable* ingredients of modern RL — **(a)** deep neural networks (to handle rich inputs like pixels), **(b)** bootstrapping (Part 1's guess-from-a-guess, for cheap online learning), and **(c)** off-policy learning (Part 2's reuse of old experience, for sample-efficiency) — are individually wonderful and, **taken all three together, can blow up.** The estimates can spiral away instead of settling. The field's own name for this is blunt:

> "there's something called the 'deadly triad,' that basically tells us using neural networks with bootstrapping and off-policy is bad."
> *(Grokking Deep Reinforcement Learning)*

Why it matters: it means the clean, provable RL theory — which mostly assumes small, listable worlds where everything is stable — **does not simply transfer** to the deep, high-dimensional worlds we actually care about. A great deal of deep-RL engineering (target networks, replay tricks, the "careful small steps" baked into PPO, GRPO's dropping of the learned critic) is really a **collection of patches to keep the triad from exploding.** So a fair verdict on the engine is: it is powerful but **temperamental** — the theory is beautiful in the tabletop case and fragile in the real one, and much of the craft is stabilisation. **[Established — the deadly triad is a well-known, load-bearing limitation of deep RL.]**

### Stuck #2 — sample-inefficiency has a structural root, and model-based only trades it

Part 4 explained *why* model-free RL is so hungry: it learns a reflex from a one-number reward, so it must try enormously. The honest frontier point is that the two escapes each just **move** the cost. **Model-based** planning (Part 4) buys sample-efficiency but pays in **model error** — you now plan against a *learned* copy of the world, and if the copy is wrong, your beautiful plan is confidently wrong (planning inside a flawed dream). **Reward models** (Part 5) buy a usable reward but pay in **hackability** (below). There is no free lunch here: the thin reward signal is baked into what RL *is*, and every method that eases the hunger takes on a new liability somewhere else. Whether some approach genuinely *dissolves* the inefficiency, rather than relocating it, is an open research question, not a solved one. **[Established as the root; Contested whether any current fix truly removes rather than moves it.]**

### Stuck #3 — the dopamine match is real, but do not over-read it

Part 1's dopamine story is one of the most cited bridges between AI and neuroscience, and it is genuinely strong. But a match between a signal and a model is **not proof the brain "is doing" that algorithm**, and the science has moved past the tidy 1997 picture. Dopamine now appears to carry **more** than a single scalar surprise: the 2020 work of Dabney and colleagues (*Nature*) found dopamine neurons behave like a **distributional** learner — many neurons encoding a whole *spread* of possible future rewards, not one average — and follow-up work through 2025 has enriched the map further. That is the reward-prediction-error idea being **deepened, not demolished** — but it is a warning against the lazy leap from "dopamine looks like TD error" to "the brain is a reinforcement learner and the card's bet is basically proven." The correspondence supports the bet; it does not settle it. **[Established that the correspondence is real and deepening; Contested how completely "the brain does RL" is true.]**

### Stuck #4 — a better engine does not touch the bet's cracks (and can sharpen one)

This is the most important doubt, and it ties the engine back to the card. Everything on this page makes the agent a **better optimizer** — better at squeezing reward out of a world. But re-read the card's four cracks: reward hacking, needing astronomical experience, one number being too poor for real goals, and "enough" being untestable. **Not one of them is a flaw in the engine — they all live *above* it, in the choice of reward and the leap to the open world.** A better engine optimizes a **flawed reward** *better*, which is worse, not better: Part 5 already showed reward hacking climbing a floor (from gaming a hand-written rule to gaming a learned reward model — flattery, confident nonsense). So the engine's progress and the bet's cracks are almost **orthogonal** *(independent — improving one does not improve the other)*. This is the mechanism-level echo of the card's big question, and it deserves stating flatly: **a more powerful engine makes reinforcement learning a stronger tool without making the "reward is enough" bet any more likely to be true.** The engine is not the bottleneck; the reward and the world are. **[Contested — but it is the sharp, honest frontier read: engine progress ≠ progress on the bet.]**

### The big question under all four

The card asked whether reward is the *source* of intelligence or a *tool* it uses. The engine answers a narrower, mechanical version of the same thing: **is this machinery the seed of a mind, or a very good optimizer that a mind would use?** Everything here points at the second. TD learning, actor-critic, model-based planning — these are superb general-purpose *optimizers*, and the strongest recent proof of that is that they turned out to be exactly what sharpens a scaled language model (Part 5). But an optimizer needs something to optimize — a reward, a world — and *those* are where intelligence is actually decided, and *those* are what the engine cannot supply. **As of July 2026, the engine is arguably the most solved part of the whole AP4 story — and the fact that the solved part is the optimizer, while the unsolved part is the reward and the world, is itself the deepest thing the engine teaches.** **[Contested — the key open question, now sharpened to: the engine works; the hard part was never the engine.]**

## ⚠️ Honesty box

- **The engine is durable; the systems and scores are snapshots.** *Temporal-difference learning, the TD error, value-based vs policy-based, actor-critic, model-free vs model-based* — these are 40-year-old, load-bearing ideas that will still be true in a decade. The specific systems (**DQN, AlphaZero, OpenAI Five, MuZero, PPO, GRPO**) and their framings are 2013–2026 snapshots that will move. Learn the mechanisms; treat the systems as illustration. **[Established for the mechanisms; the specifics are dated.]**
- **TD learning is the single most durable idea on this page.** Of everything here, "learn a guess from a guess, and let real reward inject truth one step at a time" is the one most likely to still be central in ten years — it is the mechanism credit assignment is actually solved with, and the one with a foot in real neuroscience. If you keep one thing, keep the TD error as *surprise.* **[Established — the durable core.]**
- **The dopamine tie is a real bridge, not a proof.** It is one of the tightest AI-neuroscience correspondences we have, and it genuinely supports the card's bet — but "dopamine looks like the TD error" is not "the brain is a reinforcement learner," and the picture has grown richer (distributional coding) since 1997 (Stuck #3). Hold it as strong evidence, not a settled identity. **[Established / Contested.]**
- **A better engine is not progress on the bet.** The most counter-intuitive and important point: sharpening the optimizer (this whole page) does **not** make "reward is enough" more likely, and can make reward hacking *worse* by optimizing a flawed reward harder (Stuck #4). Do not read "RL's engine keeps improving" as "the AP4 bet is being won." **[Contested — but the honest frontier read.]**
- **"RL trains the frontier" is true and easy to over-read.** RLHF and GRPO really are this engine, and they really did make chatbots and reasoning models work (Part 5). But they are the engine bolted onto a **scaled language model that already knew almost everything** — not a pure reward-agent growing a mind from scratch. The revival is real; the *pure* bet is not what got revived (the card's own warning, now mechanical). **[Established.]**

## How to use this (if you want to direct AI work)

- **First fork on any RL plan: model-free or model-based?** (Part 4.) Model-free is simple and general but needs a *fortune* of tries — sane only where experience is cheap and safe (a fast simulator). Model-based is sample-efficient but only as good as the world-model it learns — and if you are learning a world model, you are really doing [AP5](../20-the-approaches/05_ap5-world-models-jepa.md), with model-error as your new risk. Ask which cost your project can actually pay.
- **Second fork: value-based or policy-based?** (Part 2.) Small, listable set of actions and you want to reuse old data → value-based (Q-learning/DQN family). Smooth, continuous, high-dimensional actions (robot control, generating text) → policy-based, almost always as **actor-critic** (PPO and its kin). A team reaching for pure value-based methods on a continuous control problem has usually mis-chosen.
- **Expect the deadly triad, and recognise the patches.** (Stuck #1.) If a deep-RL system is unstable — scores that soar then collapse, training that diverges — suspect the triad (deep nets + bootstrapping + off-policy), not a bug in your reward. Much of the standard toolkit (target networks, small trust-region steps, GRPO dropping the critic) exists to fight it. Instability is often structural, not accidental.
- **When you see "we trained it with RL," ask what the reward model is — and how it is gamed.** (Part 5.) Frontier "RL" usually means RLHF or verifiable-reward RL on a pretrained model. The interesting question is never the engine (it works); it is the reward: is it a *learned* preference model (hackable → flattery, confident wrong answers) or a *checkable* verifier (robust, but only where answers are checkable, i.e. maths and code)? The reward, not the algorithm, decides whether the result is trustworthy.
- **Do not confuse engine progress with bet progress.** (Stuck #4.) A better RL algorithm is a better *optimizer* — genuinely valuable — but it optimizes whatever reward you hand it, harder. It moves you toward the number, not toward your intention. Fund engine work for what it is (a sharper tool); do not let "our RL got better" masquerade as "we are closer to a mind."
- **What you delegate vs what you keep.** *Delegate:* implementing PPO/GRPO, tuning the network, building the simulator, wiring the training loop. *Keep for yourself:* choosing model-free vs model-based (the affordability call), choosing and **defending the reward** against the engine's own hacking (the card's Stuck #1, now sharper because the engine is stronger), and never mistaking a more powerful optimizer for progress on the actual bet.
