## In one minute

Deep dive #1 found the body only drives a web-built mind. The whole point of this frontier is to change that — to let the robot learn from **its own** actions. Open it and you find five things.

1. **Imitation has a ceiling, and that ceiling is why the frontier exists.** Copying a human's demonstrations (behavioral cloning, deep dive #1's bottom-of-the-pyramid skill) can never *beat* the human, and worse, it **drifts**: the moment the robot makes a small error it lands in a situation the human never demonstrated, so it errs more — errors compound *quadratically* with the length of the task. To go past the demonstrator, and to recover from your own mistakes, you need to learn from **outcomes** — from what your body actually caused. That is reinforcement learning, and it is the true "grown through a body" loop the card wanted.

2. **But learning in a real body hits three walls that don't exist in a chatbot.** RL is **sample-hungry** (AP4's crack) and every sample here is a physical, real-time, breakable robot-second — you cannot run a million years of practice overnight. There is **no reward** waiting in the real world — nothing tells the robot it succeeded. And every trial needs a **reset** (someone to stand the blocks back up) and must be **safe** (a flailing arm breaks itself and its surroundings). Online interaction, in Levine's words, is "impractical, either because data collection is expensive … and dangerous." These three walls are why imitation dominated for so long.

3. **The field scales the walls with a stack of tricks — and every trick leans on something web-trained.** You don't explore from scratch; you **start from imitation and only fine-tune with RL** (so the robot needs far fewer real trials — HIL-SERL, π\*0.6). You mine the **already-collected logs** without new risky interaction (**offline RL**). And you solve the missing-reward wall by importing a reward from a foundation model — an **LLM writes the reward code** (Eureka beats human-designed rewards on 83% of tasks) or a **VLM watches and judges success** (VLAC lifts success from ~30% to ~90%). Notice the pattern: the escape from *the body's* reward wall is *the web's* judgment.

4. **The one mechanism where the body genuinely authors something new is the world-model.** Instead of inheriting frozen web features, the robot learns a **predictive model of its own sensorimotor consequences** — "if I send *this* command, *this* is what my camera will see next" — and then improves by **imagining** rollouts inside that self-learned model. This is the old **World Models** idea (train the controller "inside its own hallucinated dream") made real by **Dreamer / DayDreamer**, which learned a physical quadruped to roll over, stand, and walk **from scratch, no simulator, no demonstrations, in about an hour.** A model learned from *your own body's* interaction is grounding the web could not give — the physics of *this* body.

5. **So the honest 2026 answer splits.** Learning from experience genuinely lifts the robot **past** its human demonstrator (π\*0.6 reportedly doubles throughput and halves failures) and lets the body author a **model of its own physics** — both are more than deep dive #1's "hands." But the **concepts** (what a mug is, what "clear the table" means), the **goals**, and increasingly even the **practice** (generative simulators dreaming up experience) still come from web-trained foundation models. The body now authors the **how**; the web still authors the **what**. The card's strong bet — the body as the *source* of the mind — is, for the first time, being tested at the level of mechanism; as of 2026 it is half-won and half-open.

Deep dive #1 gave you a mind with fast hands. This page shows the hands starting to **teach themselves** — and asks whether teaching yourself to *move* ever becomes teaching yourself to *mean*.

## Part 1 — the ceiling: why copying a human can never be the whole answer

Start where deep dive #1 stopped. The robot's skill, it found, is taught by **imitation** — behavioral cloning on teleoperated demonstrations. That works, and it is how every shipping VLA is bootstrapped. But it has a ceiling built into its logic, and seeing the ceiling is the reason the entire rest of this page exists. *(One-line reminder, then we build: behavioral cloning = treat each demonstrated situation-then-action pair as a supervised example and train the policy to reproduce the human's action. Deep dive #1 owns the setup; here we open why it caps out.)*

### Two limits, one fatal

The first limit is obvious once said: **a policy trained to copy a demonstrator can, at best, match the demonstrator.** It has no signal that says *this was good* or *that was better* — only *this is what the human did.* If the human is clumsy, the robot learns the clumsiness. There is nothing in imitation that can ever make the robot **better than its teacher**, because "better" is a judgment about outcomes, and imitation never sees an outcome — only actions to copy.

The second limit is subtler and it is the fatal one: imitation learning suffers **covariate shift** *(a mismatch between the situations the model was trained on and the situations it actually meets when it runs — the inputs "shift" away from the training distribution)*. The CS234 lecture states the root cause exactly: "**Supervised learning assumes iid. (s, a) pairs and ignores temporal structure.**" *(iid = independent and identically distributed — the assumption that each training example is drawn fresh from the same fixed pool, with no example influencing the next.)* In ordinary supervised learning that assumption is fine. In a robot it is false and dangerous, because **the robot's own actions decide what it sees next.** During training the situations come from the *human's* trajectory; when the policy runs, the situations come from the *policy's own* trajectory — and the two drift apart. The lecture puts the mismatch in one line: in supervised learning the data is the same at train and test, but in a sequential decision problem the **training states are drawn from the expert's path and the test states from the policy's own path**, and those are not the same distribution.

### Why the drift compounds — the quadratic blow-up

Here is why that drift is fatal rather than merely annoying. Suppose the trained policy makes a mistake with some small probability at each step. Because each action changes the next situation, a single mistake nudges the robot into a state slightly *off* the human's demonstrated path — a state it has therefore seen **less** training data for — where it is **more** likely to err again, nudging it further off, into even less familiar territory. Errors don't stay independent; they **snowball**. The lecture makes the cost precise: for ordinary independent errors the total grows in proportion to the number of steps `T`, but for imitation's compounding errors the expected total grows like **`T²`** — the number of steps *squared*. Double the length of the task and you roughly *quadruple* the trouble. *(Deep dive #1 met "compounding error" as the reason to commit to action **chunks**; here it appears at its root — as the reason imitation, by itself, cannot hold a long-horizon task together, and the deeper reason the field needs a different kind of learning entirely. Same phenomenon, opposite use: #1 used it to pick a representation; this page uses it to motivate leaving imitation behind.)*

### The classic patch, and why it isn't enough

The standard fix is **DAgger** *(Dataset Aggregation — a 2011 method by Ross and colleagues)*, and its idea is exactly targeted at covariate shift: let the trained policy drive, and wherever it goes, **ask the human expert what they would have done there**, then add those new (situation, expert-action) pairs to the training set and retrain. Over rounds, the policy is trained on *its own* distribution of situations, so the train/test mismatch closes. The lecture's own summary: DAgger "get[s] more labels of the expert action along the path taken by the policy," yielding a policy "with good performance under its induced state distribution."

But read what DAgger *needs*, and the ceiling reappears one level up. It requires a **human expert available on tap**, ready to label the correct action for every weird state the robot wanders into, for as long as training lasts. That is expensive, and — the deeper point — it still only ever teaches the robot to **match the expert**. DAgger fixes the *drift*; it does not lift the *cap*. To exceed the human, and to learn without a human narrating every step, the robot needs a fundamentally different signal: not "what would the expert do here?" but "**did what I just did actually work?**" That signal is a **reward**, and learning from it is reinforcement learning. Everything from here is what happens when you try to run that loop inside a real body.

*(A note that will matter in Part 5: even the very idea of "the reward" hides a trap the lecture flags — if you try to *infer* the reward from watching an expert, the answer is not unique. Given a set of expert demonstrations, "there are an infinite set of R" — many different reward functions make the same behaviour look optimal. So you cannot cleanly read the goal off the demonstrations either. Hold that; it is why the reward wall in Part 2 is so stubborn.)*

## Part 2 — the three walls: why learning in a real body is brutal

So the robot should learn from reward, from its own trial and error — the sensorimotor loop the card is really about. Why did the field spend years on imitation instead? Because running RL's act-and-improve loop (the [AP4](04_ap4-deep-dive-the-engine.md) engine) inside a **physical** body runs into three walls that simply don't exist for a model learning in a data centre. Name them, because the rest of the page is the field climbing over them.

**Wall 1 — the sample wall.** RL is **sample-inefficient** — AP4's structural crack — meaning it needs an enormous number of trials to learn, because it learns only from the thin signal of reward, and often has to stumble into success by chance before it can reinforce it. In a simulator or a game that is survivable: you run millions of episodes overnight, in parallel, for free. In a real body **every trial is one real-time, physical, breakable robot-second.** A million episodes that cost nothing in silico cost months of wall-clock time and a trail of worn gears on real hardware. The very inefficiency AP4 could wave away as "just run it more" becomes the hardest physical constraint in the field.

**Wall 2 — the reward wall.** In a game the reward is handed to you (the score). In the real world **nothing tells the robot it succeeded.** Who says the mug is "in the sink," the shirt "folded," the espresso "made"? There is no number waiting in reality. And Part 1's trap means you cannot simply read the reward off human demonstrations — infinitely many rewards fit the same demonstrated behaviour. Worse, most real tasks have **sparse** reward (you only "succeed" at the very end, after a long correct sequence), which is exactly the case RL is worst at. The Eureka paper states the standing pain plainly: "reward functions are known to be **notoriously difficult to design in practice**," and a survey it cites found "**92% of polled reinforcement learning researchers and practitioners report manual trial-and-error reward design and 89% indicate that their designed rewards are sub-optimal.**" Even the *humans* can't reliably write the reward.

**Wall 3 — the reset-and-safety wall.** A game resets for free — die and you respawn at the start. A robot does not. Every RL episode needs the world put back to a **start state**: the blocks re-stacked, the laundry un-folded, the cup refilled — usually by a human, which throttles the whole loop to human speed. And exploration — RL's habit of trying random things to see what pays — is *fine* in a game and **dangerous** on a real arm that can smash itself or its surroundings. Levine's offline-RL tutorial names the two costs together as the core obstacle: "the fact that reinforcement learning algorithms provide a **fundamentally online learning paradigm** is also one of the biggest obstacles to their widespread adoption," because that online interaction "is impractical, either because data collection is **expensive** (e.g., in robotics …) **and dangerous**."

Put the three walls together and you see why deep dive #1's pyramid was built on imitation, not RL: imitation needs *no* reward (just copy), *no* exploration (so it's safe), and it wrings a lot out of few samples. It is the *safe, cheap, bounded* option — and its price is exactly the ceiling of Part 1. The frontier of this page is the set of tricks that let a robot pay a *little* of imitation's safety back in exchange for breaking through its ceiling — learning from its own experience without needing millions of dangerous, un-resettable, reward-less trials.

## Part 3 — scaling the walls: how real-world RL is actually made to work

The breakthroughs of 2024–2026 are not a single new algorithm; they are a **stack of moves**, each one knocking down one of the three walls. Read them as a set, because the shipping systems combine all of them.

### Move 1 — don't start from scratch: RL as *fine-tuning* on top of imitation

The sample wall (Wall 1) is deadly only if the robot must learn *from nothing*, exploring blindly until it stumbles on success. So don't. **Start from the imitation-trained policy** — which already mostly works — and use RL only to **polish** it: to push it past the demonstrator and teach it to recover from its own mistakes. Because the starting policy is already good, RL needs far fewer real trials to improve it; you are climbing a hill from halfway up, not searching a wilderness. This is the single most important idea in practical robot RL, and it inverts the framing: imitation is no longer the *whole* skill (Part 1's ceiling), it is the **launch pad** for learning that then goes higher.

The clearest 2024 demonstration is **HIL-SERL** *(Human-In-the-Loop Sample-Efficient Robotic RL — Luo, Xu, Wu, and Levine, 2024)*. It seeds an RL policy with a few demonstrations, then trains it in the real world while a human **intervenes** — taking over for a moment when the robot is about to fail, exactly where the policy is weakest. Those human corrections stream into the learner alongside the robot's own attempts, so the policy learns precise, dexterous tasks in the real world in a couple of hours rather than the months a from-scratch run would need. *(As of July 2026; attributed.)* The corrections are DAgger's "expert on tap" idea (Part 1) — but now feeding a **reward-driven** learner that can *surpass* the demonstrator, not merely a copier that can only match it.

Physical Intelligence's **π\*0.6** (November 2025) scales this into a shipping recipe it calls **RECAP** — a method built on **advantage conditioning** *(training the policy while telling it how much better or worse than average each attempted action turned out — its "advantage" — so it learns to favour the above-average actions and avoid the below-average ones, extracting a learning signal from failures as well as successes)*. RECAP stacks three sources of experience: initial human demonstrations, real-time expert **corrections** when the robot errs, and **self-improvement from the robot's own autonomous trials** — the same launch-pad-then-climb shape as HIL-SERL, generalized to a fleet-scale generalist model. Physical Intelligence's reported headline: learning from its own experience this way roughly **doubles throughput and cuts failures more than half** on its hardest tasks, and lets a robot run tasks like making espresso drinks for hours at a stretch. *(All π\*0.6 / RECAP figures are a November-2025 company report, dated and attributed — not verbatim, and the exact expansion of "RECAP" varies across write-ups; the load-bearing fact is the mechanism: advantage-conditioned RL fine-tuning on the robot's own experience, on top of imitation.)*

### Move 2 — mine the logs you already have: offline RL

The reset-and-safety wall (Wall 3) is about *new* interaction being expensive and dangerous. So sidestep it: learn from **experience already collected**, with no new interaction at all. This is **offline RL** *(reinforcement learning that trains a policy purely from a fixed, previously-collected dataset — the robot logs, the teleoperation data, past attempts — without ever acting in the world during training)*. Levine's tutorial frames its promise precisely — offline RL algorithms "utilize previously collected data, without additional online data collection," turning "large datasets into powerful decision making engines." Because deep dive #1's pyramid *already* pools huge cross-robot logs, offline RL tries to squeeze *improvement* (not just imitation) out of them: unlike behavioral cloning, which only copies, offline RL can stitch together the *good* parts of many mediocre trajectories into a policy better than any single one it saw. Its own hard problem is that a policy trained on fixed data can fool itself about actions the data never tried (the tutorial's central "distributional shift" caution) — but as a way to break Wall 3, it is the safe, no-new-interaction route, and it pairs naturally with a little online fine-tuning (Move 1) at the end.

### Move 3 — import the reward from a foundation model

The reward wall (Wall 2) is the one with the most surprising 2025 answer: if neither the world nor the human can reliably supply the reward, get it from a **web-trained foundation model.** Two flavours.

- **An LLM *writes* the reward.** The **Eureka** system (2023) hands a coding language model the environment's source code and a plain-language task description and has it **write the reward function as code**, then improves that code over rounds by looking at how well the resulting policy trained ("evolutionary search" plus "reward reflection"). The result: Eureka's machine-written rewards "**outperform expert human-engineered rewards**," beating human experts on "**83% of the tasks**," and even cracked tasks humans couldn't hand-design a reward for, like a simulated hand spinning a pen. The reward, in other words, is now authored by a model trained on the web.
- **A VLM *judges* the reward.** For tasks in the messy real world, where there is no clean source code, a **vision-language model watches the camera and scores progress** — acting as a learned success detector. The 2025 **VLAC** model (a vision-language-action-**critic**, built on a web-pretrained VLM) outputs a dense "how much closer did that get me to done" signal with no task-specific reward engineering; used as the reward for real-world RL, it reportedly lifted success rates from "about 30% to about 90% within 200 real-world interaction episodes." *(VLAC figures: a 2025 web result, dated and attributed.)*

Both flavours break Wall 2 — but look at *how*. The body's missing reward is supplied by a model that learned what "success" and "progress" mean **from the web**, not from the body. This is the quiet theme building toward the verdict: each wall the body climbs, it climbs by standing on a web-trained brain. Move 1's demonstrations and corrections come from humans; Move 3's rewards come from foundation models. The body's "own experience" is real — but it is *scored* by borrowed meaning.

## Part 4 — the one place the body authors something new: world-models learned from action

Moves 1–3 let the robot **improve its behaviour** past imitation. That is real, and it is more than deep dive #1's "hands" — but a sharper eye notices that it mostly makes the *controller* better; the *concepts* are still web-supplied (the reward that defines success came from a VLM). There is, however, one mechanism on this frontier where the body genuinely authors a new piece of the mind — a piece the web never contained. It is the **world-model learned from the robot's own action**, and it deserves its own part because it is the strongest form of the card's strong bet that the machine actually implements.

### The idea: learn your own physics, then practise in imagination

Recall a world model (from [AP5](05_ap5-deep-dive-learning-without-a-teacher.md)) is a learned predictor of what happens next. The move here is to learn it **from the robot's own interaction**, so that it captures the consequences of *this body's* actions — and then to improve the policy by **imagining** rollouts inside that self-learned model, cheaply, instead of paying for every trial on the real robot. This is the 2018 **World Models** idea (Ha and Schmidhuber), and its two load-bearing lines say it exactly. First, the architecture: they **divide "the agent into a large world model and a small controller model"** — a big network learns to predict the world, a tiny one learns to act using those predictions. Second, the payoff that matters here: "**We can even train our agent entirely inside of its own hallucinated dream generated by its world model, and transfer this policy back into the actual environment.**" The controller learns to act by practising against the world model's *predictions* — a "dream" — and the learned skill still works when plugged back into reality.

The crucial detail for *this* page is what the world model is a function of. Its predictor is trained to model the next situation **given the action the agent took** — formally, to predict the next latent state from the current one *and the action*. That "and the action" is everything: the model is not a passive video-predictor of the world at large, it is a model of **how this body's own actions change what it will sense.** It can only be learned by *acting*. This is grounding the web genuinely could not give — the web has no data on the consequences of *your* gripper's motions on *your* camera. Here, at last, the body authors a representation of its own.

### The proof it works on a real body from scratch: Dreamer / DayDreamer

The line from that 2018 idea to a shipping mechanism runs through **Dreamer** (and its 2023 version, **DreamerV3**, "Mastering Diverse Domains through World Models," in the corpus) — the recipe that made "learn a world model, then improve by imagining rollouts in it" work robustly across many domains. The result that matters for the card is **DayDreamer** (Wu, Escontrela, Hafner, Goldberg, and Abbeel, 2022): it put Dreamer on **physical robots with no simulator and no demonstrations**, and a real quadruped **learned to roll off its back, stand up, and walk — from scratch, without resets, in about one hour** of real-world experience, then adapted within ten minutes to shoves and to being flipped over. On robot arms it learned to pick and place from camera images and sparse rewards, approaching teleoperation quality; on a wheeled robot it learned to navigate from vision alone. *(DayDreamer: a 2022 result, dated and attributed.)*

Read what that demolishes. There was no human demonstrator to copy (so no Part 1 ceiling), no simulator to blur (so no sim-to-real tax), and no hand-written reward for the gait (a simple sparse reward sufficed). The walking skill and the model of the body's physics were both **grown by the body's own acting, in an hour.** This is the card's "a mind grown through a body" in its purest, most literal working form — and it is exactly the mechanism deep dive #1's verdict said the shipping VLAs *lacked*.

### Where it sits relative to the rest

Two placements to keep this honest. First, DayDreamer's world model is **generative** — it predicts future *observations* (or their compressed codes) and the controller dreams against them; this is the pixel/latent-predicting lineage that also runs through [AP5's](../20-the-approaches/05_ap5-world-models-jepa.md) territory. Its non-generative cousin is **V-JEPA 2-AC** (owned by the [AP5 deep dive](05_ap5-deep-dive-learning-without-a-teacher.md)): a *latent* action-conditioned world model that plans a real robot zero-shot. Both learn "what my action will do" — one by drawing the future, one by predicting an abstract summary of it — and the split between them is the very fork the (not-yet-written) AP5 second dive will open; here we only need that **both are world-models the body helps author.** Second, and sobering: DayDreamer's hour-from-scratch triumph is on a **narrow** task (a gait, a pick) with a **given** reward signal. It authors a model of *low-level physics*, not a concept like "hammer" or "clean." That boundary — the body authors the *dynamics*, the web still authors the *semantics* — is the hinge the verdict turns on.

## Part 5 — the loop closes: foundation models supply the reward, the curriculum, and even the experience

Stand back and watch the 2025–26 systems assemble Parts 3 and 4 into a single loop — and watch *who supplies each piece of it*, because that is the whole argument of the page.

The dream is a **flywheel**: the robot acts in the world, collects its own experience, improves, and redeploys — each turn better than the last, with less and less human input. π\*0.6's autonomous multi-hour runs (making espresso, folding unfamiliar laundry, assembling factory boxes, then learning from how those attempts went) are that flywheel turning in public for the first time. It directly attacks deep dive #1's **Stuck #4** — the upside-down pyramid whose skill-teaching bottom layer was the least scalable — by growing that bottom layer from the robot's *own* acting instead of a human's puppeteering. If the flywheel spins, the body's contribution to the pyramid stops being a hand-collected trickle and starts to scale.

But look at what powers the flywheel, because 2025–26's most striking move is that **the pieces the body cannot cheaply supply are increasingly supplied by web-trained foundation models:**

- **The reward is supplied by a foundation model** — Eureka writes it, VLAC judges it (Part 3, Move 3). The web brain decides what counts as success.
- **The curriculum can be supplied by a foundation model** — an LLM proposes the sequence of sub-tasks to practise, ordering them easy-to-hard (Eureka pairs its rewards with exactly this "curriculum learning" to reach skills like pen-spinning). The web brain decides what to practise next. *(This is [AP9's](../20-the-approaches/09_ap9-open-endedness.md) automatic-curriculum idea, now driven by a pretrained model — pointed at, not re-opened.)*
- **The experience itself can be supplied by a foundation model** — the newest turn is **generative world models used as simulators.** Rather than collect risky real trials, a large video-generative model *dreams up* the practice: **NVIDIA Cosmos** bills its world foundation models as a "physics-grounded world simulator" in which a policy can "predict multiple approaches, evaluate outcomes in a closed loop … without real-world risk," and **Genie Envisioner** (2025) offers an "action-conditioned neural simulator" that produces rollouts for closed-loop policy training. This is DayDreamer's "practise in a dream" (Part 4) scaled up — except the dream is now generated by a model trained on **web-scale video**, not learned from *this* robot's own hour of acting.

There is the tension, sharpened to a point. The frontier that was supposed to make the body the **source** of the mind works — but it works by wrapping the body in a scaffold of web-trained models that supply the reward, the curriculum, and increasingly the practice itself. The body's genuine self-authored contribution — the dynamics model of Part 4, the beyond-imitation skill of Part 3 — is real and growing. But the *meaning* around it, the definition of what is worth doing and what counts as doing it well, still flows in from the web, exactly as deep dive #1 found. The frontier has not removed the web-trained knower; it has given it new jobs.

## Part 6 — the 2026 synthesis and the honest verdict

Line the frontier up against deep dive #1's verdict and ask whether it has been overturned, as of July 2026.

- **Learning from experience is real and shipping — the body now improves *past* its teacher.** RL fine-tuning on the robot's own trials (HIL-SERL, π\*0.6/RECAP) demonstrably lifts a policy above the demonstrations that seeded it and teaches it to recover from its own mistakes — breaking Part 1's ceiling. This is strictly more than deep dive #1's "the body drives a web-built controller": the body now *edits* that controller from its own experience.
- **The body authors a model of its own physics — the strongest form of the card's bet the machine implements.** World-models learned from action (World Models → Dreamer → DayDreamer) let a real robot grow both a skill and a predictive model of its own body's consequences from scratch, in an hour, with no human demonstration. That is grounding the web could not supply.
- **But the concepts, the goals, and the practice still come from the web.** The reward that says "success" is written or judged by a foundation model (Eureka, VLAC); the curriculum is proposed by one; the simulated experience is dreamed by one (Cosmos, Genie). The high-level *meaning* — what a mug is, what "clear the table" means, what is worth doing — remains web-authored, exactly as before. The body authors the **how** (the skill, the dynamics); the web still authors the **what** (the concepts, the goals).

Now the synthesis, which is the point of the page. Deep dive #1 asked *how is the robot brain built?* and answered: a web-trained knower with fast hands — **the body trains the controller, not the concepts.** This page asked the follow-on the card really cares about — *can the body's own experience make it the source of the mind?* — and the honest 2026 answer is **partly, and along one axis only:**

- **Along the axis of *skill and physics*, yes, and increasingly so.** The body genuinely authors its low-level control and a model of its own dynamics, and RL-from-experience is scaling that authorship. On this axis the card is being vindicated at the level of mechanism: practice *is* reshaping part of the mind.
- **Along the axis of *concepts and goals*, no, and if anything the web's role has grown.** The semantic core is still web-pretrained, and 2025's cleverest advances *deepen* the dependence by handing the web-brain the reward, the curriculum, and the simulator. On this axis, deep dive #1's rival-bet verdict still stands.

So the two deep dives together give AP10 a precise, two-part reading of its own big question. *Can a mind be grown through a body?* Deep dive #1: as built, the body is the mind's **hands**, not its source. This page: the frontier is turning the hands into **self-teaching hands with a model of their own physics** — a real move toward "source" — but the body has not yet been shown to author a single **concept**; the meaning still comes from the web, and the newest tools import even more of it. *As of July 2026, the body has learned to teach itself to **move**; it has not learned to teach itself to **mean** — and whether the first ever becomes the second is exactly the card's open question, now being decided in the lab rather than the seminar.* The frontier has moved the line. It has not yet crossed it.

## Putting the frontier together

Hold the whole picture in one view.

1. **The ceiling (Part 1).** Imitation can only *match* the demonstrator and it **drifts** — **covariate shift** (the policy's own actions carry it off the demonstrated distribution) makes errors compound like **`T²`**. **DAgger** fixes the drift but needs an expert on tap and still can't exceed the human. To go past the teacher and recover from mistakes you need to learn from **outcomes** — reward — i.e. RL.
2. **The three walls (Part 2).** RL in a real body is blocked by the **sample wall** (every trial is a physical robot-second; AP4's inefficiency made physical), the **reward wall** (nothing in reality says "success," and rewards are "notoriously difficult to design"), and the **reset-and-safety wall** (online interaction is "expensive … and dangerous"). Imitation dominated because it dodges all three — at the cost of the ceiling.
3. **Scaling the walls (Part 3).** **RL as fine-tuning on top of imitation** (HIL-SERL corrections, π\*0.6/RECAP advantage conditioning) beats the sample wall by climbing from halfway up; **offline RL** mines existing logs, beating the reset wall; and the reward wall is beaten by **importing the reward from a foundation model** — an LLM *writes* it (Eureka beats human experts on 83% of tasks) or a VLM *judges* it (VLAC, ~30%→~90%).
4. **The body authors its own physics (Part 4).** A **world-model learned from action** — the model of "what my own actions do to what I sense" — lets a robot practise in its **"hallucinated dream"** (World Models) and improve for free; **DayDreamer** grew a real quadruped's walk **from scratch, no sim, no demos, in an hour.** This is the body authoring a representation — but of *dynamics*, not *semantics*.
5. **The loop, and who powers it (Part 5).** The self-improvement **flywheel** (π\*0.6's multi-hour autonomous runs) attacks deep dive #1's upside-down pyramid by growing the skill layer from the robot's own acting — but its **reward, curriculum, and even simulated experience** are increasingly supplied by web-trained foundation models (Cosmos, Genie). The body's self-authorship is real; the meaning around it is still borrowed.
6. **The verdict (Part 6).** Along the axis of **skill and physics**, the body now teaches itself — a real move toward *source*. Along the axis of **concepts and goals**, the web still authors everything, more than ever. The body learned to teach itself to **move**, not to **mean**.

## Judging the machinery: where the learning-from-experience frontier is stuck

The [AP10 card](../20-the-approaches/10_ap10-embodiment.md) judged the **bet** (four cracks). The [first deep dive](12_ap10-deep-dive-inside-the-robot-brain.md) judged the **machine** (four taxes about the fast-slow VLA). This page judges the **frontier** — the attempt to make the body a source through its own experience — and it has its **own** four cracks, distinct from both. Be fair first: this is real, shipping, fast-moving progress, and the DayDreamer-style world-model result is a genuine existence proof that a body can grow a skill from nothing. Nothing here says the frontier is failing. What it shows is that the frontier, read honestly, has authored the body's *skill* and *physics* but not its *meaning* — and that its cleverest new tools deepen, rather than remove, the dependence on a web-trained mind.

### Stuck #1 — the reward comes from the web brain, so the "own experience" loop is still web-supervised

This is the deepest of the four, because it turns the frontier's own success against its headline claim. Learning from experience needs a **reward**, and Part 2 showed the body can't cheaply supply one. The 2025 fix — an LLM *writing* the reward (Eureka) or a VLM *judging* it (VLAC) — works, but it means the signal that defines "success" for the body's own trials is **authored by a web-trained model.** So the loop that was supposed to let the body learn *for itself* is, at its core, a loop **supervised by borrowed meaning**: the body acts, but a web-brain grades. This sharpens deep dive #1's Stuck #2 (grounding enters above the body) into a moving fact — the grounding now enters through the *reward* as well as the perception. **[Established that the leading real-world-RL rewards are foundation-model-supplied; Contested whether a body could ever supply its own open-ended reward.]**

### Stuck #2 — real-world RL is sample-bounded, so it *fine-tunes* a web-built mind, it doesn't grow one from scratch

The sample wall (Wall 1) does not go away; it gets *managed* by starting from imitation and only fine-tuning (Part 3, Move 1). That is a real win — but read what it concedes: because you cannot afford to explore from nothing in a real body (except on the narrowest tasks, à la DayDreamer), the practical recipe is **web-pretrain and imitation-seed first, then let experience polish.** The body's learning is therefore mostly a *finishing school* on top of a mind the web already built, not a from-scratch education. AP4's sample-inefficiency, which the digital bets can buy their way around with more compute, is a **hard physical wall** here — and it caps how much of the mind the body can plausibly author. **[Established — sample cost forces the imitation-first, RL-finetune recipe on all but toy tasks.]**

### Stuck #3 — world-models-from-action reshape the *dynamics*, not the *concepts*

Part 4 is the frontier's best card — the body genuinely authors a world-model. But be precise about *what* it authors: a model of **low-level sensorimotor consequences** ("if I send this torque, my camera will see this next"), learned on narrow tasks with a given reward. That is real new grounding — of **physics**. It is not a model of **meaning**: DayDreamer's quadruped learned its own gait, not the concept "walk to the kitchen because that's where dishes go." The body reshapes its *dynamics representation*; the *semantic* representation still comes from the web. So even the strongest form of "the body authors the mind" that the machine implements authors only half the mind — and it is the half the card cared *least* about proving (that bodies have physics was never in doubt; that bodies grow *concepts* was the strong claim). **[Established that current world-models-from-action capture dynamics; Contested whether the same mechanism can ever reach concepts.]**

### Stuck #4 — self-improvement needs an autonomy the physical world resists, so the flywheel spins slowly

The flywheel (Part 5) is only as fast as its slowest un-automatable step, and the physical world is full of them: **resets** (someone re-stacks the blocks), **safety** (you can't let it explore freely), and — deep dive #1's Stuck #3 returning — **no cheap checker** for whether a continuous grasp actually improved, so every evaluation is slow, noisy real-world counting. Contrast the digital self-improving loops: [AP9's](../20-the-approaches/09_ap9-open-endedness.md) open-ended archive and [AP2's](../20-the-approaches/02_ap2-reasoning-and-test-time-compute.md) verify-and-scale spin millions of times for pennies because they have free resets, free safety, and a cheap checker. The robot flywheel has none of those for free, so it turns in hours-per-lesson, not milliseconds. The frontier's fixes (foundation-model rewards and simulators) are largely *attempts to buy back the cheap checker and the free reset* the digital loops get by default — which is exactly why they lean so hard on web-trained models. **[Established — the physical loop lacks the free reset, safety, and checker the digital self-improvement loops assume.]**

### The big question under all four

The card asked: *is a physical body the **source** of a general mind, or an **output device** a web-trained model can drive?* Deep dive #1 answered "output device, as built." This page asked the sharper follow-on: **can learning from the body's own experience turn the output device into a source?** As of 2026 the answer is *partly, and along one axis*: the body now authors its own **skill and physics** (past mere "hands"), but the **concepts, goals, and even the practice** are supplied by web-trained foundation models — and the frontier's best tools *increase* that supply. So the body has learned to teach itself to **move**; it has not been shown to teach itself to **mean.** If world-models-from-action ever climb from dynamics to concepts, and if the reward can ever come from the body rather than a web-brain, the card is vindicated at the level of mechanism. If instead the flywheel keeps spinning on foundation-model reward, curriculum, and simulation, then "learning from experience" turns out to make the body a **self-improving pair of hands guided by a web-trained teacher** — a stronger output device, not a source. *As of July 2026, the frontier has moved the line toward the body and not yet crossed it — and, unlike the card's philosophy, it is now a question being settled by which of these mechanisms scales.* **[Contested — the key open question, now located in the loop: can the reward and the concepts ever come from the body, or must they always be imported from the web?]**

## ⚠️ Honesty box

- **The frontier is real and shipping; "the body grows the mind" is still only half-true.** RL-from-experience (HIL-SERL, π\*0.6) and world-models-from-action (DayDreamer) are genuine, published, working results — the body really does now author its skill and its own physics. But the concepts, goals, and increasingly the practice come from web-trained models, so "a mind grown through a body" remains, at the level of concepts, unimplemented. This page opens the frontier deep dive #1 named; it does not claim the strong bet is won. **[Established that the mechanisms work; Contested what they prove about the bet.]**
- **π\*0.6 / RECAP numbers and even the acronym are a company snapshot.** The "doubles throughput / cuts failures 2×" figures and the multi-hour autonomy demos are Physical Intelligence's own November-2025 report, not independent results, and different write-ups expand "RECAP" differently. The durable fact is the *mechanism* — advantage-conditioned RL fine-tuning on the robot's own experience, seeded by imitation and corrections — not any specific number. **[Contested / dated 2026-07.]**
- **DayDreamer's "learn in an hour" is a narrow-task, given-reward result.** It is a real and important existence proof that a body can grow a skill and a dynamics model from scratch — but on a gait or a pick, with a simple reward provided. Do not read it as "robots can learn anything from scratch in an hour"; read it as "the world-model-from-action mechanism is real, on narrow tasks." **[Established narrow; do not over-generalize.]**
- **Foundation-model rewards can be gamed, and that's a live risk.** An LLM-written or VLM-judged reward is only as good as the model's grasp of the task, and RL is a champion at **exploiting** a flawed reward (reward hacking — [AP4's](04_ap4-deep-dive-the-engine.md) crack). A VLM that scores "looks folded" can be satisfied by a robot that *looks* folded without folding. Treat "the foundation model supplies the reward" as an advance that imports the foundation model's blind spots, not a solved problem. **[Established risk.]**
- **The generative-simulator turn overlaps another card and is young.** Using web-trained video models (Cosmos, Genie) to *dream up* robot experience is genuinely new in 2025–26 and genuinely powerful — but it inherits every sim-to-real worry (deep dive #1's Tax 3) plus the generative model's own hallucinations, and the deeper "generative vs. non-generative world model" fight belongs to a future [AP5](../20-the-approaches/05_ap5-world-models-jepa.md) dive. Here it is one more way the web supplies what the body cannot. **[Contested / dated 2026-07.]**

## How to use this (if you want to direct AI work)

- **When a robot is claimed to "learn from experience," ask what seeds it and what scores it.** (Parts 1, 3.) Almost no real system learns from scratch; it is imitation-seeded and RL-fine-tuned. Ask: *how many demonstrations seeded it, and where does the reward come from?* If the reward is a foundation model (LLM-written or VLM-judged), the "own experience" is being graded by borrowed meaning — fine, but know it, and watch for the model's blind spots being exploited.
- **Separate the two axes the frontier lives on.** (Part 6.) *Skill and physics* — does the body improve its own control and learn its own dynamics? (Genuinely happening — world-models-from-action, RL fine-tuning.) *Concepts and goals* — does the body author any new meaning? (Not shown; still web-supplied.) A pitch that a robot "understands its world through experience" almost always means the first and quietly implies the second. Force the split.
- **Respect the three walls before believing any timeline.** (Part 2.) Sample cost, the missing reward, and the reset-and-safety burden are physical, not incidental. A plan that assumes "we'll just run more RL on the real robot" is under-costing all three. Ask how each wall is being paid down — imitation-seeding, offline RL, foundation-model reward, generative simulation — and whether those escape routes fit the task.
- **Prize world-models-from-action, but check what they model.** (Part 4.) A learned model of the body's own consequences is the real thing — the mechanism where the body authors part of the mind. But check whether it captures *dynamics* (physics of this body — common) or *semantics* (concepts — not yet). The former is a control win; the latter would be the card vindicated. Don't confuse them.
- **Treat the generative-simulator flywheel as sim-to-real with extra steps.** (Part 5.) Dreaming up practice with Cosmos/Genie is powerful, but the dream is a web-trained model's guess at physics — it inherits every sim-to-real gap plus generative hallucination. A policy that only ever practised in a dream is not a working robot until it survives reality.
- **What you delegate vs what you keep.** *Delegate:* the RL fine-tuning, the offline-RL pipeline, the world-model training, the teleoperation and correction rigs, wiring in a foundation-model reward. *Keep for yourself:* asking whether the body in your system is authoring any new *concept* (the frontier) or only a better *skill and dynamics* (the shelf); noticing when "learning from experience" is really "learning from a web-brain's reward"; and holding the honest line that as of 2026 the body has learned to teach itself to *move*, not to *mean*.
