## In one minute

The card said "copy the cortical algorithm" and then, honestly, "nobody has it." That is only half true — and the missing half changes the whole picture.

1. **There is a concrete candidate for what the cortex computes: predictive coding.** The card's "a column predicts its next input" is not just a slogan — it has decades of math under it. The theory: the cortex is a **hierarchy** that constantly sends **predictions downward** and passes only its **errors upward.** The brain is not a camera taking in the world; it is a **guessing machine** that ships only its surprises.

2. **The grand version says the brain minimises "surprise" — the free-energy principle.** One quantity — how unexpected your senses are — that the brain lowers two ways: change your **mind** to fit the world (perceive), or change the **world** to fit your mind (act). It is beautiful and unifying. It may also be too general to test.

3. **The one concrete gap the card never names: how brains learn.** Every capable AI learns by **backpropagation** — and the brain almost certainly **can't run it** (a synapse would need to be read backwards; the update would need information from far-away neurons it has no way to see). The brain learns **locally.** And the striking 2020s result is that **predictive coding can approximate backprop using only those local updates** — the tightest bridge yet between the one working mind and the one working AI method.

4. **The same story in silicon is efficient — and stranded.** Brain-style **spiking chips** copy the cortex's local, event-driven, only-fire-when-you-must style and are **tens to a hundred times** more energy-efficient — but they are **incompatible with the backprop training** that produces capable models, so the efficiency has nothing capable to run.

Put it together and the card's Stuck #1 flips. The problem is not that the cortical algorithm is a total mystery. It is that **every time we make a brain mechanism precise enough to test, it lands on "a more efficient, more biological *implementation* of the gradient learning AI already does"** — not a secret new algorithm. Which reframes the whole bet: the brain's edge, so far, looks like better **implementation** (efficiency, continuous local learning, spikes), not a better **algorithm** of intelligence. This page is about why that is the real question — and why it is uncomfortable for AP6.

## Part 1 — predictive coding: the actual mechanism under "the predictive brain"

The card's Piece 3 said "with every movement, a column predicts what its next input will be." True — and left as a picture. The concrete theory behind it is **predictive coding**, first worked out for the visual cortex by Rao and Ballard (1999) and reviewed by Huang and Rao (2011). It answers a precise question: *if the cortex is constantly predicting, how is that wired, and what actually travels along the wires?*

The answer inverts the naive picture of perception. You might imagine the eye sends a picture up into the brain, layer after layer, building it into understanding. Predictive coding says the opposite is at least as important: the **higher** levels send their best **guess** of the world **downward**, and the **lower** levels send back only the part the guess got **wrong.** In the Rao–Ballard account that Huang and Rao review, the **feedback** connections — the ones running *down* the hierarchy, from higher and more abstract areas toward the senses — carry **predictions** of the lower level's activity ("given what I think is out there, here is what your next input should look like"), while the **feedforward** connections running *up* carry only the **residual error** *(residual error = the leftover — the difference between what was predicted and what actually arrived)*. So the signal climbing your cortex is not the world; it is the world **minus what you already expected** — your surprises only. Huang and Rao give the reason this is a smart design, and here their exact words:

> "By transmitting only the unpredicted portions of an incoming sensory signal, predictive coding allows the nervous system to reduce redundancy and make full use of the limited dynamic range of neurons."
> *(Huang & Rao, 2011)*

*(Two glosses: **redundancy** = repeated, predictable information that carries nothing new; **dynamic range** = how much a neuron can say with its limited firing — a scarce budget.)* Read what this buys. Most of what hits your senses is predictable — the wall stays where it was, the hum keeps humming — and sending all of it up would waste the brain's tiny bandwidth on old news. By sending **only the unpredicted part**, the cortex spends its scarce neural signalling on what actually changed. Perception becomes a running conversation: the top guesses, the bottom corrects, the guess updates, and what you *see* is the guess that finally stops being corrected.

Three things fall out of this, and each matters for the bet.

- **The brain is an inference machine, not a camera.** What reaches awareness is a **generative model's best guess** *(a generative model = an internal machine that can produce, or "imagine," what its inputs should be — the same idea as [AP5's world model](../20-the-approaches/05_ap5-world-models-jepa.md), here built in cortex)*, continuously checked against the error. This is Hawkins' "the model is the knowledge" (card's Piece 1), but as a specific, wired mechanism rather than a metaphor.
- **It maps onto real anatomy.** Predictive coding predicts a *division of labour* between cortical layers — some carrying predictions down, others carrying errors up — and the cortex's famous six-layer structure is broadly consistent with exactly that. It is a **testable** claim about tissue, not just a story.
- **It is the same shape as modern self-supervised AI.** "Predict your own next input, learn from the error" is precisely the [next-word machine](../10-how-ai-works-today/01_guessing-the-next-word.md)'s trick and the [JEPA](05_ap5-deep-dive-learning-without-a-teacher.md)'s trick — which is the first hint of this page's ending: the brain's headline operation and deep learning's headline operation are *the same idea*, found twice.

Hold that. Now the grander theory that tries to swallow predictive coding whole.

## Part 2 — the free-energy principle: one quantity the brain is said to minimise

Predictive coding explains perception. Karl Friston's **free-energy principle** makes a far larger claim: that predictive coding is just one face of a *single principle* governing every self-organising thing — a cell, a brain, an organism. The principle is that any such system acts to **minimise its "free energy,"** which is — in the one line that makes it graspable — a mathematical **bound on "surprise"**:

> "[free energy] represents a bound on the surprise inherent in any exchange with the environment, under expectations encoded by its state or configuration. A system can minimise free-energy by changing its configuration to change the way it samples the environment, or to change its expectations. These changes correspond to action and perception."
> *(Karl Friston, on free energy and the brain)*

*(Glosses: **surprise** here is technical — how *unexpected* a sensory input is, given your internal model; a bound = a ceiling you can push down even when you can't compute surprise directly. "Sufficient statistics / configuration" = the settings of your internal model.)* Read the crucial middle sentence, because it is the whole idea: there are **two** ways to make your senses less surprising.

- **Change your expectations to fit the world** — update your internal model until it predicts what's arriving. That is **perception**, and mechanically it *is* predictive coding (Part 1). Friston is explicit that the two coincide: optimising the brain's internal model to reduce free energy, he argues, works out mathematically to be the very predict-and-correct process of Part 1 — perception and predictive coding are one thing.
- **Change the world to fit your expectations** — *act* on the environment so your senses match what you predicted. Predict "my hand is on the cup," and if it isn't, **move the hand** until it is. This is **active inference** *(acting to make your own predictions come true, so as to reduce surprise)*.

So one quantity — surprise — unifies **perception, action, and learning**: perceiving lowers it by fixing your beliefs, acting lowers it by fixing the world, and learning lowers it by slowly improving the model that does both. This is the most ambitious form of the card's "the brain is a prediction machine": *everything the brain does is surprise-reduction.* And it is a close cousin of [AP5's energy-based view](05_ap5-deep-dive-learning-without-a-teacher.md) — LeCun's "energy" and Friston's "free energy" are two dialects of the same idea, *a single number the system drives down* — which is why AP5 and AP6 are cards that keep pointing at each other.

The power is obvious: one principle for all of cognition. The danger is the mirror image, and we return to it in the judgement: a principle general enough to cover a cell, a brain, and a society, and to be satisfied by *either* changing your mind *or* changing the world, is in danger of being **compatible with any observation at all** — which would make it a beautiful lens that predicts nothing specific. Hold that tension.

## Part 3 — the one concrete gap: why the cortex can't run backpropagation

Now the mechanism the card never mentions, and the sharpest brain-versus-AI fact in the whole subject. Everything modern AI can do — [scale](../20-the-approaches/01_ap1-scale-and-foundation-models.md), [reasoning](../20-the-approaches/02_ap2-reasoning-and-test-time-compute.md), [agents](../20-the-approaches/03_ap3-agents-and-cognitive-architectures.md) — it learned by **one** algorithm: **backpropagation** *(after each mistake, compute exactly how much every single weight contributed to the error, by passing the error signal backward through the network layer by layer, then nudge each weight to shrink it — the "backward pass" the [AP1 deep dive](07_ap1-deep-dive-anatomy-of-a-scaling-law.md) named; it is the engine under all of deep learning)*. The brain, as far as neuroscience can tell, **does not do this** — and cannot, for two concrete reasons.

- **The weight-transport problem.** To pass an error *backward* across a connection, backprop must use the **exact same weight** that was used to send the signal *forward* — as if every synapse could be read in reverse. But a real synapse is a **one-way** valve: it passes signals from neuron A to neuron B, and neuron B has **no access** to the strength of that synapse to reuse it for a return trip. There is no known biological way to "transport" the forward weights into a matching backward path. Backprop quietly assumes a symmetric, two-way wire that the brain does not have.
- **Non-locality.** Backprop's correction to one synapse depends on error signals computed **far away** — across many downstream layers, gathered and routed back precisely. Biological plasticity is **local**: a synapse changes based only on the activity of the **two neurons it connects** — the classic **Hebbian** rule, *"cells that fire together, wire together,"* sharpened by timing into **STDP** *(spike-timing-dependent plasticity — a synapse strengthens if neuron A fires just *before* neuron B, and weakens if just after; the order, in milliseconds, sets the change)*. A synapse has no wire to a distant layer's error and no way to know it.

These two are why, in the plain words of the field:

> "Backprop is often criticised for lacking biological plausibility."
> *(Millidge, Tschantz & Buckley, "Predictive Coding Approximates Backprop along Arbitrary Computation Graphs," 2020)*

This is the concrete content of the card's vague "today's AI is built nothing like a brain." It is not built like a brain in one **specific, nameable** way: it learns by a **global, weight-symmetric** rule the brain has no machinery to run. For decades this looked like a dead end for both sides — either the brain does something *other* than gradient learning (and we don't know what), or it approximates gradients by some trick nobody could find.

Then came the result that reframed everything, and it runs straight through Part 1. It turns out that **predictive coding** — the cortex's own perception mechanism — can, when you also let it adjust its weights, **compute the same gradients backprop does, using only local updates.** Millidge and colleagues proved the general version:

> "Predictive coding converges asymptotically (and in practice rapidly) to exact backprop gradients on arbitrary computation graphs using only local learning rules."
> *(Millidge et al., 2020)*

> "Our models perform equivalently to backprop on challenging machine learning benchmarks, while utilising only local and (mostly) Hebbian plasticity."
> *(Millidge et al., 2020)*

Sit with what this says. The prediction-error signals that predictive coding already computes for **perception** turn out to carry, when the network settles, **the same information backprop's backward pass carries for learning** — and they do it with nothing but **local, Hebbian** changes, no weight transport, no long-range error routing. So the brain *could* be doing (a close approximation of) the very gradient learning that powers modern AI — with only the local, biologically-real machinery it actually has. The one working mind and the one working AI method may be **the same algorithm in two implementations.** (Other bridges exist too — **feedback alignment**, which replaces the exact backward weights with *random* fixed ones and still learns, showing the brain doesn't need perfect weight symmetry — but these "underperform slightly" and predictive coding is the tightest match.)

This is the most important — and, for AP6, most double-edged — result on the page. It is a triumph: it removes the mystery of how a brain could learn as well as a deep net. But read the second quote again: *"perform equivalently to backprop."* Equivalently. The bridge shows the brain's learning can **match** backprop, not **beat** it. If the cortical learning rule, made precise, is "backprop, implemented locally," then it is a more **plausible** and more **efficient** way to run the same gradient descent — not a **superior algorithm of intelligence**. Hold that; it becomes the engine's sharpest crack.

## Part 4 — the same story in silicon: spiking neurons and neuromorphic chips

The card name-dropped Loihi and NorthPole as "Leg 3 silicon." Here is what those chips actually do differently — and it is the same local, event-driven story as Parts 1–3, cast in hardware.

Start with the biological fact today's neural networks ignore. A real neuron does not output a smooth number. It stays quiet, and then it **fires** — a **spike**:

> "The action potential is a short voltage pulse of 1-2 ms duration and an amplitude of about 100 mV."
> *(Gerstner et al., Neuronal Dynamics)*

Three things about that spike matter for machines. It is **all-or-none** (a neuron spikes or it doesn't — no in-between). Information lives partly in **when** it fires (a **temporal code**, not just a rate). And crucially, most neurons are **silent most of the time** — real brains are **sparse** and **event-driven**: a neuron costs energy only in the instant it spikes. A standard AI chip is the opposite: it computes *everything, everywhere, every step*, whether or not anything changed.

A **spiking neural network (SNN)** on a **neuromorphic chip** *(neuromorphic = shaped like the nervous system)* copies the biology: units that compute only when they spike, and — the key hardware move — **memory placed right next to compute.** That last point attacks the single biggest energy cost in a normal computer, the **von Neumann bottleneck** *(the constant shuttling of data back and forth between a separate memory and a separate processor, which on today's chips burns far more energy than the arithmetic itself)*. Put the memory in the compute and the shuttling stops. The payoff, reported for 2026 hardware (Intel's **Loihi 2/3**, IBM's **NorthPole**), is large: on the order of **25× to 100× better energy efficiency** than a GPU — *(as of 2026-07; vendor and benchmark figures, so treat the exact multiple as a dated, workload-specific snapshot)*. IBM's NorthPole, for instance, distributes memory across 256 on-chip cores and reports ~25× the energy efficiency of a leading GPU on image recognition.

And the learning rule on these chips is **local** — STDP, the same spike-timing rule from Part 3 — because that is what the hardware can physically do. Which is exactly why neuromorphic computing, for all its efficiency, is **stranded**, and the honesty is now standard in the field:

- **The efficiency is real but narrow.** The 100× holds only for workloads that are naturally **sparse and event-driven** — continuous audio, sensor streams, always-on detection. Dense workloads — standard image classification, and above all the **transformer** language models that are the whole frontier — do not fit the shape, and the advantage largely evaporates.
- **You can't train them the way capable AI is trained.** SNNs' discrete spikes and local plasticity are, in the field's words, *mathematically incompatible with standard backpropagation.* So the training methods that produce every frontier model do not run on them, and the bridge of Part 3 — predictive coding approximating backprop — is still a research result, not a production toolchain.
- **They are too small for the frontier.** A 2026 NorthPole tops out around a few **hundred million** parameters; a frontier language model has **billions to hundreds of billions.** The efficient chip has no room for the capable model.

The consensus verdict, dated 2026: *"the neuromorphic deployment gap is primarily a software and ecosystem problem, not a hardware problem."* The brain-shaped substrate exists, and it is astonishingly efficient — but the algorithms, the training, and the tooling to fill it with real capability are not there. The hardware arrived a decade before the software that would make it matter. **[Established / dated — the efficiency figures and the training gap are both real; the exact numbers are 2026 snapshots.]**

## Part 5 — the 2026 frontier and the honest synthesis

Line the four mechanisms up against the evidence, as of July 2026.

- **Predictive coding** is one of the leading theories of cortical *computation*, and the bridge to backprop (Part 3) has made it central to machine-learning theory too. But its **direct** neural evidence is still debated: the cortex's anatomy is *consistent* with predictive coding, yet also consistent with rival accounts, and the crisp experiments that would rule the others out have not settled it. The theory is ahead of the data.
- **The free-energy principle** is enormously influential and genuinely unifies perception, action, and learning under one quantity — but it is widely criticised as so general that it is hard to **falsify** *(falsify = find an experiment whose result could prove it wrong — the mark of a scientific claim)*. A principle satisfiable by changing your mind *or* the world can be read into almost any behaviour.
- **Bio-plausible learning** (predictive coding, feedback alignment, target propagation) has matched backprop on real benchmarks with only local rules — a profound *plausibility* result — but as of 2026 it has **not surpassed** backprop, and on the largest models it lags. The brain's learning, where we can make it precise, keeps coming out **equivalent** to gradient descent, not superior to it.
- **Neuromorphic hardware** is real, shipping, and 25–100× more efficient on the right workloads — and stranded by the training-and-software gap (Part 4). No production, general-purpose neuromorphic system runs a frontier-scale model.

Now the synthesis, which is the point of the whole page. Look at what happened *every* time we turned one of the card's brain **slogans** into a precise, testable **mechanism**:

- "The brain is a prediction machine" → **predictive coding**, which is the same *predict-and-correct* loop as self-supervised deep learning.
- "The brain learns continuously and differently" → a **local learning rule** that, made exact, **approximates backpropagation.**
- "Brain-style chips will be more efficient" → **spiking hardware** that is more efficient at *running* networks, but must be trained by — or bridged back to — the same gradients.

In each case the concrete mechanism did **not** reveal a secret, alien algorithm of intelligence. It revealed **a more efficient, more local, more biologically-real *implementation* of computations deep learning already performs.** That is the honest 2026 state of brain-based AI at the level of mechanism — and it reframes the card's Stuck #1 completely. The problem is no longer "the cortical algorithm is an unknown mystery." The problem is that **our best concrete candidates for it keep turning out to be *the same algorithm AI already uses*, in a better body** — which raises a sharper, and for the bet more dangerous, question than "what is the algorithm?": *is the brain's difference a new **capability**, or only a better **implementation**?*

## Putting the machine together

Hold the whole picture in one view.

1. **Predictive coding (Part 1).** The cortex is a hierarchy: **predictions flow down**, **only errors flow up** (*"feedback connections … carry predictions … bottom-up connections convey the residual errors"*). Perception is a generative model's guess, corrected by surprise — an inference machine, not a camera. Same shape as self-supervised AI.
2. **The free-energy principle (Part 2).** One quantity — a **bound on surprise** — that the brain lowers by **perception** (change your mind) or **action** (change the world — *active inference*). Unifies everything; a cousin of [AP5's energy](05_ap5-deep-dive-learning-without-a-teacher.md). Possibly too general to test.
3. **The learning-rule gap (Part 3).** The brain can't run **backpropagation** — the **weight-transport problem** (synapses are one-way) and **non-locality** (biology learns locally, via **Hebbian/STDP**). But **predictive coding "converges … to exact backprop gradients … using only local learning rules"** (Millidge 2020) — the tightest bridge yet. It shows the brain can **match** backprop, not beat it.
4. **Spiking / neuromorphic (Part 4).** Real neurons fire sparse, timed **spikes**; neuromorphic chips copy this and kill the **von Neumann bottleneck** → 25–100× efficiency on sparse workloads — but are **incompatible with backprop training**, too small for frontier models, and **stranded** by the software gap.
5. **The synthesis (Part 5).** Every brain slogan, made precise, becomes **a more efficient implementation of what deep learning already does** — so the real question flips from *"what is the algorithm?"* to *"capability, or just implementation?"*

## Judging the machinery: where the concrete brain-mechanisms are stuck

The [AP6 card](../20-the-approaches/06_ap6-brain-based.md) judged the **bet** (four cracks: we don't know the algorithm · which biology to copy · the brain-ignorers are winning · it may be too slow). This page judges the **mechanisms we actually have** — a sharper question: *given predictive coding, the free-energy principle, the learning-rule bridge, and neuromorphic silicon, what do the concrete pieces tell us the card's slogans could not?* Be fair first: these are real, deep, beautiful results — predictive coding is a genuine theory of cortex, and "the brain approximates backprop with local rules" is one of the most important bridges between neuroscience and AI in twenty years. Nothing here says the brain is understood or that AP6 is wrong. But the mechanisms have their **own** four cracks, and each sharpens a crack the card only stated.

### Stuck #1 — predictive coding is a beautiful theory the brain data can't yet confirm

The card's Stuck #1 was "we don't know the algorithm." The mechanism sharpens it: we have a strong **candidate** — predictive coding — but the brain's anatomy **underdetermines** it. The six-layer cortex is *consistent* with predictions-down-errors-up, but it is also consistent with several rival theories, and the decisive experiment has not landed. So predictive coding is in the awkward middle: precise enough to build with, not yet confirmed as *what the cortex actually does.* This is worse than it sounds for the bet, because AP6's plan is to **copy** the cortical algorithm — and you cannot be sure you are copying the brain's mechanism when the brain's data is equally happy with three mechanisms. We have a candidate; we do not yet have a verdict. **[Contested — predictive coding is a leading but unconfirmed theory of cortical computation.]**

### Stuck #2 — the free-energy principle explains everything and therefore predicts little

The grand unification is also the grand weakness. A principle that (a) covers cells, brains, and societies and (b) can be satisfied by *either* changing your beliefs *or* changing the world is in constant danger of being **unfalsifiable** — compatible with any behaviour you observe, and so unable to tell an engineer what specifically to build. That does not make it wrong; deep organising ideas often start this way. But it means the free-energy principle, as of 2026, functions more as a **lens** than as a **blueprint** — it reframes what the brain is doing without handing you a machine to build. For a bet whose entire promise is "read the blueprint and rebuild it," a principle that resists being turned into a spec is a real limit. **[Contested — the FEP's generality is celebrated and criticised in equal measure.]**

### Stuck #3 — the brain's learning, made precise, matches backprop rather than beating it

This is the deepest and most double-edged finding. The card's Stuck #3 was "the brain-ignoring approaches are winning." The mechanism explains *why that might be inevitable*: when we finally made the brain's local learning precise, it turned out to **approximate the very gradient descent the winners already use** (Part 3) — "perform equivalently to backprop." So the hoped-for outcome of brain-based AI — a *superior* learning algorithm hidden in the cortex — has, at the one place we can check it rigorously, come back as **"the same algorithm, implemented more plausibly."** That is a stunning scientific result and a sobering strategic one: if copying the brain's learning gets you backprop-with-local-rules, then the brain-ignorers weren't missing a better algorithm — they had the algorithm all along and merely ran it on the wrong hardware. The brain's advantage, if any, is pushed out of the *algorithm* and into the *implementation* (efficiency, locality, continuous learning). **[Likely — the equivalence is proven on benchmarks; whether the brain has *additional* algorithmic tricks beyond it is open.]**

### Stuck #4 — the efficient substrate is stranded from the capable software

The card's Stuck #4 was "right but too slow." The mechanism locates the slowness precisely: it is not that neuromorphic hardware is weak — it is **10–100× more efficient** — it is that the hardware and the capability live on **opposite sides of a training gap.** Capable models are made by backprop at scale; efficient chips run local, spiking rules incompatible with backprop and too small for frontier models. So the two halves of the brain-based dream — its *efficiency* (spiking silicon) and its *capability* (would need frontier-scale learning) — cannot yet be in the same system. Until the bridge of Part 3 becomes a production toolchain, the brain-shaped substrate is a superbly efficient engine with no capable fuel, and the frontier keeps racing ahead on hardware that wastes energy but *works today.* **[Established / dated — the efficiency and the deployment gap are both real as of 2026.]**

### The big question under all four

The card asked: *is the brain the necessary blueprint for intelligence, or just one way evolution happened to build it?* The mechanisms answer a sharper, and more uncomfortable, version: **wherever we have turned a brain principle into a precise, testable mechanism, it has come back as a more efficient, more local, more biologically-real *implementation* of computations deep learning already performs — not a new algorithm of intelligence.** Predictive coding is self-supervised prediction; the cortical learning rule is local backprop; neuromorphic silicon is a cheaper way to run the same networks. So the deepest 2026 verdict on brain-based AI is not "we haven't found the secret recipe yet" — it is that **our best concrete findings keep pointing at the brain's edge being *implementation* (efficiency, continuous local learning, spikes) rather than *algorithm.*** That relocates the entire bet. If what blocks AGI is **capability** — some missing algorithmic idea — then brain-based AI, so far, has not supplied it, and the card's Stuck #3 stands. But if what blocks AGI is **implementation** — if the frontier's real walls are energy, continuous learning, and the inability to learn without forgetting (the card's first attribute) — then the brain's efficient, local, always-learning style is not a footnote but the missing piece, and AP6 wins after all — not with a secret algorithm, but with a radically better body for the algorithm we already have. *As of July 2026, the concrete question under AP6 is no longer "what is the cortical algorithm?" but "is the brain's difference a new capability or a better implementation?" — and every precise result so far points, uncomfortably for the classic bet, toward implementation.* **[Contested — the key open question, now located precisely: capability vs implementation.]**

## ⚠️ Honesty box

- **The mechanisms are real; "the brain is understood" is not.** Predictive coding, the free-energy principle, and the backprop bridge are genuine, published, substantial results — but each is a *leading theory*, not settled fact (Stuck #1/#2). This page opens the concrete machinery the card lacked; it does not claim the cortex is solved. **[Contested.]**
- **"Predictive coding = backprop with local rules" cuts both ways.** It is a triumph (it shows a brain *could* learn as well as a deep net) and a warning (the brain's learning, made precise, *matches* rather than *beats* gradient descent). Do not report the bridge as "the brain has a better algorithm" — the evidence says "the same algorithm, more plausibly implemented." **[Likely.]**
- **Neuromorphic efficiency is real and narrow.** The 25–100× figures are genuine for sparse, event-driven workloads and largely vanish for dense ones (transformers included). And no frontier-scale model runs on neuromorphic hardware today. Treat "brain chips are 100× more efficient" as true-but-workload-specific, and dated. **[Established / dated.]**
- **The free-energy principle is a lens, not yet a blueprint.** Its unifying reach is real; so is the worry that it is too general to falsify or to build from. Useful for *thinking* about brains; not yet a spec you can hand to an engineer. **[Contested.]**
- **"Sensory prediction error" ≠ "reward prediction error."** This page's predictive coding is *cortical, sensory* — guessing your next input. The dopamine signal of [AP4](04_ap4-deep-dive-the-engine.md) is *subcortical, reward* — guessing your next reward. Same phrase, different systems; don't merge them. **[Established.]**
- **Names and numbers age; the mechanisms don't.** Loihi 2/3, NorthPole, the 25–100× figures, the specific bio-plausible-learning papers — all 2020–2026 snapshots. The lasting parts are **predictive coding** (predict down, error up), the **free-energy** framing, the **weight-transport / locality** reason the brain can't run backprop, the **predictive-coding-approximates-backprop** bridge, **spiking / neuromorphic** efficiency and its **training gap**, and the **implementation-vs-algorithm** question. **[Established core, dated specifics.]**

## How to use this (if you want to direct AI work)

- **When someone says "brain-like," ask: computation or learning rule?** These are different claims (Part 1 vs Part 3). "It uses predictive coding" is about *what it computes*; "it learns with local Hebbian rules" is about *how it learns.* Most "brain-inspired" hype blurs the two — and most real substance is in the second, which the card never even named.
- **Read "the brain approximates backprop" correctly.** (Stuck #3.) It means the brain's learning is *plausible and efficient*, not *superior.* If a pitch claims a brain-based method is a better *algorithm*, ask what it does that gradient descent can't — the honest 2026 answer is usually "nothing yet, but more cheaply / locally / continuously."
- **Separate capability from implementation.** (The big question.) The sharp question about any brain-based effort is which one it targets. Efficiency, continuous learning, and no-forgetting are *implementation* wins that could matter enormously; a new *algorithm of intelligence* is a different and so-far-unmet claim. Know which you're being sold.
- **Treat neuromorphic efficiency as workload-specific.** (Stuck #4.) The 100× is real for sparse, event-driven data and gone for dense transformer workloads. Before betting on brain chips, ask what runs on them *and how it was trained* — the training gap, not the hardware, is the wall.
- **Take the free-energy principle as a lens, not a plan.** (Stuck #2.) It is a genuinely useful way to *think* about perception-and-action as one thing; it is not yet a blueprint you can build from. Use it to frame problems, not to promise systems.
- **What you delegate vs what you keep.** *Delegate:* simulating predictive-coding networks, building the neuromorphic chip, running the bio-plausible training. *Keep for yourself:* the judgement of whether a brain mechanism is a new capability or a cheaper implementation, the refusal to read "matches backprop" as "beats backprop," and the habit of asking whether the wall in front of AGI is an algorithm problem (where AP6 has, so far, little) or an efficiency-and-continual-learning problem (where it may have everything).

## Connections

- **Keep only three things:** ① The card's "predictive brain" has a real mechanism — **predictive coding**: the cortex sends **predictions down** and passes **only errors up**, so perception is a guess corrected by surprise (an inference machine, not a camera); its grand form, the **free-energy principle**, says the brain minimises one quantity — **surprise** — by perceiving (change your mind) or acting (change the world). ② The concrete brain-vs-AI gap the card skipped is the **learning rule**: the cortex **can't run backpropagation** (synapses are one-way — the *weight-transport problem* — and biology learns *locally*, Hebbian/STDP), yet **predictive coding can approximate backprop using only local updates** — so the brain can **match**, not beat, the algorithm AI already uses. ③ The same local, spiking style in silicon (**neuromorphic** chips) is **25–100× more efficient** but **stranded** — incompatible with backprop training and too small for frontier models — so the honest 2026 verdict is that the brain's edge looks like a better **implementation** (efficiency, local continuous learning), not a new **algorithm** of intelligence.
