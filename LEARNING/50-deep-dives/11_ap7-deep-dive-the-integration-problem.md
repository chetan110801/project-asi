# AP7 · Deep dive — the join, opened up: the six ways to wire a learner to a reasoner, and why you can keep the logic or learn the seam but not both

*This is a **deep dive** past the [AP7 card](../20-the-approaches/07_ap7-neurosymbolic-and-hybrid-ai.md). The card made the "join the learner and the reasoner" bet — keep today's flexible neural network, bolt on an exact symbol-machine, and get an AI that both learns and reasons — and it named the deepest problem (its Stuck #1): **the integration problem.** A neural network is smooth and a logic engine is jagged, so "there is no gradient through logic," and nobody knows how to join the two in general. The card stated that problem in one paragraph and moved on. This page opens it. It shows the **actual machinery** people build when they try to weld a learner to a reasoner — first the real map of the field (six named ways to wire them, from loosest to tightest), then the loose join that genuinely works (the mechanism inside AlphaGeometry, not the medal), then the tight join that tries to truly fuse the two (differentiable, or "soft," logic), and the three heavy taxes it pays. Nobody has both at once — and the dream, a truly exact reasoner you can train end-to-end, does not yet exist. Everything the card already said — the bet, what a symbol is, curve-fitting, systematicity, robust AI, the AlphaGeometry result, the Bitter Lesson, the four bet-cracks — is referenced, not repeated.*

## In one minute

The card said the two halves are made of opposite stuff — smooth vs jagged — so joining them is unsolved. True. But "unsolved" hides the real shape of the problem, which is what this page is about.

1. **"Neurosymbolic" is not one thing — it is a dial, from loose to tight.** There is a standard map (Kautz's six types) that lines up every hybrid by *how tightly* the neural and symbolic parts are wired. At the **loose** end, the two sit in separate boxes and pass messages. At the **tight** end, the logic lives *inside* the network. The whole argument of the page is that where you sit on this dial decides what you win and what you lose.

2. **The loose join works — because it never actually joins.** The systems that succeed (AlphaGeometry; any model that calls a calculator or a proof-checker) keep the neural net and the logic engine in *separate boxes* with a clean, human-designed pipe between them. That sidesteps "no gradient through logic" entirely — you don't need a gradient to cross the seam, because nothing learns across it. The price: the symbolic engine and the seam are **hand-built, per domain**, and none of the join is *learned*.

3. **The tight join is the real attempt to solve the integration problem — and it pays three taxes.** To get one gradient to flow through both halves, you make the logic **soft** ("fuzzy") — truth becomes a dial from 0 to 1 instead of a hard true/false. Now learning can reach into the rules. But: **(a)** soft logic gives up **exactness** — the very works-in-every-case guarantee that made symbols worth having; **(b)** the soft rules are **hard to actually train** (their gradients misbehave); and **(c)** they **don't scale** — the exact-inference cost blows up on deep chains of reasoning.

4. **So the integration problem is a fork, not a wall.** Keep the logic and you can't learn the seam (it's hand-built, and the Bitter Lesson keeps absorbing it). Learn the seam and you lose the logic (it goes soft and doesn't scale). **Nobody has both.** The dream — a genuinely exact reasoner trained end-to-end, Kautz's "Type 6" — *"there is no NeSy approach to-date that can truly meet the standard"* (the field's own survey, 2024).

5. **And the fresh 2026 evidence cuts hard against the hand-built seam.** The neurosymbolic pipeline (AlphaProof + AlphaGeometry 2) won Math-Olympiad **silver** in 2024. One year later the **gold** went to a *plain, end-to-end* model reasoning in ordinary language — no bolted-on proof-search pipeline. The hand-crafted join, winning one year, absorbed the next. That is the card's Bitter Lesson, playing out on the field's showcase problem.

Put it together and the card's Stuck #1 gets sharper and more troubling. It is not that "we haven't figured out the wiring yet." It is that the two things the bet most wants — **a learner that trains itself** and **a reasoner that is exact** — pull in opposite directions the moment you try to wire them into one trainable machine. This page is about that trade-off, exactly where it bites, and why it may be the deepest fact about the whole approach.

## One line of base, then we build

Two reminders, because the page turns on them — both are owned by other pages, so here they are *pointed at*, not re-taught.

- The [AP7 card](../20-the-approaches/07_ap7-neurosymbolic-and-hybrid-ai.md) stated the **integration problem** (its Stuck #1): a neural network is **continuous and differentiable** — its knobs are smooth dials you nudge by a hair, and learning works by measuring which tiny nudge lowers the error (the "gradient," *the slope that says which way is downhill*). A symbolic system is **discrete** — separate, all-or-nothing steps; a rule fires or it doesn't. You cannot smoothly nudge a hard rule, so **no gradient runs through logic**, and the net's way of learning does not reach into the symbolic part. This page does **not** re-argue that; it takes it as the starting wall and opens every real attempt to get over, around, or through it.
- The [AP8 deep dives](03_ap8-deep-dive-the-research-frontier.md) already opened one instance of the loose join — a **program search steered by a neural net** — and named its **compositionality wall** (a fixed neural map of programs can *blend* known ones but not *chain* arbitrary new steps). That wall is the [systematicity](../20-the-approaches/07_ap7-neurosymbolic-and-hybrid-ai.md) gap in its most modern dress. AP8 owns that story; this page points at it where it recurs and does not retell it.

One framing to carry, because it is the spine of everything below. Every hybrid you will ever see answers one question: **how tightly is the logic wired into the learning?** Keep that single dial in mind — *loose* (separate boxes, a pipe between) to *tight* (logic dissolved into the network) — and the whole range of "neurosymbolic" systems falls into order, and the trade-off this page is about becomes visible: **the tighter you wire, the more the logic learns — and the less it stays logic.**

## Part 1 — the real map: six ways to wire a learner to a reasoner

The card name-dropped "a well-known map of the field (by the researcher Henry Kautz) [that] lists six different ways to combine neural and symbolic parts." It used that only to make a point about vagueness and then dropped it. But that map is the single most useful thing in the whole subject, because it turns the mush of "neurosymbolic" into an ordered dial. Here it is, opened.

The field's standard survey adopts **Kautz's six types** *(a taxonomy = an ordered classification; Henry Kautz gave it in a 2020 keynote at the AAAI conference, the field's main meeting)*. Read them as a single dial from **loose** (the two parts barely touch) to **tight** (the logic lives inside the net):

- **Type 1 — Symbolic → Neuro → Symbolic.** Symbols go *in*, a neural net churns, symbols come *out*. This is just a normal deep-learning model whose input and output happen to be symbols — a language model is the clearest case (words in, words out). The survey includes it mostly to mark the zero-point: strictly, it is barely a hybrid at all, because there is no separate reasoner.
- **Type 2 — Symbolic[Neuro]:** a **symbolic** system in charge, calling a neural net as a **subroutine** *(a helper the main program calls to do one job, then takes back control)*. The classic example is **AlphaGo** *(DeepMind's champion board-game program that beat the world's best Go players)*: the boss is a symbolic search over the game tree (**Monte Carlo Tree Search** — *try many move-sequences at random, keep exploring the promising ones*), and it calls a neural net just to score how good a board looks. The survey's exact words: here "the symbolic and neural parts are only loosely-coupled" *(loosely-coupled = joined by a thin, clean interface, each able to work almost independently)*.
- **Type 3 — Neuro | Symbolic:** a **pipeline** where a neural part and a symbolic part do *different, complementary* jobs and pass results between them — not one calling the other as a servant, but two collaborators in a line. The textbook case: a neural net **perceives** (turns a messy image into clean symbols — "there is a red cube"), then a symbolic engine **reasons** over those symbols to answer a question. Also here: a language model that reads a task and writes out a little program or plan for a symbolic tool to run.
- **Type 4 — Neuro : Symbolic → Neuro:** symbolic knowledge is **compiled into** the network — used to *build* its structure or *generate* its training, so the finished net carries the rules baked in. The rules shape the learner up front; at run time there is no separate engine.
- **Type 5 — Neuro_Symbolic:** the logic becomes a **term in the loss** *(the loss = the single error-score a network is trained to push down; a "term" is one piece added to it)*. In the survey's words, this type "turns symbolic knowledge into additional soft-constraints in the loss function used to train DNNs" *(DNN = deep neural network; a "soft constraint" = a rule the training is pushed to obey, but can bend, rather than a hard wall)*. The net is nudged toward obeying the rules, and the knowledge ends up "compiled into the weights."
- **Type 6 — Neuro[Symbolic]:** the tight end of the dial — a **symbolic reasoning engine embedded *inside* a neural engine**, fully fused, so the network itself does genuine symbol-manipulation. This is the dream: a single machine that learns like a net and reasons like a logic engine, all the way down, trainable **end-to-end** *(end-to-end = the whole system trained as one piece, from raw input to final answer, by a single learning signal)*. Hold this one — its status is the key point of the page.

Do not memorise the six labels; memorise the **dial** they describe. Types 1–3 are the **loose** end: the neural and symbolic parts sit in **separate boxes**, connected by a clean interface, each doing what it is good at. Types 5–6 are the **tight** end: the logic is pushed *into* the learning machine — into its loss, or into its very architecture. And here is the whole reason the map matters: **as you slide from loose to tight, the "no gradient through logic" problem changes from something you *avoid* to something you *must solve*.** At the loose end you never need a gradient to cross the seam, so the integration problem doesn't arise — but nothing about the join is learned. At the tight end the whole point is to make one gradient flow through everything — which is exactly where the logic starts to melt. The next three parts walk that dial: the loose join that works, the tight join that tries, and the taxes the tight join pays.

## Part 2 — the loose join that works: neuro proposes, symbolic disposes

Start at the loose end, because that is where the wins are. The card's headline success — **[AlphaGeometry](../20-the-approaches/07_ap7-neurosymbolic-and-hybrid-ai.md)** — is a Type-2/Type-3 loose join, and the card gave you its *result* (it solved 25 of 30 Olympiad geometry problems, near a human gold medallist; the neural half "guesses the helpful line," the symbolic half "proves it"). Good — that is owned. What the card did **not** open is the *mechanism*: how, exactly, the two boxes are wired, and why that wiring quietly steps around the integration problem instead of solving it. That is the useful part, because the *shape* repeats everywhere.

Look inside. AlphaGeometry has two separate machines.

- **The symbolic deduction engine.** This is a classic logic engine, hand-built for geometry. It takes the facts of a problem (this point is on that line, these two angles are equal) and grinds forward with exact rules — a **deductive database** of geometry theorems, plus an **algebra** part for the numeric relations — working out *everything that necessarily follows*, over and over, until it either reaches the goal or runs out of new things to say. In DeepMind's words, it runs "the symbolic engine to deduce new statements about the diagram until the solution is found or new statements are exhausted." It is exact and it is tireless — but it is also **stuck inside the diagram it was given.** It can only combine the objects already on the page; it cannot have the *idea* to draw a brand-new helper line.
- **The neural language model.** This is the creative half. Its one job is to look at a stalled problem and **propose a new object** — "add this point," "draw this line" — the flash of insight a hard geometry proof needs. It was trained on a hundred million machine-made proofs to get a feel for which additions tend to help.

Now the wiring, which is the whole lesson. The two run in a **loop**, and the survey's own words describe it exactly: "If no solution is found, AlphaGeometry's language model adds one potentially useful construct, opening new paths of deduction for the symbolic engine. This loop continues until a solution is found." Read the division of labour: the exact engine goes as far as pure logic can; when it jams, the neural net *suggests one new thing*; the exact engine restarts with that suggestion and grinds again; repeat. It is the [AP2](../20-the-approaches/02_ap2-reasoning-and-test-time-compute.md) fast-vs-slow split — the fast guesser and the slow checker — built out of two physically separate machines.

Here is the part the card never says, and it is the key to the whole page. **Notice what does *not* happen at the seam.** The neural net's suggestion is handed to the logic engine as a plain, discrete fact — "point X exists." No gradient crosses that gap. The logic engine's grind never sends a smooth error-signal back into the net during a proof. The two boxes talk in **finished, discrete messages**, like two people passing notes, not like one nervous system. So the integration problem — "there's no gradient through logic" — **simply never comes up**, because the design never asks a gradient to travel through the logic. AlphaGeometry did not *solve* the join of smooth-to-jagged. It **avoided** it, by keeping the halves in separate boxes and letting a human design the note-passing.

This is the same shape as every loose win the card mentioned. A chatbot that calls a **calculator**, a model that calls a **theorem prover** *(a program that checks a proof step by step with certainty)* or a **code interpreter** *(a tool that runs small programs the model writes)* — all of them are this exact pattern: **the neural net proposes, a discrete tool disposes,** with a clean human-built pipe between them and no gradient crossing it. It works, it is reliable, and it is genuinely useful. But look at what it costs, because the cost is the whole reason the *tight* join exists at all:

- **The symbolic engine is hand-built, per domain.** AlphaGeometry's deduction engine knows *geometry* — its theorems, its algebra. It knows nothing about chemistry, or law, or planning a holiday. Each new domain needs a *new* hand-built engine and a *new* hand-designed seam. Nothing about how to join learner-to-reasoner is *learned*; it is re-engineered by people every time.
- **The seam is a human's design, not the system's.** *What* the net is trusted to guess, *what* gets checked, *how* the messages are shaped — a person decided all of it. The system cannot improve its own join, because the join is not part of what it learns.

So the loose join buys reliability by giving up the very thing the bet was most excited about: a mind that **learns its own way of reasoning**. It keeps the logic pristine (the engine is fully exact) at the cost of the seam being frozen and hand-made. Which raises the obvious question — *can't we make the join itself learnable?* That is the tight end of the dial, and Part 3 is what happens when you try.

## Part 3 — the tight join that integrates: make the logic soft so a gradient can flow

The tight join takes the integration problem head-on. Its reasoning is simple and bold: the reason no gradient runs through logic is that logic is **hard** — a rule is true or false, on or off, with nothing in between, and you cannot take the slope of an on/off switch. So **change the logic.** Make it **soft**: let a statement be *partly* true. Then the on/off switch becomes a smooth dial, the dial *has* a slope, and a gradient can finally flow straight through the reasoning — and the whole thing, net plus logic, can be trained as one machine, end-to-end.

This is **differentiable logic**, and the survey states its core move in one line worth reading slowly:

> "The core idea is to relax boolean first-order logic as soft fuzzy logic, which deals with reasoning that is approximate instead of fixed and exact."
> *(Wang, Yang & Wu, 2024)*

Unpack every heavy word, because the whole trade-off is hiding in them. **Boolean logic** *(named after the mathematician George Boole — the exact true/false "and / or / not" of a computer)* is the hard kind: every statement is `true` or `false`, full stop. **First-order logic** *(the standard logic of "for all" and "there exists" — it can say things like "every human is mortal," not just fixed facts)* is the expressive kind symbolic AI is built on. To **relax** it means to loosen the hard true/false into a range. And **fuzzy logic** is the result:

> "In fuzzy logic, variables have a truth degree that ranges in [0, 1]: zero and one meaning that the variable is false and true with certainty, respectively."
> *(Wang, Yang & Wu, 2024)*

So instead of "the cube is red" being just `true`, it can be `0.8` — mostly true. That one change is the master key, because a number that can slide between 0 and 1 has a *slope*, and a slope is a gradient, and a gradient is exactly what the neural net needs to learn. But there is a catch you can already feel: logic's power came from the operators — **and**, **or**, **not**, "for all," "there exists" — and those were defined on hard true/false. If truth is now a dial, you need **soft versions** of every operator. The tools of this field are exactly those soft operators. **Logic Tensor Networks (LTN)**, the survey says, "approximate non-differentiable logic connectives (i.e., ∧, ∨, ¬, ⇒), and quantifiers (i.e., ∃, ∀) with differentiable fuzzy logic operators" *(the symbols are "and, or, not, implies" and "there exists, for all"; "differentiable" = smooth enough to take a slope of)*. The soft "and" of two dials might be their product (0.8 and 0.9 → 0.72); the soft "for all" might be a smoothed minimum. The details vary; the move is always the same — **replace each hard switch with a smooth dial that has a usable slope.**

With soft operators in hand, there are two main ways to actually wire the logic into the learning.

- **Logic as a term in the loss (Type 5).** Write your background knowledge as logic rules — "a thing cannot be both a cat and a dog," "if X is north of Y then Y is south of X" — turn each into a soft, differentiable score of *how well the network's outputs obey it*, and **add that score to the training loss.** Now the net is trained not only to fit the data but to **obey the rules**, and both pressures reach it through one gradient. The survey's summary of the type: it "turns symbolic knowledge into additional soft-constraints in the loss function used to train DNNs." **Semantic loss** and **LTN** are the well-known members. The rules are not a separate engine at run time — they have been **melted into the weights** during training.
- **Logic as a probabilistic program with neural facts (DeepProbLog).** A different and more ambitious wiring keeps a real logic program, but lets **neural networks supply the facts** it reasons over. In the survey's exact description, **DeepProbLog** "adopts neural networks as predicates to compute the probabilities of probabilistic facts, and hence uses the inference mechanism of ProbLog … to compute the gradient of the desired loss" *(a **predicate** = a statement-with-blanks that is true or false once filled in, like "is-a-digit(?)"; **ProbLog** = a logic-programming language where facts carry probabilities)*. In plain terms: a neural net looks at a photo of a digit and outputs "this is a 7, with probability 0.9"; the logic program treats that 0.9 as the truth-degree of a fact and reasons with it; and because every step is now a smooth calculation of probabilities, a gradient can be pushed **all the way back** from the final logical answer into the neural net that read the pixels. Learner and reasoner, at last, sharing one gradient. This is the integration problem being genuinely *attacked*, not sidestepped.

This is real, and at small scale it is beautiful: you can train a system to add two hand-written numbers where a net learns to *read the digits* and a logic program does the *arithmetic*, and the whole thing learns end-to-end from nothing but "the answer was 12." The seam is no longer hand-frozen — the gradient flows across it, and the join itself is, in a real sense, *learned*.

So the tight join delivers exactly what the loose join could not: **one trainable machine, logic and learning fused, gradient flowing through both.** Which sounds like the answer to AP7's whole problem. It is not — and Part 4 is why. Making the logic soft enough to learn through quietly breaks the three things the logic was *for*.

## Part 4 — the three taxes of the tight join

The tight join buys integration, and it pays for it three times over. Each tax is a case of the same irony: *the very softening that let the gradient flow is the thing that damages the logic.*

**Tax 1 — you give up exactness, which was the whole point of symbols.** Go back to why anyone wanted symbols at all (the card's core argument): a rule like `x + y = y + x` is valuable because it is **exact** — it holds for *every* value, guaranteed, including cases you have never seen. That guarantee is what "works in almost all situations" meant. But fuzzy logic, by the survey's own words, "deals with reasoning that is approximate instead of fixed and exact." The moment truth is a dial at 0.8 instead of a hard `true`, the hard guarantee is gone: a soft rule is *usually mostly* obeyed, not *always exactly* obeyed. You went to symbols to escape the neural net's "great near the data, shaky far from it" — and soft logic quietly brings that same shakiness back into the reasoning itself. The exactness that made the reasoner worth bolting on is exactly what you sold to make it trainable.

**Tax 2 — the soft logic is genuinely hard to train, and the versions that train best stop being logic.** You might hope the softening is a free lunch — same logic, now with a slope. It is not. It turns out most of the "obvious" soft operators are **bad to learn through**: their slopes go flat or misbehave, so the gradient carries little useful signal. This was measured carefully. Van Krieken and colleagues studied the soft operators one by one and found:

> "We find that many of these operators, including some of the most well-known, are highly unsuitable in this setting."
> *(van Krieken et al., 2022)*

*(In this setting = when you try to learn by gradient descent through them.)* The trouble is concrete: a common soft "and" (multiply the two dials) **collapses toward zero** as you chain more terms, and its slope collapses with it — so a long rule gives almost no gradient to learn from *(this is the same "vanishing gradient" trouble that plagues deep networks, now coming from the logic itself)*. And the punchline is worse than "it's fiddly." When the same researchers searched for the operator combinations that actually learn *well*, they landed on this:

> "We find that, to achieve the largest performance improvement over a supervised baseline, we have to resort to non-standard combinations of logical operators which perform well in learning, but no longer satisfy the usual logical laws."
> *(van Krieken et al., 2022)*

Sit with that. The soft operators that are *good at learning* are the ones that have **stopped obeying the laws of logic.** So you face a dilemma with no good answer: pick operators that are faithful to logic and they barely train; pick operators that train well and they are no longer really logic. The softening did not give you "logic you can learn through." It gave you a slider between "logic" and "learnable," and the two ends pull apart.

**Tax 3 — the honest tight join doesn't scale.** The loss-term versions (Type 5) are cheap but weak — they only *nudge* a net toward rules, they don't *reason*. The genuinely reasoning tight join (DeepProbLog-style, where the logic program actually does inference over neural facts) hits a hard wall of **cost.** To push an exact gradient back through a chain of logical reasoning, the system must, in effect, account for *every way* the conclusion could have been reached — and the number of ways **explodes combinatorially** *(combinatorial explosion = the count of possibilities multiplies so fast with problem size that it becomes astronomical)*. In the neurosymbolic-programming literature (2025–26), exact inference in these systems is **#P-hard** *(a class of problems believed to require astronomically much computation as they grow — even harder, in a precise sense, than the famous "NP-hard" problems)*, and the internal bookkeeping can grow **exponentially** with the number of neural facts. In plain terms: the toy that adds two digits end-to-end is genuinely charming; scale it toward a real problem with many facts and deep reasoning, and the exact version becomes **intractable** — it simply cannot be run. The field's response has been to give up exactness *again* — to **approximate** the inference so it scales — which loops right back to Tax 1: to make the honest reasoner tractable, you soften it, and softening it costs the guarantee that was its reason to exist. *(As of July 2026; the exact-vs-approximate scaling wall is an active research front, not a solved problem.)*

Line the three up and the pattern is unmistakable. Each tax is the *same coin*: the softening that let learning reach the logic is what strips the logic of its worth — its certainty (Tax 1), its lawfulness (Tax 2), and its tractable depth (Tax 3). The tight join solved "no gradient through logic," and in solving it, mostly dissolved the logic.

## Part 5 — the 2026 frontier and the honest synthesis

Line the two ends of the dial up against the current evidence, as of July 2026.

- **The loose join is where the wins are — and they are formal-domain wins.** In the checkable world of competition maths the loose join is spectacular. **AlphaGeometry 2** now solves **42 of 50** (84%) of the last 25 years of Olympiad geometry problems, past the average gold medallist *(as of early 2025)*. Its sister **AlphaProof** writes proofs in **Lean 4** *(a formal proof language a computer can check line by line)*, so every answer is machine-certified, not guessed; together they reached **silver** at the 2024 International Mathematical Olympiad (4 of 6 problems). These are genuine, dated, peer-reviewed results — and every one of them lives in a **formal, checkable** domain with a **hand-built** symbolic engine and a **hand-designed** seam. The loose join works exactly where the card's Stuck #3 said it would (tidy, verifiable worlds) and, as Part 2 showed, it works by *avoiding* the integration problem, not solving it.
- **The tight join remains a lab result.** Differentiable logic, LTN, DeepProbLog and their kin are real, active, and illuminating — but as of 2026 they run on small, controlled problems. None of them powers a frontier-scale system, for the three taxes of Part 4. The genuinely fused machine — logic and learning as one trainable substrate — is not deployed anywhere.
- **And the dream, Type 6, does not exist.** This is the flat verdict of the field's own survey, and it is worth quoting exactly. A Type-6 system, the tight end of the dial, is described as "fully-integrated systems that directly embed a symbolic reasoning engine inside a neural engine," and Kautz "believes [it] has the greatest potential to combine the strengths of logic-based and neural-based AI." Then the survey's judgement:

> "From Kautz's viewpoint, it seems that there is no NeSy approach to-date that can truly meet the standard of Type 6."
> *(Wang, Yang & Wu, 2024)*

The single architecture that would actually fulfil AP7's bet — a machine that learns like a net and reasons *exactly* like a logic engine, fused and trainable end-to-end — is, by the field's own reckoning, **not yet built.**

Now the fact that reframes everything, fresh off the 2026 web, and it is about the *loose* join being challenged from the other side. The neurosymbolic pipeline (AlphaProof + AlphaGeometry 2, with its hand-built Lean engine and its careful seam) won Olympiad **silver** in 2024. **One year later, the 2025 Olympiad gold went to a *plain, end-to-end* model** — an advanced general reasoning model that read the problems in ordinary language and produced full proofs directly, with **no bolted-on symbolic proof-search pipeline** *(as of July 2026; reported by the model's developers and graded against the official 2025 problems)*. Read what that means against the card's Bitter Lesson (Stuck #2): the hand-crafted symbolic scaffolding that was **necessary** to reach silver in 2024 was **absorbed** by a stronger, plainer learner one year later. The exact seam people engineered by hand did not become the road to the top; it became the thing a bigger model made unnecessary. This is not a knock-down argument — the general model still had to be trained to reason, and the deep question of whether it *internalised* symbol-like reasoning or merely *imitated* it is exactly [AP7's open crux](../20-the-approaches/07_ap7-neurosymbolic-and-hybrid-ai.md). But as a dated data-point on the field's own showcase problem, it points hard in the Bitter Lesson's direction.

Now the synthesis, which is the point of the whole page. Look at what happened at *both* ends of the dial when we opened the actual machinery:

- **The loose join** (Types 1–3) works — by keeping the halves in separate boxes and letting a **human** hand-build the seam and the symbolic engine, per domain, with **no gradient** and **nothing learned** about the join itself. It keeps the logic exact and pays with a frozen, hand-made seam that scale keeps absorbing.
- **The tight join** (Types 5–6) genuinely fuses learner and reasoner into one trainable machine — by making the logic **soft**, which strips it of the **exactness**, the **lawfulness**, and the **tractable depth** that were the reasons to want a reasoner at all. It learns the seam and pays with logic that is no longer quite logic, and doesn't scale.

So the integration problem the card stated in one paragraph is really a **fork with a cruel trade-off.** You can have a join that keeps the logic *or* a join that learns the seam. **Not both.** The exactness that makes symbols worth having and the smoothness that makes learning possible are, at the level of mechanism, **at war** — and every real system is a particular truce between them, buying one by surrendering the other. That is why, thirty-five years into the neurosymbolic project, there is no general, learned, exact join: not because nobody has been clever enough, but because the two properties the bet most wants may be genuinely in tension. *As of July 2026, the sharpest question under AP7 is no longer "how do we join them?" but "is there any join that is exact **and** learnable end-to-end — or is that combination the thing we can't have?" — and every mechanism on the dial, so far, answers by giving up one to get the other.*

## Putting the machine together

Hold the whole picture in one view.

1. **The dial (Part 1).** Every hybrid lines up on one axis — **Kautz's six types**, from *loose* (Types 1–3: separate boxes, a clean pipe between) to *tight* (Types 5–6: logic pushed into the loss or the architecture). Where you sit decides whether "no gradient through logic" is something you **avoid** or something you must **solve**.
2. **The loose join that works (Part 2).** **AlphaGeometry**'s mechanism: an exact **deduction engine** grinds until stuck; a **neural net** proposes *one* new object; repeat ("this loop continues until a solution is found"). The seam passes **discrete messages**, so no gradient crosses it — the integration problem is **sidestepped, not solved**, at the cost of a **hand-built engine and seam, per domain**.
3. **The tight join that integrates (Part 3).** Make the logic **soft** — truth a dial in [0, 1] — so operators become smooth and one gradient flows through everything. **Logic-as-loss** (LTN, semantic loss) and **logic-as-probabilistic-program** (DeepProbLog, "neural networks as predicates") genuinely fuse learner and reasoner.
4. **The three taxes (Part 4).** Softening the logic costs its **exactness** (approximate, not guaranteed), its **lawfulness** (the operators that learn best "no longer satisfy the usual logical laws"), and its **tractable depth** (exact inference is #P-hard, blows up combinatorially).
5. **The synthesis (Part 5).** The integration problem is a **fork**: keep the logic (loose join — hand-built, absorbed by scale — 2025 Olympiad gold went to a *plain* model) *or* learn the seam (tight join — soft, un-exact, doesn't scale). **Type 6, the exact-and-learnable dream, "no NeSy approach to-date can truly meet."** Exactness and learnability are at war.

## Judging the machinery: where the concrete join-mechanisms are stuck

The [AP7 card](../20-the-approaches/07_ap7-neurosymbolic-and-hybrid-ai.md) judged the **bet** (four cracks: the integration problem is unsolved · the Bitter Lesson / "just a crutch" · wins trapped in tidy checkable domains · "neurosymbolic" is too loose to falsify). This page judges the **mechanisms we actually have** — a sharper question: *given the Kautz dial, the loose join, and the tight join, what do the concrete pieces reveal that the card's one-paragraph "it's unsolved" could not?* Be fair first: these are real, working, valuable systems — AlphaGeometry is a genuine triumph, and differentiable logic is a deep and honest attempt at the hardest problem in the field. Nothing here says neurosymbolic AI is worthless; loose-join systems are among the most reliable AI we have. But the mechanisms have their **own** four cracks, and each sharpens a crack the card only stated.

### Stuck #1 — the join that works doesn't integrate; it avoids

The card's Stuck #1 was "the integration problem is unsolved." The mechanism sharpens it into something more specific and more awkward: the systems that **work** (Part 2) do not solve the join at all — they **dodge** it. AlphaGeometry, tool-using models, AlphaProof — every reliable neurosymbolic success keeps the neural and symbolic halves in **separate boxes** with a **discrete, human-designed pipe** between them, precisely so that no gradient ever has to cross the logic. That is a real achievement, but it is the *opposite* of what "integrate the two halves" promised: nothing about the join is learned, the symbolic engine is rebuilt by hand for each new domain, and the seam is a person's design, not the system's. So the honest status of the integration problem is not "unsolved but progressing." It is "**avoided** where we succeed, and **attempted-but-taxed** where we actually try." The card's "we don't know how to join them in general" becomes "the joins we know either don't learn the seam or don't stay logic." **[Established — every deployed neurosymbolic win is a loose join that sidesteps the gradient problem.]**

### Stuck #2 — the tight join dissolves the exactness that was the point

This is the deepest of the four, because it turns a *hoped-for* fix into a *structural* trade-off. The tight join (Part 3) is the real attempt to solve integration — and Part 4 showed it can only do so by making the logic **soft**, which surrenders the three properties (exactness, lawfulness, tractable depth) that were the *entire reason* to add a reasoner. This is not an engineering wrinkle to be ironed out later; van Krieken's result is that the operators which *learn best are the ones that stopped obeying logic's laws* — the tension is baked into the mathematics of softening. So the neurosymbolic bet faces a possibility it rarely states out loud: the exactness that makes symbols worth having and the smoothness that makes learning possible may be **fundamentally opposed**, so that "an exact reasoner you can train end-to-end" is not a hard target we haven't hit but a **combination we may not be able to have.** If that is right, the bet's central promise — fuse the two into one better machine — is asking for something the mechanism forbids. **[Contested — the exactness-vs-learnability tension is demonstrated on today's methods; whether it is a permanent law or a temporary limit is open.]**

### Stuck #3 — the honest tight join doesn't scale

The card's Stuck #3 was "the wins are trapped in tidy, checkable domains." The mechanism adds a second cage *inside* the tight join: even in a tidy domain, the genuinely-reasoning tight join (DeepProbLog-style) is **throttled by cost.** Exact gradient-based reasoning over a logic program is **#P-hard** and blows up combinatorially with the number of facts (Part 4, Tax 3), so the elegant end-to-end demos stay small; scaling them forces a retreat to **approximate** inference, which forfeits the exactness that motivated the tight join in the first place. So the tight join is squeezed from both sides: keep it exact and it won't scale; scale it and it won't stay exact. The loose join escapes this only by not reasoning *through* the neural facts at all — it hands finished, discrete facts to a separate engine. The honest 2026 picture is that **no neurosymbolic system reasons deeply, exactly, *and* at scale through a learned join** — you get at most two of the three. **[Established / dated — the exact-inference scaling wall is well-documented; the exact frontier of what approximations can recover is active research.]**

### Stuck #4 — the working join is hand-built, and scale keeps absorbing it

The card's Stuck #2 (the Bitter Lesson) said hand-built structure tends to lose to more scale. The mechanism gives that abstract worry a **dated, concrete instance on the field's showcase problem.** The loose join's strength — a hand-built symbolic engine and seam — is exactly the kind of hand-built structure the Bitter Lesson warns about. And in 2025 the warning came true in public: the neurosymbolic pipeline that reached Olympiad **silver** in 2024 was **outdone by a plain end-to-end model that reached gold in 2025**, with no bolted-on proof-search pipeline (Part 5). One data-point is not a verdict — the general model is itself trained to reason, and whether it *has* the exactness or only *mimics* it is the open crux. But it makes the Bitter Lesson's threat to AP7 painfully specific: the parts of the join that are **hand-engineered** are the parts most exposed to being **absorbed** by the next, stronger, plainer learner — and on the one problem where we can watch the race year-over-year, that is what happened. **[Contested but sharpened — the 2024→2025 silver→gold shift from pipeline to plain model is real; its meaning for the bet is the live dispute.]**

### The big question under all four

The card asked: *is the split between learning and symbol-manipulation a real, permanent fact — or a temporary name for abilities scale will grow on its own?* The mechanisms answer a sharper, harder version of it: **is there any join of learner and reasoner that is both *exact* and *learnable end-to-end* — or are those two properties in genuine, permanent tension?** Every mechanism on the dial answers by **surrendering one to get the other.** The loose join keeps exactness and gives up learning the seam (so the seam is hand-built, and scale absorbs it). The tight join learns the seam and gives up exactness (so the logic goes soft, unlawful, and unscalable). The dream that would end the argument — Kautz's Type 6, a fused machine that is exact *and* trainable — **does not exist**, by the field's own survey. So the deepest 2026 verdict on neurosymbolic AI at the level of mechanism is not "we haven't found the right wiring yet." It is that **the two things the bet most wants — a reasoner that is exact and a join that is learned — may not be simultaneously buildable**, and every working system so far is a truce that keeps one by surrendering the other. If that tension is *temporary* (a limit of today's soft-logic math), AP7 still has its general, learned, exact join somewhere ahead, and the bet is alive. If the tension is *permanent* — if exactness and end-to-end learnability are genuinely opposed — then the neurosymbolic dream in its strong form is asking for a machine the mathematics forbids, and the field's real future is the **loose** join it already has: hand-built seams, forever partly hand-built, forever partly absorbed by scale. *As of July 2026, which of those two worlds we live in is genuinely unknown — and it may be the deepest open question the whole approach turns on.* **[Contested — the key open question, now located precisely: is exact-and-learnable a hard target or an impossible one?]**

## ⚠️ Honesty box

- **The mechanisms are real; "the two halves are joined" is not.** The Kautz dial, AlphaGeometry's loop, and differentiable logic are genuine, published, working machinery. But opening them shows the join is either **avoided** (loose) or **taxed into softness** (tight) — not achieved in the strong sense the bet promises. This page opens the machinery the card lacked; it does not claim the integration problem is solved. **[Established that the machinery exists; Contested that it fulfils the bet.]**
- **The loose join's wins are real and narrow — and that cuts both ways.** AlphaGeometry / AlphaProof are medal-level and genuinely useful. But they live in **formal, checkable** domains with **hand-built** engines and **hand-designed** seams, and they work by *sidestepping* the integration problem. They prove the loose join *works*; they do not prove the two halves were *integrated*, and they do not reach the open, messy, common-sense world. **[Established win, contested reach.]**
- **"Make the logic differentiable" is a real research program, not a solved trick.** Differentiable / fuzzy logic is a serious, honest attack on the hardest problem in the field. Report it as *promising and taxed*, not as *done*: it gives up exactness, its best-learning operators stop obeying logic's laws, and exact inference doesn't scale. Do not read "neurosymbolic system with differentiable logic" as "an exact reasoner you can train." **[Contested / dated.]**

It does **not** prove neurosymbolic is a dead end: the plain model is itself trained to reason, and whether it *has* real symbol-like exactness or *imitates* it is exactly the open crux. - **Names, systems, and scores age; the trade-off doesn't.** AlphaGeometry 2's 84%, the 2024 silver, the 2025 gold, LTN / semantic-loss / DeepProbLog, the specific #P-hardness results — all 2020–2026 snapshots. The lasting parts are the **loose-vs-tight dial** (Kautz's six types), the **loose join sidesteps the gradient** (neuro proposes, discrete tool disposes), the **tight join softens the logic** (fuzzy relaxation) and pays the **three taxes** (exactness, lawfulness, scale), and the **exactness-vs-learnability trade-off** with **Type 6 still empty.** The scores around them will change. **[Established core, dated specifics.]**

## How to use this (if you want to direct AI work)

- **First ask where a "neurosymbolic" system sits on the dial — loose or tight.** (Part 1.) That one question tells you almost everything. **Loose** (separate boxes, a clean pipe — a model calling a checker, a solver, a database) is reliable *today* and easy to build, but nothing about the join is learned and the symbolic part is hand-made per domain. **Tight** (differentiable logic, logic-in-the-loss) is where the deep research is, but expect small scale and softened guarantees. Most working products want the loose end; most research excitement is at the tight end. Know which you are being sold.
- **For the loose join, watch the seam and the domain.** (Part 2, Stuck #1.) The engineering that matters is *what the net is trusted to guess* and *what the discrete tool verifies* — and it is hand-built for one domain. Before believing a loose-join demo will transfer, ask: *is there a hand-built exact engine for this new domain, and who designed the seam?* If the answer is "the model just does it internally," you are back to trusting a bare net on exact work.
- **For the tight join, ask what the softening cost.** (Part 4.) If someone made the logic differentiable, three questions expose the taxes: *Is the reasoning still exact, or only approximately obeyed?* *Do the operators still obey logic's laws, or were they swapped for ones that merely learn well?* *How deep a chain of reasoning does it handle before inference blows up?* A tight-join pitch that can't answer these is selling the word, not the machine.
- **Treat "exact and learnable end-to-end" as an unmet claim, not a feature.** (Stuck #2, the big question.) The fused machine that both learns and reasons exactly — Kautz's Type 6 — does not exist as of 2026. If a system claims it, assume it has quietly paid one of the three taxes and find out which. The honest state of the art is *pick one*: exact (loose, hand-built seam) **or** learned (tight, soft logic).
- **Respect the Bitter Lesson on the seam specifically.** (Stuck #4.) Every hand-built symbolic engine and hand-designed seam is reliable today and *exposed* to being absorbed by the next stronger model — as the 2024→2025 Olympiad silver→gold shift showed. Prefer loose-join components that are **cheap to keep or drop** over deep hand-engineered pipelines you would be sorry to throw away.
- **What you delegate vs what you keep.** *Delegate:* building the neural net, wiring the symbolic tool, implementing the differentiable-logic layer, running the proof-checker. *Keep for yourself:* deciding *what must be exact* (and therefore handed to a discrete tool, not guessed), reading "differentiable logic" as "softened logic with three taxes" rather than "logic you can train," and holding the hard question in view — that the join which keeps the logic can't learn its seam, the join which learns the seam can't keep the logic, and nobody has yet built the machine that does both.
