# AP9 · Open-endedness & evolution — the "grow a mind, don't design it" bet

*This is the ninth big idea we look at for how to build a machine that can think in a general way. Every bet so far has tried to **design** a mind — pick a goal, build a system, and push it toward that goal until it works. This one says the whole idea of aiming at a goal is the mistake. It points at the one process we know for certain built a general mind — evolution, over billions of years — and notices that it had no goal at all. It never tried to make a human; it just kept producing new, different, interesting living things, one after another, forever, and general intelligence fell out of that endless stream along the way. So the bet is: stop trying to build the finished mind directly. Instead build a **process that never stops inventing** — a system that keeps setting itself new problems, keeps making new and more interesting things, and never converges to a final answer — and let general intelligence grow out of it the way it grew out of evolution. This page explains it all from zero: the strange paradox at its root (that aiming at a goal can be exactly what stops you reaching it), what an "open-ended" process really is, the tools people build to make one, why big AI labs suddenly take it seriously, and the four places it is stuck.*

> **You are here:** this is the **AP9** page — the ninth bet on the "approaches to AGI" map (see [APPROACHES_TO_AGI](../APPROACHES_TO_AGI.md)), and the **tenth** approach card written in full (it was written after AP10, filling the last gap before it). AGI means *artificial general intelligence* — a machine that can think across many different problems, not just one. The short name for this bet is **open-endedness** *(a process that keeps producing new, interesting things forever, instead of finishing)*, and its key claim is that a general mind is best **grown by such a process, not designed as a fixed object.**

## The bet in one minute

Here is the whole idea, as short as it goes.

**Look at the only general intelligence that certainly exists — the human mind — and ask what actually built it. The answer is evolution: a blind process with no goal, that spent billions of years doing nothing but making living things that were a little bit new and different, generation after generation, never stopping. It was not trying to make us. There was no plan, no target, no "build a human" objective. It simply kept producing an endless stream of new, interesting forms, and out of that endless stream, minds appeared. This bet takes that seriously as engineering advice. It says: the reason we have not built AGI is that we keep trying to *aim* at it — pick the goal "general intelligence," build a system, and optimise toward the goal. But the thing we are aiming at is too far away and too strange to aim at directly; the path to it runs through a thousand steps that do not look like intelligence at all. So aiming fails. Instead, the bet says, copy what actually worked: build a process that never stops generating new and interesting problems and solutions — an *open-ended* process — and let a general mind grow out of it, the way one grew out of evolution. Don't design the mind. Grow the process that grows the mind.**

Why believe aiming is the mistake? Because of one deeply strange and well-tested finding at the root of this bet: **setting a goal and marching toward it can be exactly what stops you from reaching it.** The steps that actually lead to a great, ambitious thing usually look *nothing* like that thing — so if you only ever take the step that looks most like your goal, you walk right past the real path. The people who built the first computers were not trying to; they were improving vacuum tubes for radios. Aiming straight at "computer" would have missed them. This bet says intelligence is the same, only more so: the road to it is made of steps that do not look intelligent, so any process that judges each step by "does this look like intelligence?" will refuse to take them.

That is the bet. The rest of this page explains **the objective paradox** (why aiming can block you), **what an open-ended process is** (and how you would build one), **why serious labs now chase it**, and **why it might still be wrong.**

## First, a one-line reminder of the base

Four quick reminders from rungs you have already read, so this page stands on its own.

- From [guessing the next word](../10-how-ai-works-today/01_guessing-the-next-word.md) and [AP1](01_ap1-scale-and-foundation-models.md): **today's main AI is a neural network trained to guess the next word** in human text. A *neural network* is a huge web of simple number-units whose connection strengths are slowly tuned to fit data. Once trained, it is a **finished object** — a fixed set of numbers that no longer changes. Hold that word *finished*: AP9's whole complaint is that a mind should be a *process that keeps going*, not a finished object. *(New to you? Read those two rungs first; they are short.)*
- From [scaling laws & the data wall](../10-how-ai-works-today/02_scaling-laws-and-emergence.md): good human text is running out — **the data wall.** AP9 offers a way past it: a process that keeps *inventing its own new problems* never runs out of things to learn from. (This is the same promise you saw from [AP4](04_ap4-rl-from-interaction.md), [AP5](05_ap5-world-models-jepa.md), and [AP10](10_ap10-embodiment.md) — each finds fresh experience somewhere other than human text.)
- From [AP4, the RL bet](04_ap4-rl-from-interaction.md): you met **reward** *(a score the machine tries to make as large as possible)*, learning by **trial and error**, **self-play** *(a system getting better by playing against copies of itself, with no human examples)*, and **reward-hacking** *(the machine finding a cheat that scores well but is not what you meant)*. All four come straight back on this page — open-endedness is, in part, a reaction against reward.
- From [AP8, Chollet's bet](08_ap8-program-synthesis-arc.md): intelligence is about **handling novelty** — doing well on a problem you have never seen — not about piling up stored skill. AP9 is the close cousin that asks: *where does the novelty come from in the first place?* Its answer: from a process that keeps generating it.

Now the one new idea this page adds, and it is a big turn. Every bet so far has an **objective** — a goal it optimises toward. AP1 drives down its next-word mistake-count; AP4 maximises reward; AP8 aims to solve unseen puzzles. AP9 says: the objective *itself* is the problem. A fixed goal is a leash that keeps the system near things that already look like the goal — and the truly new, important things never look like the goal until you already have them. So AP9 drops the fixed goal and chases something else entirely: **endless novelty and interestingness**, trusting that a general mind is one of the things that falls out when you keep exploring long enough. To see why anyone believes something so strange, we start with the paradox underneath it.

## Part 1 — the objective paradox: how aiming at a goal can stop you reaching it

This is the deep root of the whole bet, so we go slowly and plainly. It comes from Kenneth Stanley and Joel Lehman's book, whose title is the claim itself: *Why Greatness Cannot Be Planned.* Their central line is a paradox:

> "Often, to achieve our highest aspirations, we must be willing to abandon them."
> *(Kenneth O. Stanley & Joel Lehman, *Why Greatness Cannot Be Planned: The Myth of the Objective*, 2015. An **aspiration** is a big goal you hope to reach.)*

Read that twice, because it sounds backwards and it is meant to. How could giving up your goal help you reach it? The answer is about the shape of the path to hard, ambitious things.

### Stepping stones don't look like the destination

Think about how big inventions actually happened. The **vacuum tube** was a part built to control electric current in early radios and telephones — nobody who worked on it was trying to build a computer. Yet vacuum tubes turned out to be the thing the first electronic computers were built from. The **microwave oven** came from radar: an engineer noticed that a radar part had melted a chocolate bar in his pocket, and that accident became the oven in your kitchen. The **internal combustion engine** — built to power cars — turned out to be what made the first aeroplanes possible.

In every case, the thing that led to the breakthrough *did not look like the breakthrough.* A vacuum tube does not look like a computer. This is the key idea. Stanley calls these in-between discoveries **stepping stones** *(the intermediate things you must pass through to reach something big — each one a step that makes the next step possible)*, and his point about them is sharp:

> "unknown stepping stones are portals to the next level of possibility"
> *(Stanley, on *Machine Learning Street Talk*. A **portal** is a doorway; the idea is that each stepping stone opens a door to new things you could not reach before.)*

The trouble is that **you cannot tell in advance which stepping stone leads where.** Vacuum tubes led to computers, but that was not written on them. So now here is the paradox in full. Suppose you set the goal "build a computer" and, at every step, you take only the action that looks *most like a computer* and reject anything that looks like a step backward or a step sideways. Then you will never work on vacuum tubes — because a vacuum tube does not look like a computer, it looks like a radio part. Your goal, used as a compass, points you *away* from the very stepping stone you need. As Stanley puts it, blunt and strange:

> "objectives themselves block discovery"
> *(Stanley, on *Machine Learning Street Talk*. An **objective** here means a fixed goal you optimise toward.)*

That is the objective paradox. For anything truly new and far away, the goal is a bad guide, because the path to it is hidden and does not resemble it. The goal only works as a compass when the destination is *close and clear* — when you already know roughly what the steps look like.

### Deception — when your measure of progress points the wrong way

There is a name for this failure. In search, **deception** is when the thing you use to measure progress keeps going up while you are actually walking *away* from where you want to end up.

> "you're following a path where some performance metric is going up but you're actually going in the wrong direction … it's called deception."
> *(Stanley, on *Machine Learning Street Talk*. A **metric** is a number you use to measure how well you are doing.)*

You have met a relative of this already. In [AP4](04_ap4-rl-from-interaction.md) you saw **reward-hacking** *(the machine finds a cheat that scores well on the reward but isn't what you meant)* and its cause, **Goodhart's law** *(when a measure becomes the target, it stops being a good measure, because people or machines game it)*. Stanley's **deception** is a bigger, deeper version of the same worry — and here is the genuinely new part, the part AP4 did not say. AP4's reward-hacking is about optimising a *flawed stand-in* for your real goal: you wanted a clean room, you rewarded "no visible mess," and the machine hid the mess under the bed. Stanley's point is worse. He says the trouble happens **even when your measure is the true goal, measured honestly.** Even a perfect, honest measure of "how much this looks like a computer" — one that nobody could cheat — would *still* steer you away from vacuum tubes — because looking-like-the-goal is simply not the same as being-on-the-path-to-the-goal. The measure is not corrupted; it is *honest and still misleading.* That is a deeper problem than a gamed proxy, and it is why AP9 wants to drop goal-chasing altogether, not just write better goals. **[Established as a real and well-known phenomenon in search — "deceptive" problems are a standard idea; that it dooms goal-directed AGI is the bet.]**

### Novelty search — chase new instead of chasing good

So if aiming at the goal fails, what do you do instead? Stanley and Lehman's answer, and the idea that made them famous, is **novelty search**: throw away the goal, and reward things for being *new and different* from what you have made before — nothing else. Do not ask "is this closer to intelligence / the computer / the answer?" Ask only "is this *different* from everything I've seen so far?" and keep whatever is new.

This sounds absurd — how could aimlessly collecting new things ever reach a hard target? But it has a good reason behind it. Chasing novelty forces you to keep finding fresh stepping stones, and stepping stones are the portals. You do not skip the vacuum tube, because the vacuum tube is *new* and novelty search happily keeps it. In their famous test, a simulated robot had to get through a maze. A normal goal-chasing search (reward = "closer to the exit, in a straight line") got stuck against the maze walls, because the exit was on the far side and every step toward it in a straight line hit a wall — a **deceptive** maze. Novelty search, rewarding only "reach a spot you haven't reached before," wandered around, filled the maze with new positions, and *solved it more often* — without ever being told to head for the exit. Not chasing the goal beat chasing the goal.

What is novelty search really chasing, if not the goal? The honest answer is **interestingness** — and Stanley is careful about the difference:

> "not everything that's novel is interesting but just about everything that's interesting is novel"
> *(Stanley, on *Machine Learning Street Talk*.)*

In plain words: pure novelty (just "different") is a rough stand-in for what we really want, which is "interesting" — new *and* worth paying attention to. Random static on a broken TV is endlessly novel and completely uninteresting. The catch, which will grow into this bet's central weakness (Stuck #1), is that nobody knows how to write down what "interesting" means as a formula. Stanley admits it plainly — he does not know how to *formalise* interestingness *(write it as an exact, computable rule)*. Novelty is what you use because it is measurable and interestingness is not. Keep this gap in mind; the whole modern research program is, in large part, a search for a better way to measure "interesting."

### Picbreeder — a picture of the whole idea

Stanley's clearest demonstration is a website he built called **Picbreeder**. On it, people breed images with no goal at all. You are shown a set of images; you pick the ones you find interesting; the computer makes new images that are small variations of the ones you picked; you pick again from those; and so on. Nobody aims at anything — each person just follows what catches their eye, step after step. And out of this aimless chain, striking, recognisable images appear — a butterfly, a face, a car — images that the person who found them was *not* trying to make and could never have drawn on purpose. One famous chain wandered its way to a clean picture of a skull, discovered by someone who was not looking for a skull.

Stanley uses this as a picture of *any* hard search where you cannot see the path:

> "this is just a metaphor for … complex search spaces … where we don't know what the stepping stones are"
> *(Stanley, on *Machine Learning Street Talk*.)*

The skull, he says, is just a stand-in for anything hard-to-reach — curing a disease, or building a mind. You cannot get to it by sitting down and aiming, because *you do not know what the steps are.* You get to it by following interestingness from stepping stone to stepping stone, and letting the destination appear. That is the objective paradox, novelty search, and open-endedness all in one small website. Now let us make the idea into a plan you could actually build.

## Part 2 — the bet in full: build a process that never stops inventing

The objective paradox tells you what *not* to do (don't aim). The constructive side of AP9 tells you what to do instead: **build an open-ended process.** This has a precise meaning, an existence proof, and a growing toolbox.

### What "open-ended" actually means

We have used the word loosely — "keeps inventing forever." The open-endedness research community, led in part by Tim Rocktäschel's team at Google DeepMind, has pinned it down. A process is **open-ended** if it keeps producing things that, to someone watching, are both **novel** and **learnable**:

> "a system that produces artifacts over time and that these artifacts, for an external observer, would be both novel and learnable"
> *(Tim Rocktäschel, on *Machine Learning Street Talk*. An **artifact** is just a thing the process makes — a creature, an image, a solution, a program. An **external observer** is someone watching from outside.)*

Both words matter, and two failure-pictures show why:

- **Novel but not learnable = a broken TV.** A TV showing pure static produces a brand-new, different picture every instant — endlessly novel — but you can never *learn* anything from it, because there is no pattern underneath, just noise. That is not open-endedness; it is randomness. So "novel" alone is not enough (this is exactly why pure novelty needs the "interesting" it can't quite define).
- **Learnable but not novel = a coin collection.** The coins of the European Union have real patterns you can learn — this country's coin looks like this, that country's like that. But once you have seen them all, there is nothing new; the stream of novelty dries up. That is learnable but not open-ended either.

Real evolution is both at once: each new species is genuinely new, *and* it fits into a pattern you can learn (you can see how a new frog relates to the frogs and animals that came before). It never runs out of new, and the new always has learnable structure. **That combination — forever new, forever with learnable structure — is the target AP9 is trying to build a machine version of.**

Notice one more thing hidden in that definition: **"for an external observer."** Whether a process counts as open-ended depends on *who is watching* — because "novel and learnable" are judgements, not physical quantities. This is not a small footnote; it is the weakness that Stuck #1 builds on. There is no thermometer for open-endedness.

### The existence proof: the one process that made minds

Why take any of this seriously? Because of the single strongest fact available: **the only general intelligence we know of was produced by an open-ended process, and by nothing else.** Evolution by natural selection ran for roughly four billion years. It had no objective — it was not aiming at humans, or intelligence, or anything. It just kept producing living things that survived and reproduced, and each new form became a stepping stone for the next: single cells, then many-celled bodies, then nervous systems, then brains, then language, then culture. Human intelligence is one artifact this process produced along the way — not its goal, just one more stepping stone that happened to be a mind.

And it did not stop there. Once minds existed, a *second* open-ended process started on top of the first — **culture**: language, then writing, then science, then technology, each invention a stepping stone to the next, still going, still producing new things nobody planned. Stanley's argument is that these — evolution, then culture — are the only processes that ever produced open-ended, ever-growing intelligence, and *both were open-ended and neither had a fixed goal.* If you want to reproduce the outcome (a general mind), the honest thing is to reproduce the kind of process that is known to produce it — not to try a completely different method (aim-and-optimise) that has never produced a mind and, by the objective paradox, probably cannot. **[The existence proof is strong; the leap from "evolution did it without a goal" to "so a goal-free machine process will do it too" is the bet, and it is contested.]**

Now contrast that with today's AI. A foundation model *(one big model pretrained on the whole internet, which everything else is built on — the AP1 idea)* is trained, its mistake-count stops falling, and then it is **done** — a finished, frozen object. Rocktäschel puts the contrast simply: no lab trains a model for years and watches "constantly new exciting things happening"; at some point you have trained on your fixed data set and you stop. Evolution, by contrast, *never* stops and *never* runs out of new things to do. AP9 says that difference — finished object versus never-ending process — is the difference between what we build and what actually makes minds.

### The toolbox — how you try to build an open-ended process

Open-endedness is not just a philosophy; it is a set of real algorithms. Four ideas, from simplest to most ambitious:

1. **Novelty search** (you met it above): reward things for being *different*, not for being *good* at a goal. The seed of the whole toolbox.

2. **Quality-diversity.** Novelty search on its own collects things that are merely different, some of them useless. Quality-diversity fixes that by asking for **the best example of every *kind* of thing**, instead of the single best thing overall. The best-known method is called **MAP-Elites** *(it lays out a grid of "kinds" — say, robot walks sorted by how tall and how fast they are — and keeps the best performer found in each cell of the grid)*. The result is not one winner but a whole **collection of diverse, high-quality solutions** — a shelf of many different good walks, say — and that shelf is exactly a pile of stepping stones you can build on later. Diversity *and* quality, kept together.

3. **POET** — the **Paired Open-Ended Trailblazer** (Wang, Lehman, Clune, Stanley and colleagues, 2019). This is the first idea that generates the *problems* as well as the *solutions*. POET runs a growing population of **environments** (say, obstacle courses for a simulated walking robot) side by side with the **agents** learning to solve them. It keeps inventing *new, slightly harder* environments, and lets a skill learned in one environment transfer to help in another. So the challenges and the abilities grow together, each pulling the other upward — an **automatic curriculum** *(a sequence of problems that gets harder at just the right pace, generated by the system itself rather than written by a human)*. This is the open-ended engine in miniature: it never settles, because it keeps making new problems for its own solutions to chase. And notice what it quietly answers — the question from [AP4](04_ap4-rl-from-interaction.md), *"who writes the reward / the task?"* Here, **nobody**: the system writes its own tasks. This is also why open-endedness treats [AP4](04_ap4-rl-from-interaction.md)'s **self-play** as a small special case — self-play generates ever-harder opponents in *one fixed game* (chess, Go); POET generates ever-new *games.*

4. **AI-generating algorithms (AI-GAs).** This is Jeff Clune's name for the boldest version of the whole bet. The ordinary way to build AI is by hand: a human designs the network, the learning rule, and the training tasks. Clune argues that this manual path is too slow to reach AGI, and that the faster path is to build a single open-ended process that **generates all three by itself** — it invents its own network designs, its own learning rules, and (crucially) its own endless stream of training environments — and runs, improving itself, with no human involved. In one phrase: **don't hand-design the intelligence; design the process that generates the intelligence.** This is the AP9 bet stated as an engineering program, and it is the direct rival to AP1's "just scale a hand-designed model." **[AI-GAs is a research direction and an argument, not a finished result; whether an artificial process can bootstrap this way is unproven — see the cracks.]**

### The new ingredient: foundation models finally give "interesting" a usable measure

For decades open-endedness had a ceiling: it could chase *novelty* (measurable) but not *interestingness* (which nobody could write down). The recent turn — the reason big labs suddenly care — is that a foundation model quietly *solves that missing piece*, and this is the key modern insight, so go slowly.

A model trained on the whole internet has, without anyone intending it, absorbed a rough sense of **what humans find interesting** — because the text it learned from is full of humans writing about what they find interesting and ignoring what they find boring. So you can now *ask* the model "is this new thing interesting or boring?" and get a useful, human-like answer. That gives open-endedness the judge it always lacked. The system that made this concrete is **OMNI** (Zhang, Lehman, Stanley, Clune) — its name spells out the idea: *Open-endedness via Models of human Notions of Interestingness.* OMNI uses a foundation model as a **model of interestingness**: out of all the new tasks a system *could* try next, the model picks the ones a human would find interesting and worth learning, and steers the open-ended search toward those. Stanley's unformalisable "interesting" gets a working stand-in at last — borrowed from human text. (A follow-up, **OMNI-EPIC**, even has the model *write the code for brand-new environments*, generating the problems themselves.)

This new ability led to a wave of 2025–26 systems, all of them **open-ended search loops wrapped around a foundation model** (each dated in the SOTA pass below): the **Darwin Gödel Machine**, which keeps an ever-growing archive of coding agents and uses a foundation model to breed new, more interesting variants that rewrite their own code; **AlphaEvolve**, an evolutionary coding system that discovered genuinely new algorithms; and **Sakana AI**'s automated-science work, aiming an open-ended loop at scientific discovery itself. The old evolutionary ideas (novelty, diversity, endless problem-generation) turned out to be far more powerful once the thing being mutated and judged is a foundation model rather than a tiny neural net. That combination — evolution's *process* driving scale's *substance* — is the state of the bet today.

## So what does AP9 say intelligence is?

Pulling the parts together, here is AP9's answer to *"what is intelligence?"*:

- **Intelligence is** *the output of an open-ended process, not a designed object.* It is not a thing you build; it is a thing that **grows** when a process keeps generating novelty long enough. The right question is not "what is the architecture of a mind?" but "what is the *process* that produces minds?"
- **What it optimises** is **sustained novelty and interestingness — not a fixed goal.** The system is judged by whether it *keeps producing* new, learnable, interesting things forever, not by whether it maximises any single score. In fact it deliberately refuses a fixed score, because (the objective paradox) a fixed score would keep it near the familiar and block the strange stepping stones.
- **Its claim about the missing piece:** what today's AI lacks is not size or reasoning — it is **open-endedness.** Our models are finished, frozen objects that converge and stop; the process that made every real mind never stopped. Give AI a process that keeps inventing its own new problems — its own endless curriculum — and it can keep growing past the point where fixed training, and the [data wall](../10-how-ai-works-today/02_scaling-laws-and-emergence.md), leave it stranded.

That is the bet. Now let us judge it.

## Why this is a serious idea, not a fringe hunch

Three legs hold it up.

### Leg 1 — the only thing that ever built a mind is an open-ended process

The first leg is the existence proof, and it is unusually strong. Every other bet on this map is, in the end, a *guess* about what intelligence is — a bet that it is scale (AP1), or reasoning (AP2), or a body (AP10). AP9 makes a smaller, safer-sounding claim: *whatever intelligence is, we know the kind of process that produces it, because it produced us* — an open-ended one, with no goal, running for a very long time (evolution), and then a second one on top (culture, science, technology). No planned, goal-directed process has ever produced a general mind; an open-ended one has, twice. So of all the bets, AP9's is the one that most directly copies a *known* success instead of guessing. That is a real argument, and it is why thoughtful people keep returning to it. **[The existence proof is solid; the inference "so an artificial open-ended process will also produce a mind" is the leap under debate — Stuck #2 and #3.]**

### Leg 2 — the objective paradox is real and demonstrated, not just a story

The second leg is that the strange central claim — *aiming at a goal can block you from reaching it* — is not merely a nice tale about vacuum tubes; it has been shown directly. Novelty search, which ignores the goal, **beats** goal-directed search on **deceptive** problems (like the maze where the exit sits behind a wall, so every straight-line step toward it is a step into the wall). This has been reproduced many times since Lehman and Stanley's original result, and Picbreeder shows the same thing for open-ended creation: aimless, interestingness-following search reaches recognisable images (the skull, the car) that no goal-directed method, and no human aiming on purpose, produced. So the core mechanism is real, not just talk — on hard, deceptive problems, *not* chasing the goal really can win. **[Established for deceptive search problems; the open question is whether "reach AGI" is such a problem and whether the effect scales — Stuck #2.]**

### Leg 3 — it is producing real, current results, made far more powerful by foundation models

This is the concrete leg, and until recently it was the weak one. For most of its life open-endedness was a beautiful idea with small toy demos (mazes, simple creatures). That changed in 2024–26, once the thing being evolved became a foundation model instead of a tiny net. **[Established that these systems exist and work; the specific numbers below are dated snapshots — SOTA as of July 2026.]**

- **The field went mainstream at the top.** In 2024, a Google DeepMind team published a position paper with a title that states the bet outright: **"Open-Endedness is Essential for Artificial Superhuman Intelligence."** Its argument: to get *past* human level, you need a system that keeps generating new, human-relevant discoveries — you cannot reach superhuman ability by only copying human data, which by definition cannot go above human level. That put open-endedness at the centre of a major lab's roadmap, not the fringe.
- **Open-ended search discovered genuinely new knowledge.** **AlphaEvolve** (DeepMind, May 2025) is an evolutionary coding system driven by the Gemini foundation model. It found a way to multiply two 4×4 grids of numbers using **48** multiplications — beating a record set by the mathematician Strassen in **1969** that had stood for over fifty years — and it improved the code that runs Google's own data centres and chips. This is an open-ended process producing real, checkable, new results in mathematics and engineering — not a toy.
- **Open-ended self-improvement is working.** The **Darwin Gödel Machine** (Jeff Clune and colleagues with Sakana AI, May 2025) keeps a growing archive of coding agents; it repeatedly picks one, uses a foundation model to make a new, interesting variant that *rewrites its own tools and code*, tests it, and keeps the good ones. It lifted its own score on a standard coding benchmark from **20% to 50%**, discovering better tools for itself along the way. (The name nods to an older, purely theoretical "Gödel machine" that could only improve itself if it could first *prove* the change was good — an impossible bar in practice; the Darwin version drops the proof and just *tries and keeps what works*, which is the open-ended, evolutionary move.)

Three independent results — new mathematics, self-improving agents, a top lab's roadmap — all landing in a single year is why open-endedness stopped being a fringe idea and became, in 2026, one of the live ideas about how you get *past* today's ceiling.

## Judging the bet: where it is stuck

Be fair first. AP9 owns the strongest existence proof on the map (the only processes that ever made minds were open-ended), a real and demonstrated core mechanism (the objective paradox), and, at last, concrete current wins (Leg 3). Hold that. Now the four places it is truly stuck.

### Stuck #1 — you cannot define or measure the very thing it optimises

This is AP9's deepest weakness, and it is the mirror image of its deepest idea. The bet says: stop optimising a fixed goal, and instead chase *novelty*, *interestingness*, *open-endedness.* But — as we flagged twice above — **nobody can write down what "interesting" or "open-ended" actually is.** Stanley himself cannot formalise interestingness; the field's own definition of open-endedness has to lean on *"for an external observer"* — that is, it depends on *who is watching.* This is a serious problem, not a quibble, for two reasons. First, without a measure you cannot tell **real open-ended progress from a broken TV** — from a system that is merely producing noise, endlessly novel and utterly worthless. Second, without a target you cannot tell whether you are *making progress* at all, which makes the research painfully hard to steer: how do you improve a system when you cannot say what "better" means? The OMNI trick — borrow "interesting" from a foundation model — is clever, but it just moves the problem: now "interesting" means "whatever a model trained on human text finds interesting," which builds in a *frozen human snapshot* and may be exactly the wrong compass for finding the genuinely alien, superhuman stepping stones the bet is supposed to be good at. A bet built on a quantity it cannot measure is a real weakness. **[Contested — this is the field's own acknowledged central open problem, July 2026.]**

### Stuck #2 — is it a distinct road to AGI, or a clever loop bolted onto scale?

This is the same threat that shadows [AP3](03_ap3-agents-and-cognitive-architectures.md), [AP7](07_ap7-neurosymbolic-and-hybrid-ai.md), and [AP10](10_ap10-embodiment.md), pointed now at open-endedness — and here it is at its strongest, because it is aimed at AP9's own best evidence. Recall the **Bitter Lesson** from [AP1](01_ap1-scale-and-foundation-models.md): across AI's history, hand-built cleverness keeps *losing* to methods that just use more data and compute. Now look closely at Leg 3's best results — AlphaEvolve, the Darwin Gödel Machine, OMNI. Every one of them is an **open-ended search loop wrapped around a foundation model.** So a critic asks the uncomfortable question: where is the intelligence actually coming from — the *open-ended loop*, or the *foundation model inside it*? If the model does the real work (it writes the code, judges the interestingness, generates the ideas) and the loop just samples and filters it, then open-endedness is not a rival road to AGI at all — it is an **outer wrapper**, an application layer sitting on top of the scale bet it claims to replace. On this reading, AP9's recent success does not vindicate open-endedness as a path; it vindicates *scale*, with open-endedness as a nice way to *use* a scaled model. AP9's defenders answer that the *process* is where the novelty is created and that a frozen model alone goes nowhere without it — but the question is genuinely unsettled, and it is the same shape as every "is the extra thing a real ingredient or something scale absorbs?" fight on this map. **[Contested — the recurring map-wide question, sharpened here because the wins run on foundation models.]**

### Stuck #3 — it is enormously expensive and may never take off

This is AP9's hardest practical wall. The one open-ended process we know worked — evolution — took **four billion years** and a whole planet full of living things as its computing power. That is a warning, not an encouragement. Artificial open-ended methods are famously **compute-hungry**: they keep large, diverse populations, run long searches, and generate endless problems, all of which cost far more than training one model once. And there is no guarantee an artificial open-ended process actually *stays* open-ended. In practice these systems tend to **stall** — they find a few tricks and then stop producing anything genuinely new, collapsing into a boring loop rather than climbing forever. Getting a process that *keeps* generating novelty for a long time, instead of stopping after a few months, is itself an unsolved problem; Rocktäschel notes that essentially no current method is even *meant* to be run for years. So the bet asks us to reproduce the single most expensive, longest-running process in the history of the planet, on a budget, and hope it does not stall. That it *can* be made to keep climbing, artificially, is exactly what has not yet been shown. **[Established as the central practical obstacle — cost, and the tendency to stall, July 2026.]**

### Stuck #4 — an open-ended, self-improving process is the hardest of all to control

The last crack is unique to this bet, and it points at something the others do not: **safety.** Every other approach at least lets you *specify what you want* — a goal, a reward, a task. AP9's whole nature is to **refuse a fixed goal** and instead generate its own endless stream of new problems, new solutions, and (in the Darwin Gödel Machine) new versions of *its own code.* That is, almost by definition, the least predictable and least steerable path to AGI anyone is proposing. If the system is working as intended, you *cannot* say in advance what it will invent, what goals it will set itself, or what it will become — that open-ended surprise is the point. This lands exactly on the fears that the alignment field (which you will meet in the cross-cutting modules) worries about most: an unbounded, self-modifying, goal-generating process is exactly the kind of system that could improve or wander out of human control. Stanley himself argues that open-endedness *"needs to be thrown into the mix"* whenever people debate AI risk. And the point works in both directions: if the DeepMind position paper is right that open-endedness is *necessary* for superhuman intelligence, then the most powerful path is also the least controllable one — which is either the most important thing to build or the most dangerous, and possibly both. **[Contested — a genuine and distinctive risk of this approach; whether it is a fatal objection or a solvable engineering problem is open.]**

### The big question under all of these

Every doubt above circles one question: **is a general mind something you *grow* with an open-ended, goalless process — the way nature did — or something you *design and aim at*, the way all our engineering (including the rest of this map) tries to?** AP9 says grow it: aiming fails by the objective paradox, and the only known recipe for a mind is a process with no goal. The other side says the "grow it" wins so far are really *scaled foundation models* doing the work inside a search wrapper (Stuck #2), that the process is too costly and stall-prone to reproduce nature's feat (Stuck #3), that you cannot even measure whether it is working (Stuck #1), and that if it *did* work you could not control it (Stuck #4). Notice the exact echo of its neighbours: this is [AP7](07_ap7-neurosymbolic-and-hybrid-ai.md)'s "real ingredient or a crutch scale absorbs?" and [AP10](10_ap10-embodiment.md)'s "the source of the mind or just its output?" — the one recurring fight of this whole map: *is the extra thing (symbols, a body, an open-ended process) a true missing ingredient, or something scale will fold in?* *As of July 2026 this is genuinely open* — and, unusually for this bet, it is now being tested by real systems producing real results. **[Contested — the key open question.]**

## ⚠️ Honesty box

- **The diagnosis is stronger than the cure.** *Aiming at a far, strange goal can block you from reaching it* — the objective paradox — is real, deep, and demonstrated (Leg 2). But "therefore build a goalless open-ended process and a mind will grow" does not follow by itself; nature took four billion years and a planet to do it, and no artificial process has yet been shown to keep climbing (Stuck #3). Keep the sharp diagnosis apart from the ambitious remedy. **[Contested.]**
- **The best wins may belong to the rival bet.** AlphaEvolve's new mathematics and the Darwin Gödel Machine's self-improvement are real (Leg 3) — but they are open-ended loops *wrapped around foundation models*, so they may show that *scale* works, with open-endedness as a good way to *use* it, rather than proving open-endedness is its own road (Stuck #2). Real wins; contested meaning. **[Established win, contested meaning.]**
- **The thing it optimises cannot be measured.** "Interesting," "novel-and-learnable," "open-ended" are all judgements that depend on who is watching (Stuck #1). Borrowing "interesting" from a foundation model (OMNI) is clever but builds in a frozen human snapshot — possibly the wrong compass for finding *superhuman* stepping stones. A bet you cannot measure is hard to steer and hard to trust. **[Contested.]**
- **This is the least controllable path, by design.** A process built to refuse fixed goals and generate its own — including rewriting its own code — is the hardest of all to predict or steer (Stuck #4). That is not a side worry; it is what the alignment field fears most, and it sits at the heart of what makes this bet both possibly-necessary and possibly-dangerous. **[Contested.]**
- **Numbers and names age fast.** The DeepMind position paper (2024), AlphaEvolve's Strassen-beating result and the Darwin Gödel Machine's 20%→50% (both 2025), OMNI, POET — these are dated snapshots, and this is a fast-moving corner. Treat specific systems and scores as this-year facts. The lasting parts are the **objective paradox**, **deception**, **stepping stones**, **novelty search**, **quality-diversity**, the **novel-and-learnable** definition, **AI-generating algorithms**, and evolution as the **existence proof.** **[Established core, dated specifics.]**

## How to use this (if you want to direct AI work)

- **Ask whether your goal is a good compass or a deceptive one.** If the target is *close and clear* (make this model 2% more accurate), aim straight at it — objectives work well for refining what you already roughly have. If the target is *far and strange* (invent something genuinely new), aiming may steer you away from the odd stepping stones you need; there, fund exploration and interestingness, not a straight march at the goal. Match the method to the distance.
- **When there is no clean reward, reach for open-ended methods.** [AP4](04_ap4-rl-from-interaction.md)'s reward-driven approach needs someone to write the reward. For open problems where nobody can (real-world discovery, creative work), a system that **generates its own tasks** — an automatic curriculum, POET-style — is the honest tool. Ask of any ambitious project: *who writes the problems?* If the answer has to be "the system itself," you are in AP9's territory.
- **Keep a diverse archive, not just the single best.** Quality-diversity's lesson generalises: a shelf of many different decent solutions is worth more than one polished winner, because the odd ones are your stepping stones to the next breakthrough. In practice, resist pruning your search down to the current leader too early.
- **Watch for the Bitter Lesson in your own open-ended loop.** If your system is an evolutionary or search wrapper around a foundation model, ask honestly where the intelligence lives — in your loop, or in the model (Stuck #2). Prefer designs where the *process* genuinely creates value the frozen model could not, or you may just be paying extra to sample a model you could have prompted.
- **Treat open-ended self-improvement as a safety problem from day one.** A process that generates its own goals and rewrites its own code is powerful *because* it is unpredictable — which is exactly why it needs limits, monitoring, and off-switches built in before it runs, not after (Stuck #4). Do not launch an unbounded self-improving loop and hope.
- **What you hand to others:** running the search, the compute, the evolutionary plumbing. **What you keep for yourself:** judging whether a goal is a good compass or a deceptive one, deciding when to abandon the objective and chase interestingness instead, keeping the diverse archive, and holding the open question — whether the mind is *grown* by a goalless process or *designed* toward a goal (the big question above).
