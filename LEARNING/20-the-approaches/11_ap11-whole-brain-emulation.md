# AP11 · Whole-brain emulation — the "copy it without understanding it" bet

*This is the eleventh and last big idea we look at for how to build a machine that can think in a general way — and it is the strangest of them all. Every other bet tries to work out **what intelligence is** and then build it: copy human text (AP1), think longer (AP2), chase reward (AP4), model the world (AP5), search for programs (AP8), copy the brain's method (AP6). This one gives up on understanding entirely. It says: we have one machine that is already generally intelligent — a human brain — so stop trying to figure out how it works. Instead, **scan a real brain in complete detail, down to every cell and every connection, and copy that exact structure into a computer.** Run the copy, and — if the scan was faithful enough — a mind wakes up inside the machine, thinking the same way the original did, whether or not anyone understands **why**. This is the most literal "copy the brain" bet, and the most far-off: it needs scanning and computing powers far beyond anything we have. But it is a real, funded scientific effort, and it raises the deepest questions on the whole map — about what a copied mind **is**, and whether making one is even allowed. This page explains it all from zero: the bet in one minute, how "copy without understanding" is supposed to work, how far the copying has actually got, why serious people take it seriously, and the four places it is stuck.*

> **You are here:** this is the **AP11** page — the eleventh and final bet on the "approaches to AGI" map (see [APPROACHES_TO_AGI](../APPROACHES_TO_AGI.md)), and the **eleventh (last) approach card written in full — so this page completes the map.** AGI means *artificial general intelligence* — a machine that can think across many different problems, not just one. The short name for this bet is **whole-brain emulation** *(emulation = an exact working copy of one system running inside another; here, a copy of a real brain running inside a computer)*, often also called **mind uploading** *(the popular name for moving — or rather copying — a mind from a brain onto a computer)*.
>
> **This page builds on rungs of the ladder you have already climbed**, all short and plain: [guessing the next word](../10-how-ai-works-today/01_guessing-the-next-word.md) — how today's AI learns from text; [scaling laws & the data wall](../10-how-ai-works-today/02_scaling-laws-and-emergence.md) — the steady curve scale rides; [AP1 · the "make it bigger" bet](01_ap1-scale-and-foundation-models.md) — the mainstream text-and-scale bet that this one is the complete opposite of; and above all [AP6 · brain-based](06_ap6-brain-based.md) — its **closest cousin and its opposite twin**, where you met the idea of copying the brain. A one-line reminder of each is given where it is used, so you will not get lost. **AP11 is the deep home for whole-brain emulation, mind uploading, and the connectome.**
>
> **Where the facts come from:** the durable idea is grounded in **Sandberg & Bostrom's *"Whole Brain Emulation: A Roadmap"*** (2008, Future of Humanity Institute, Oxford) — the founding document that laid the bet out carefully. The state of the real copying work is grounded in the published **connectome** maps (the worm, the fruit fly, and specks of mouse and human brain) and the big brain-simulation projects. A fresh check of the field, done on the web (**as of July 2026**), covers the newest maps, scanning methods, and funded efforts. Because this bet moves slowly but its *specifics* still age, each fast-moving number is dated below.

## The bet in one minute

Here is the whole idea, as short as it goes.

**Every other bet on this map is a *theory* — a guess about what intelligence really is, turned into a thing to build. This bet refuses to guess. It points at the one machine we already agree is generally intelligent — a human brain — and says: we do not need a theory of it. We just need to *copy* it. Take a real brain, measure its physical structure completely — every one of its billions of cells, and every one of the roughly one hundred trillion tiny connections between them — and build a model of exactly that structure inside a computer. Then switch the model on. Each simulated cell behaves like the real cell it was copied from; the whole copied network then does what the real brain did — it perceives, remembers, decides, talks. A mind runs in the machine. And here is the key, almost unbelievable, claim: this could work *even though no one understands how the brain produces a mind.* You are not building intelligence; you are photocopying it. The picture does not need to understand the page. The champions of this bet are a small community of neuroscientists, computer scientists, and futurists who study "connectomics" and mind uploading.**

Why would anyone believe copying-without-understanding could work? Because of one simple idea: **a faithful enough copy behaves like the original for the same reason the original does — automatically.** If you rebuilt a car engine part for part from an exact blueprint, it would run, even if you had no idea *why* engines run. The bet is that a brain is the same: get the copy detailed enough, and thinking happens too, as a side effect of the structure being right. You never have to solve the mystery of intelligence. You just have to be an extremely good copier.

That is the bet. The rest of this page explains **how "copy without understanding" is supposed to work** (the three steps), **how far the real copying has actually got** (from a worm to a speck of human brain), **why serious people back it**, and **why it might still be wrong.**

## First, a one-line reminder of the base

Three quick reminders from rungs you have already read, so this page stands on its own.

- From [guessing the next word](../10-how-ai-works-today/01_guessing-the-next-word.md) and [AP1](01_ap1-scale-and-foundation-models.md): **today's main AI is a neural network trained to guess the next word** in human text. A *neural network* here means a huge web of simple number-units, *loosely* inspired by brain cells but not a real copy of any brain. AP11 is the far end away from this: not "loosely inspired by the brain," but "an exact copy of one real brain." *(New to those? Read those two rungs first; they are short.)*
- From [AP6 · brain-based](06_ap6-brain-based.md): that bet also said *copy the brain* — but it meant something quite different. AP6 (Jeff Hawkins) wants to **understand the brain's method** (the one repeated "cortical algorithm" *— the single basic recipe the thinking part of the brain runs, copied about 150,000 times*) and then rebuild *that method* in a machine, throwing away the messy biology. Hold that idea tight, because AP11 is its **opposite twin**, and the next paragraph turns on the difference.
- Also from [AP6](06_ap6-brain-based.md): the strongest reason to look at the brain at all is that **it is the only thing we know for certain is generally intelligent** — it learns almost anything, from little, on about 20 watts of power *(a dim light bulb's worth)*. Both AP6 and AP11 start from this one fact. They split on what to *do* with it.

Now the one new idea this page adds — and it is a single, sharp split from AP6. Picture a brilliant old clock you want to reproduce. **AP6 is the watchmaker:** it opens the clock, studies the gears until it *understands the mechanism*, then builds a new clock its own way — maybe out of different parts. **AP11 is the photocopier:** it does not open the clock or understand anything; it measures the exact position of every single part and makes a perfect physical duplicate. AP6 needs to *understand* the brain's algorithm — and, as you saw, its biggest problem is that nobody knows that algorithm yet. AP11 needs to understand *nothing* — only to copy accurately enough. That is the whole bet in one line: **you can have a working mind without any theory of how minds work, if you are willing to copy an existing one in full detail.** Everything below is about whether that is possible, and how far off it is.

## Part 1 — how "copy without understanding" is supposed to work

This is the heart of the bet, so we go slowly and plainly. The founding statement of it is from a careful 2008 report by two Oxford researchers, **Anders Sandberg and Nick Bostrom**, called *"Whole Brain Emulation: A Roadmap."* They set out the whole idea in one clean sentence:

> "The basic idea is to take a particular brain, scan its structure in detail, and construct a software model of it that is so faithful to the original that, when run on appropriate hardware, it will behave in essentially the same way as the original brain."
> *(Sandberg & Bostrom, "Whole Brain Emulation: A Roadmap," 2008)*

Unpack it slowly, because every word carries weight. *"A particular brain"* — one specific real person's brain, not a general design. *"Scan its structure in detail"* — measure its physical make-up completely, cell by cell. *"A software model … so faithful … it will behave in essentially the same way"* — the copy in the computer acts like the original brain because it *is* the original brain's structure, rebuilt. *(**Software** = the instructions and data a computer runs, as opposed to the physical machine; **hardware** = the physical machine itself. A **model** here = a working copy inside the computer, not a description of it.)*

Now the deep part, the part that makes AP11 different from every other bet on the map. Sandberg and Bostrom's central argument is this: **whole-brain emulation, unlike every other route to machine intelligence, does not require us to *understand* intelligence.** Their reasoning is worth holding carefully. To build a mind *from scratch* (the AP1–AP10 way), you must have some working theory of what a mind is — you need to know *why* the parts you are building produce thinking. But to *copy* a mind, you do not. You only need two things: (1) a complete map of the brain's low-level parts and how they are wired, and (2) the local rules for how each part behaves moment to moment — how one brain cell reacts to a signal from the cells connected to it. Get those two things right at the small scale, run them all together, and the high-level thinking *emerges on its own* — for the same reason it emerges in the real brain. You never have to answer the big question *"how does a brain make a mind?"* at all. As the roadmap puts it, this is the one path where you might reach a thinking machine **without any real understanding of intelligence.**

That is a genuinely strange and powerful idea, so let me name the exact reason it might work — and the exact thing it is quietly assuming.

### The one big assumption: "scale separation"

Copying-without-understanding rests on a single hopeful assumption, and the whole bet lives or dies by it. It is called **scale separation** *(the idea that the small scale and the large scale can be "separated" — that below a certain level of fine detail, nothing more matters for how the brain behaves)*. In plain words: the claim is that there is **some level of detail that is "enough"** — copy the brain down to *that* level, and everything finer than it (the exact wobble of individual atoms, say) makes no difference to the resulting mind, so you can safely ignore it.

Why this matters so much: a real brain is detailed *all the way down*, forever — cells, inside them molecules, inside them atoms, inside them still-smaller physics. You can never copy *infinite* detail. So the bet only works if you can stop somewhere — if there is a floor below which the details are just noise that does not matter. Sandberg and Bostrom think that floor probably exists and is somewhere around the level of the connections between cells (perhaps with some chemistry added). But — and this is the honest hole in the plan — **nobody yet knows where the floor is, or even for certain that there is one.** That single unknown is the source of most of the trouble later on this page. Hold it.

### The three steps

Given the idea, the plan itself is simple to state (and extremely hard to do). It has three steps, in order:

1. **Scan.** Take a brain and measure its complete physical structure — where every cell is, and every connection between cells. Today the only way to do this at the needed fineness *destroys* the brain: you freeze it, slice it into unimaginably thin sheets, and photograph each sheet with a powerful microscope. *(This is called **destructive scanning** — the original brain does not survive the process. Whether a non-destructive scan of a living brain will ever be possible is an open question.)*
2. **Translate.** Turn that mountain of pictures into a working map: trace every cell through the stack of images, find every connection, and record its properties. This step is now done mostly by AI, because there is far too much to do by hand. The finished map is called a **connectome** *(a complete "wiring diagram" of a brain or nervous system — every cell and every connection; the word is built like "genome," but for connections)*. The field that builds these is **connectomics.**
3. **Simulate.** Load the connectome into a computer, give each simulated cell the rules for how a real cell behaves, and run the whole thing forward in time. If scale separation held — if you copied down to a fine-enough level — a mind now runs in the machine.

Keep those three words: **scan, translate, simulate.** They are the spine of the whole approach, and — as the next part shows — the world has only barely begun the first two, and has never truly done the third for any real mind.

## Part 2 — how far the copying has actually got

This bet is not about ideas; it is about *doing* an unimaginably hard copy job. So the fair question is: how far along is it, really? The honest answer is a ladder that climbs from a worm to a speck — and then faces an enormous jump. Every number below is a **dated snapshot (as of July 2026)**; connectomics is moving, so treat the specifics as this-year facts, not permanent ones.

### The connectome ladder — real, and genuinely climbing

- **The worm — *C. elegans* (302 brain cells).** A tiny roundworm's entire nervous system — just **302 neurons** and about **7,000 connections** — was fully mapped by hand back in **1986.** *(A **neuron** is a single brain cell; a **synapse** is one connection point where a signal passes from one neuron to the next — reminders from [AP6](06_ap6-brain-based.md).)* This is the simplest complete connectome there is, and it has been finished for about **forty years.**
- **The fruit fly (about 140,000 brain cells).** In **October 2024**, a large team (the *FlyWire* project, ~200 people across ~50 labs) published the first complete connectome of an adult fruit fly brain in the journal *Nature*: about **140,000 neurons** and more than **50 million** connections. This was a landmark — the first whole-brain wiring diagram of a creature that flies, walks, and learns.
- **A speck of mouse brain.** In **April 2025**, the *MICrONS* project (Allen Institute, Baylor, Princeton) mapped **one cubic millimetre** of a mouse's brain — a piece the size of a grain of sand — and found about **84,000 neurons** and over **500 million** connections in that speck, plus, remarkably, a record of the living cells' activity before the tissue was cut. It is the most detailed map of a piece of mammal brain ever made.
- **A speck of human brain.** A similar **one-cubic-millimetre** piece of human brain has been mapped (a Harvard–Google effort): about **16,000 neurons** and **150 million** connections in that one tiny fleck.

That is real progress, and it is speeding up, helped by better microscopes and by AI doing the tracing. So the copying is not science fiction; it is a live, funded science. *(For scale of effort: in 2023 the US NIH began a **$150 million** program, "BRAIN CONNECTS," just to build better connectome-mapping tools.)*

### …and then the enormous jump

Now stand back and look at the size of the last step. A whole human brain has about **86 billion neurons** — not thousands, not millions, but **tens of billions** — and on the order of **100 trillion** connections. The best human map so far covers **one cubic millimetre.** A whole human brain is well over **a million times** larger than that. And that is only the *scanning* — a job that today destroys the brain, takes years for a single speck, and would produce an amount of data measured in **hundreds of exabytes** *(an exabyte is a billion billion bytes; hundreds of exabytes is a sizeable fraction of all the data humanity currently stores — for **one** brain)*.

Then you still have to *run* the copy. Estimates of the computing power needed to simulate a whole human brain in real time run from roughly **10^18 operations per second** (a top supercomputer of today) up to numbers **many trillions of times** larger — the honest range spans about **fifteen zeros of uncertainty**, because, again, nobody knows how much fine detail you must include (the scale-separation question from Part 1, which now becomes a real problem). **[Established that the scale is enormous and the required detail is unknown; the exact numbers are contested estimates, as of July 2026.]**

So the state of AP11 in one line: the first rungs of the ladder are real and being climbed, but the top of the ladder — a whole human brain, scanned, translated, and *actually run as a mind* — is not close. No one has yet fully **simulated** even the 302-neuron worm as a living, learning creature (more on that hard fact in Stuck #1). AP11 is a real road with its first steps really taken — and a destination still a very long way off.

## Why this is a serious idea, not just science fiction

Given how far off it is, why do careful people study it at all? Three legs hold it up.

### Leg 1 — it is the one bet that needs no theory of intelligence

This is AP11's unique strength, and it is real. Every other approach on this map can fail simply by being *wrong about what intelligence is* — scale might not be enough (AP1), reward might not be enough (AP4), the brain's algorithm might never be found (AP6). AP11 sidesteps that entire risk. It does not propose a theory of intelligence that could be mistaken; it proposes to *copy a working example*, and a faithful copy works **for the same reason the original works** — no theory required. Sandberg and Bostrom stress that this gives the bet something the others lack: a **clear, well-defined target.** With most routes to AI we are not even sure what the milestones are. With emulation the milestones are obvious and physical — scan better, translate better, simulate more cells. You may not reach the target for a long time, but you always know exactly what "closer" means. A hard problem with a clear finish line is a very different, and more hopeful, thing than a vague one. **[Established — the "no theory needed, clear target" property is the bet's genuine and distinctive advantage.]**

### Leg 2 — the copying is really happening, and accelerating

This is the concrete leg. For most of its history emulation was pure speculation. That has changed. The connectome ladder in Part 2 is real published science: a fly brain fully mapped in 2024, specks of mouse and human brain mapped in 2025, each with genuine cell-and-synapse detail. The enabling tools are improving fast — for example **expansion microscopy** *(a clever trick that physically swells the brain tissue in a water-absorbing gel, like a sponge, so that ordinary microscopes can suddenly see tiny, synapse-sized details)*, which by 2025 could resolve features as small as **20 nanometres** *(a nanometre is a millionth of a millimetre)* — and AI now does the once-impossible job of tracing billions of cells through the image stacks. Progress that was flat for decades is now clearly moving. A bet whose first two steps are being demonstrated, at rising speed, on real brains is a serious scientific program, not a daydream. **[Established that connectomics is real and accelerating; that it will ever reach a whole human brain is unproven — see Stuck #2.]**

### Leg 3 — the existence proof, plus "substrate independence"

The third leg is shared with [AP6](06_ap6-brain-based.md) and made sharper. Start from the one hard fact: **the brain is the only general intelligence we know exists** — so copying it is the one route we are *certain* aims at a real target, not a mirage. Now add the assumption that makes copying-into-a-computer even thinkable: **substrate independence** *(the idea that a mind depends on the **pattern** of activity — the information being processed — not on the **stuff** it runs on; so the same pattern, run on brain cells or on silicon chips, would be the same mind)*. If mind is *what the brain does* (the computation) rather than *what the brain is made of* (the wet biology), then re-running that same activity on a computer should re-create the same mind. This is the same family of thinking as the **computational functionalism** you met in [AP10 · embodiment](10_ap10-embodiment.md) *(short reminder: the view that intelligence is just information-processing, and any machine that does the same processing has the same mind, whatever it is built from)*. If that view is right, then a faithful enough copy of a brain, running on a chip, would not merely *act* like the person — it would, in the fullest sense, *be* a mind. **[Likely as the mainstream scientific view; that a copy would truly be conscious is contested — see Stuck #4.]**

## So what does AP11 say intelligence is?

Pulling the legs together, here is AP11's answer to *"what is intelligence?"*:

- **Intelligence is** *a particular physical pattern of activity* — the specific dance of billions of connected cells that a real brain performs. Not a formula, not an algorithm we can write down, not a theory — just *this exact working structure*, whatever it turns out to be.
- **How it is built** is by **exact copying**, not by understanding, designing, or growing. You reproduce the pattern in full detail and let the mind result from getting the structure right.
- **Its claim about the missing piece:** what every *from-scratch* approach lacks is a correct, complete theory of intelligence — and maybe no such tidy theory exists, or maybe we are decades from finding it. AP11 says: you do not need one. The complete answer to "how does intelligence work?" is already written down — inside every human head — in a form we can, in principle, *read off and copy* without ever decoding it.

That is the bet. Now let us judge it.

## Judging the bet: where it is stuck

Be fair first. AP11 owns two things no other bet does: it needs no possibly-wrong theory of intelligence (Leg 1), and it aims at the one system we *know* is generally intelligent (Leg 3); and its first steps are real, accelerating science (Leg 2). Hold that. Now the four places it is truly stuck — and for this bet, they are steep.

### Stuck #1 — a wiring diagram is not a running brain

This is AP11's deepest technical problem, and the tiny worm makes it painfully clear. Recall that *C. elegans*, with only **302 neurons**, has had its complete connectome — its full wiring diagram — for about **forty years.** You would think that with the whole map in hand, simulating the worm as a living creature would be easy. **It is not. No one has done it.** As of 2026 the most advanced attempt (a project called *BAAIWorm*) can reproduce some basic wriggling movement using fixed, guessed-at connection strengths — but it cannot learn, and it is not the worm.

Why does having the full map not give you the running animal? Because a connectome tells you *that* two cells are connected, but almost nothing about *how*. Think of it this way: a connectome is like an electrical wiring diagram with **all the components rubbed out — every wire is drawn, but every switch, battery, and bulb is a blank box.** The map does not tell you whether a connection makes the next cell fire *more* or *less*, how *strong* the push is, how *fast* the signal travels, which of many chemical messengers it uses *(a **neurotransmitter** — the chemical a neuron releases to signal the next one; there are many kinds, and which one is used changes everything)*, or how all of that shifts from second to second as the brain learns. A real brain runs on this constantly-changing chemical and electrical detail, and the static wiring diagram captures **none** of it. **The connectome is the easy part — and it is already extraordinarily hard.** The truly hard part is measuring the live, moving inner state of every cell, which today we cannot read at anything like the needed scale. This is scale separation (Part 1) turning from a hopeful assumption into a wall: we do not know how much of this fine detail is essential, and every drop we must include makes the whole job vastly harder. **[Established — that a connectome alone is far from sufficient for emulation is a central, well-known result; the worm is the standing proof.]**

### Stuck #2 — the scale is beyond anything we can do, and the best-funded tries under-delivered

This is the practical wall, and it is huge. Part 2 laid out the arithmetic: a whole human brain is over a **million times** bigger than the largest speck yet mapped; scanning it (today, destructively) would take a very long time and produce hundreds of exabytes of data; running the copy could need computing power thousands of billions of times beyond today's largest machines — with the true figure unknown to within fifteen zeros. None of these is a small gap to be closed with a bit more effort; each is a huge one.

And the cautionary history is real. The two most ambitious, best-funded attempts to simulate brain tissue both fell far short of their promises. Europe's **Human Brain Project** — funded at around **one billion euros** over a decade (2013–2023) — promised a simulated human brain and did not deliver one; hundreds of neuroscientists signed an open letter of protest, and the project was reorganized away from that goal. Switzerland's **Blue Brain Project**, which built detailed simulations of small pieces of rat brain, **ended in December 2024**, having simulated on the order of tens of thousands of neurons — impressive, but an extremely small step toward 86 billion. Critics of both argued the same thing: **simulating brain tissue was attempted before we understood enough to know what to simulate** — you can build a giant model and still not have a mind, or even much insight. The lesson is serious: even with major money and talent, the road proved far longer than its supporters hoped. **[Established — the scale gap is enormous and the flagship simulation projects under-delivered, as of 2026.]**

### Stuck #3 — even if it worked, it may be the slowest path, and it teaches you nothing

Now suppose, generously, that all of this is solved one far-off day. There is still a strange double problem, unique to AP11.

First, **it is almost certainly the slowest road on the map.** The scanning, translating, and simulating powers it needs are so far beyond today's that the mainstream bets — scale ([AP1](01_ap1-scale-and-foundation-models.md)), reasoning ([AP2](02_ap2-reasoning-and-test-time-compute.md)) — will very likely reach AGI *decades* before emulation matures. This is [AP6's](06_ap6-brain-based.md) "right but too slow" worry in its most extreme form: even if copying a brain would work in principle, some faster approach almost surely gets to a general machine first, and by then a slow, exact human copy is a scientific curiosity, not the way the future was built.

Second — and this is the deep one — **AP11 is the one bet that, even in total success, hands you no understanding.** Every other approach, if it works, *teaches* you something: a theory of reasoning, of reward, of world-models, of the cortical algorithm. You end up knowing *how* intelligence works, and so you can improve it, scale it, redesign it. AP11 hands you a working mind you understand *no better than before* — a sealed box you copied but cannot read. *(A **sealed box**, sometimes called a "black box," = a system you can run and watch from outside but whose inner workings you cannot see into or explain.)* You would have made a digital person without learning the secret of minds. And a copied *human* mind is, by definition, roughly *human*-level — not obviously the superhuman intelligence the rest of the map is chasing (though a copy could in principle be run faster, paused, or duplicated, which brings its own strangeness). So AP11's very selling point — *no understanding required* — is also its ceiling: no understanding **produced.** **[Likely — the "slowest path, and yields a mind we still can't explain" critique is a serious and distinctive limit.]**

### Stuck #4 — the questions no other bet has to face: identity, consciousness, and suffering

Every other approach is, in the end, an engineering question. AP11 is also a **moral and philosophical** one, and this is its most distinctive crack — the equivalent of the special, path-specific worry each of the harder bets carries. Three problems, none with an agreed answer:

- **The copy problem — is the emulation *you*, or just a copy of you?** Today's scanning destroys the original brain. So a person is scanned, dies, and a mind that remembers being them wakes up in a computer. Is that *survival*, or has the person simply died and been *replaced* by a very good imitation that only *thinks* it is them? Worse: if you can make one copy, you can make a thousand. If ten emulations of one person run at once, which — if any — is the real continuation of the original? There is no settled answer; philosophers split between those who say what matters is the continuing *pattern* of memories and personality (so a copy *is* you) and those who say a copy is always just a copy. This is not a side-issue for AP11 — it goes to the whole point of "uploading" a mind.
- **Is the emulation *conscious* — does it actually feel anything?** If substrate independence (Leg 3) is right, the copy has inner experience — it can suffer, fear, hope. If that view is wrong, the copy is an empty puppet that acts like a person but feels nothing. We currently have **no test** to tell these apart, which leads straight to the darkest problem.
- **The suffering problem.** An emulation is a research subject you can create, pause, copy, edit, and delete at will. If it is conscious, then a half-finished, buggy emulation could be a mind in confusion or pain that you made by accident — and every failed experiment might be the creation and destruction of a suffering being. A path whose experiments might be **moral catastrophes** carries a weight no other approach on this map does.

These are not reasons the bet is *wrong* — but they are reasons it may be, in part, **forbidden**, or at least far more limited than a pure engineering project. **[Contested / open — these are unresolved ethical and philosophical questions, and they are decisive for whether the path is even permissible.]**

### The big question under all of these

Every doubt above folds into one question: **can you truly get a mind by faithful copying alone — with no understanding of how minds work — and even if you can, is that a road worth or allowed to travel, given it likely arrives last, teaches us nothing, and forces questions about identity and suffering we cannot answer?** AP11 says yes to the first part: the complete recipe for intelligence is already written inside every skull, and we can copy it out without ever decoding it. The doubters answer that a copy without understanding may be impossible in practice (the worm shows a wiring diagram is not a mind, Stuck #1), is beyond our powers for the foreseeable future (Stuck #2), would be overtaken long before it arrived (Stuck #3), and brings in moral problems no other bet must face (Stuck #4). And notice the clean contrast with its twin: [AP6](06_ap6-brain-based.md) asks *how much of the brain do we need to understand?* and answers "the algorithm." AP11 answers "none of it — just copy everything." It is the purest test on the whole map of a single question: **is understanding necessary to build a mind, or can a good-enough copy do it blind?** *As of July 2026 this is wide open, and — unlike the mainstream bets — likely to stay open for a long time.* **[Contested — the key open question, and the longest-horizon one on the map.]**

## ⚠️ Honesty box

- **"No theory needed" is its strength *and* its ceiling.** AP11 alone needs no possibly-wrong theory of intelligence (Leg 1) — but that means, even in success, it produces no theory of intelligence either (Stuck #3). It could hand you a working mind you understand no better than before. Keep both halves of that in view. **[Established.]**
- **The connectome is the beginning, not the answer.** The mapped fly and the mapped specks are real and exciting (Leg 2) — but a wiring diagram is not a running mind (Stuck #1). The forty-years-mapped, still-not-simulated worm is the standing proof. Do not mistake a connectome for an emulation. **[Established.]**
- **Scale separation is an assumption, not a fact.** The entire bet rests on there being a level of detail that is "enough" to copy (Part 1). We do not know that level, or even for sure that it exists. If the essential detail runs very deep, the job is far harder — perhaps impossible — than the hopeful case. **[Contested.]**
- **This is the longest-horizon bet on the map.** Every other approach is arguing about the next few years. AP11 is arguing about the next several *decades* at least (Stuck #2, #3). It is worth understanding as an *idea* — the pure "copy without understanding" idea — far more than as a near-term plan. **[Established.]**
- **The ethics are not a footnote.** Identity, consciousness, and the risk of creating suffering minds (Stuck #4) are central to whether this path can be walked at all. A technically successful emulation could still be a moral disaster. **[Contested / open.]**
- **Numbers and names age.** The 2024 fly connectome, the 2025 mouse and human specks, expansion microscopy's resolution, the Blue Brain Project's 2024 close, the compute and storage estimates — these are dated snapshots (2024–2026). The lasting parts are the **copy-without-understanding idea**, the **scan→translate→simulate** plan, the **connectome vs running-brain** gap, the **scale-separation** assumption, **substrate independence**, and the **identity/consciousness** problems. The specific milestones will move; the shape of the bet will not. **[Established core, dated specifics.]**

## How to use this (if you want to direct AI work)

- **Use AP11 as the map's clean test of one question: is understanding necessary?** When someone claims a route to AGI, ask where it sits between AP11 (copy, understand nothing) and the theory-driven bets (understand, then build). AP11 is the pure extreme of "no understanding needed." Placing a proposal on that line tells you what it is really betting.
- **Never accept a connectome as proof of a mind.** If a project claims to have "simulated a brain," ask the Stuck #1 questions: do they have the *dynamics* (connection strengths, signs, chemistry, live state) or only the *wiring*? A map of connections is a real achievement and *not* a working mind. The worm is your reference: full map, four decades, still no living simulation.
- **Discount timelines hard.** AP11 is the slowest path on the map (Stuck #3). Treat any near-term "mind uploading" claim as a warning sign; the honest horizon is decades, held back by scanning and compute powers we do not have. Budget your attention accordingly — this is an *idea to understand*, not a program to join this year.
- **Keep the scale-separation question in front of you.** For any brain-copying claim, the decisive question is *how much detail do you actually need?* The whole feasibility swings on it, and no one knows the answer. A plan that waves this away is not being honest about its hardest unknown.
- **Take the ethics seriously before, not after.** If emulations are ever built, the identity and suffering questions (Stuck #4) are not just abstract debate — they decide whether the work is permissible at all. Anyone directing such work must weigh the chance of creating a conscious mind that can suffer *first*.
- **What you hand to others:** the scanning, the image-tracing, the simulation engineering. **What you keep for yourself:** judging whether a "brain simulation" is really a mind or just a map, holding the scale-separation unknown, weighing the decades-long timeline honestly, and refusing to let the deep identity/consciousness questions be treated as an afterthought.

## Connections

- **Keep only three things:** ① AP11 = **copy a brain without understanding it.** Scan a real brain completely (every cell, every connection = its **connectome**), rebuild that exact structure in a computer, and run it; a mind emerges because the copy is faithful — no theory of intelligence required (Sandberg & Bostrom, 2008). The plan is three steps: **scan → translate → simulate.** ② It is the **opposite twin of [AP6](06_ap6-brain-based.md):** AP6 extracts and rebuilds the brain's *algorithm* (understand the method, drop the biology); AP11 copies the *biology* and understands nothing — the purest test of whether understanding is needed to build a mind. Its first steps are real, accelerating science (the fly connectome 2024; specks of mouse and human brain 2025). ③ It is stuck on four steep cracks: **a wiring diagram is not a running brain** (the 302-neuron worm, mapped 40 years, still not simulated — a connectome is "wires with the components rubbed out"), **the scale is beyond us** (a whole human brain is a million-plus times the largest speck mapped; the flagship Blue Brain / Human Brain Projects under-delivered), **even success is slow and teaches nothing** (it likely arrives last and yields a mind we still cannot explain), and **the ethics** (is the copy you? is it conscious? could a failed run be a suffering mind?).

- **Its closest cousin and opposite twin:** [AP6 · brain-based](06_ap6-brain-based.md) — both start from "the brain is the only general intelligence we know," then split: AP6 *understands the algorithm and rebuilds it*; AP11 *copies everything and understands nothing.* Read them together to see the whole "how much understanding do we need?" axis.
- **Its other links:** [AP1 · scale](01_ap1-scale-and-foundation-models.md) — the mainstream bet whose speed is AP11's Stuck #3 (scale will very likely reach AGI first). [AP10 · embodiment](10_ap10-embodiment.md) — shares **computational functionalism / substrate independence**, the idea that mind is the pattern, not the stuff (Leg 3), and a copied mind would also need a body or world to act in. [AP5 · world models](05_ap5-world-models-jepa.md) and [AP6](06_ap6-brain-based.md) — the brain AP11 copies is itself a prediction-and-planning machine.
- **How sure are we?** That the brain is the one known general intelligence, that connectomics is real and accelerating, and that a connectome alone is far short of a running mind — **[Established]**. That copying-without-understanding could ever yield a whole-human emulation, that scale separation holds, that the copy would be conscious or "you" — **[Contested, open, and long-horizon]**.
